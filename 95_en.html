Poem 95
おほおけなく 憂うき世よの民たみに おほおふうかな
わがたつ杣そまに 墨染すみぞめの袖そで
Author: Former Senior High Priest Jien (1155–1225)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
Unfit to rule this wicked world With all its pomp and pride, I'd rather in my plain black robe A humble priest abide, Far up the mountain side.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
How bold it is of me — yet I spread my ink-black sleeves to shelter the people of this troubled world.
Commentary
The end of the Heian period and the start of the Kamakura period brought disasters, epidemics, famine, and bandits, as power passed from the court to the shogunate amid repeated wars. Jien probably wrote this grieving for the world and wishing to save people through Buddhism.
About the Poet
Jien was the sixth son of Fujiwara no Tadamichi and became a monk as a child. A famous monk, he headed the Tendai school at Enryaku-ji four times. He wrote the Gukanshō, a history of Japan, and is thought to have tried to stop Go-Toba from going to war.

Jien was a son of Fujiwara no Tadamichi (the Lay Novice of Hosshō-ji Temple, former Kampaku and Chancellor of the Realm), and a younger brother of Kujō Kanezane by the same mother. He was also the uncle of Kujō Yoshitsune, the poet of Poem 91.

In 1192, at 38, he became a guardian monk (gojisō) of the emperor and was also highly valued by Retired Emperor Go-Toba. He had influence not only in the religious world but also in politics.

Jien summed up the deeds of the emperors and the flow of history in the Gukanshō. One theory says he wrote it to warn Retired Emperor Go-Toba against the uprising that would become the Jōkyū War.

Appendix
The Original Poem (Honka)
Jien borrowed (honkadori) from the next poem.
This poem is said to have been written by Saichō when he built the Konponchūdō, the main hall of Enryaku-ji.

In Japanese

阿耨多羅あのくたら 三藐三菩提さんみやくさんぼだいの 仏ほとけたち
わが立たつ杣そまに 冥加みょうがあらせたまえ anokutara sanmiyakusanbodaino hotoketachi
wagatatsusomani myougaarasetamae Shin Kokin Wakashū, Saichō

In English

O Buddhas of supreme wisdom (anokutara sanmyaku sanbodai),
grant your unseen blessing (myōga) to this mountain where I stand.
An Autumn Scene of Wild Geese and the Moon
The next poem by Jien is in the Shin Kokin Wakashū.

In Japanese

大江山おおえやま かたぶく月つきの 影かげさえて
鳥羽田とばたの面おもに 落おつる雁かりが音ね ooeyama katabukutsukino kagesaete
tobatanoomoni otsurukarigane Shin Kokin Wakashū, Jien

In English

The moon sinking toward Mount Ōe shines cold and clear,
and I hear the cries of wild geese coming down onto the rice fields of Toba.
The View from Mount Hiei

Mount Hiei is a mountain about 848 m high on the border of Shiga Prefecture and Kyoto City. Enryaku-ji, whose grounds cover the whole mountain, was founded in the early Heian period by Saichō, the founder of the Tendai school.
