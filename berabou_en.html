Waka Poems Featured in
Berabou

In Episode 20 of the 2025 NHK historical drama Berabou, Jūzaburō (Ryūsei Yokohama) is invited by Ōta Nanpo (Kenta Kiritani) and takes part in a kyōka (comic poetry) gathering. Two of the poems composed there were based on poems from the Hyakunin Isshu.

The Kyōka of Akera Kankō (Bun'ichi Hamanaka)

This poem is based on Hyakunin Isshu Poem 92, Sanuki, attendant to retired Emperor Nijō.

In Japanese

わが恋こいは 鰻うなぎの桶おけの 見みえぬ桶おけのうちの
ぬらぬらふらふら 乾かわく間まもなし wagakoiha unaginookeno mienuokenouchino
nuranurafurafura kawakumamonashi Akera Kankō (Bun'ichi Hamanaka)

In English

My favorite eels are gone, and the woman I loved has left me too. Only I go on wandering, wet, with never a moment to dry.

In Japanese

わが袖そでは 潮干しおひに見みえぬ 沖おきの石いしの
人ひとこそ知しらね かわくまもなし wagasodeha shihohinimienu okinoishino
hitokososhirane kawakumamonashi Hyakunin Isshu Poem 92, Sanuki, attendant to retired Emperor Nijō

In English

My sleeves are like a rock hidden beneath the tides — unseen by others, they are never dry.

The Kyōka of Tsuchiyama Sōjirō / Keishō Naran (Shuntarō Yanagi)

This poem is based on Hyakunin Isshu Poem 97, Acting Middle Counselor Sadaie.

In Japanese

来こぬ人ひとを 待まつほど恨うらむ 夕鰻ゆううなぎは
焼やくやも塩しおか タレ惑まどいつつ konuhitoo matsuhodouramu yuuunagiha
yakuyamoshioka taremadoitsutsu Keishō Naran (Shuntarō Yanagi)

In English

The evening eel, growing bitter as it waits for one who does not come, frets in confusion: will it be grilled with salt, or with sauce?

In Japanese

来こぬ人ひとを 松帆まつほの浦うらの 夕ゆうなぎに
焼やくやもしほの 身みもこがれつつ konuhitoo matsuhonourano yuunagini
yakuyamoshihono mimokogaretsutsu Hyakunin Isshu Poem 97, Acting Middle Counselor Sadaie

In English

Waiting for someone who will not come, at the quiet evening shore — like salt burned from the seaweed, I too am consumed by longing.

The Kyōka of Yomo no Akara / Ōta Nanpo (Kenta Kiritani)

This poem has nothing to do with the Hyakunin Isshu, but it is woven with clever wordplay. It plays on ana ("hole," a word associated with eels) and the exclamation ana ("ah!"); on the imo ("yam") of the proverb "a mountain yam turns into an eel" and imose ("lovers"); on the eel being split open and lovers being split apart; and on burning with longing and the eel being grilled (kogasu, "to scorch") as kabayaki.

In Japanese

あな鰻うなぎ いづくの山やまの いもとせを
さかれて後のちに 身みを焦こがすとは anaunagi izukunoyamano imotoseo
sakaretenochini miokogasutoha Yomo no Akara / Ōta Nanpo (Kenta Kiritani)

In English

Ah, the eel! Who knows from what mountain's yam it came? Split open like lovers torn apart, and still burning with longing as it is grilled: how cruel.

Poems in Episode 14 of Berabou

In Episode 14 of Berabou, there was a scene in which the masters of the Yoshiwara houses traded poems that parodied the Hyakunin Isshu. In the Edo period, there were schools called terakoya all over Japan, where reading, writing, arithmetic, and more were taught. The Hyakunin Isshu was used there as a textbook, so it seems to have been very familiar to people of the time.

Ritsu's Poem (Yumi Adachi)

This poem is based on Hyakunin Isshu Poem 9, Ono no Komachi.

In Japanese

店みせの場所ばしょ うつらんとてか 家田屋いえだやに
わが身世みよにふる ながめ狭せましに misenobasho utsurantoteka iedayani
wagamiyonifuru nagamesemashini Ritsu (Yumi Adachi)

In English

You're moving the shop to Iedaya's place!? It's gotten too cramped, hasn't it.

In Japanese

花はなの色いろは うつりにけりな いたづずらに
わが身みよにふる ながめせしまに hananoiroha utsurinikerina itazurani
wagamiyonifuru nagameseshimani Hyakunin Isshu Poem 9, Ono no Komachi

In English

How the beauty of the cherry blossoms faded away, soaked by the long spring rains. And just like them, my own looks have faded too, lost in a haze of endless longing.

Jūzaburō's Poem (Ryūsei Yokohama)

This poem is based on Hyakunin Isshu Poem 86, Priest Saigyō.

In Japanese

嘆なげけとて 茶屋ちゃやか本屋ほんやか 惑まどわする
かこち顔がおなる わが馴染なじみかな nageketote chayakahonyaka madowasuru
kakochigaonaru waganajimikana Tsutaya Jūzaburō (Ryūsei Yokohama)

In English

The customers are confused too: they can't tell whether it's a teahouse or a bookshop.

In Japanese

嘆なげけとて 月つきやは物ものを 思おもはわする
かこち顔がおなる わが涙なみだかな nageketote tsukiyahamonoo omohasuru
kakochigahonaru waganamidakana Hyakunin Isshu Poem 86, Priest Saigyō

In English

Did the moon ask me to grieve? These tears on my face — it is as if I am blaming the moon for making me feel this way.

Chōjiya Chōjūrō's Poem (Hideomi Shima)

This poem is based on Hyakunin Isshu Poem 48, Minamoto no Shigeyuki.

In Japanese

金かねの痛いたみ 浅草紙あさくさがみの 己おのれの身み
破やぶけてものを 思おもうことかも kanenoitami asakusagamino onorenomi
yabuketemonoo omoukotokamo Chōjiya Chōjūrō (Hideomi Shima)

In English

What are you going to do about the money!? It'll be too late once you've gone bust!

In Japanese

風かぜをいたみ 岩いわうつ波なみの おのれのみ
くだけて物ものを 思おもふうころかな kazeoitami ihautsunamino onorenomi
kudaketemonoo omofukorokana Hyakunin Isshu Poem 48, Minamoto no Shigeyuki

In English

Like the waves crashing against the rocks in the fierce wind, I alone am broken to pieces by this sorrow.

Tsutaya Jūzaburō's Poem (Ryūsei Yokohama)

This poem is based on Hyakunin Isshu Poem 41, Mibu no Tadami.

In Japanese

富本本とみもとぼん 我わが名なはまだき 立たちにけり
稽古本けいこぼんもと 思おもひいそめしか tomimotobon waganahamadaki tachinikeri
keikobonmoto omoisomeshika Tsutaya Jūzaburō (Ryūsei Yokohama)

In English

My name has already gotten around thanks to the Tomimoto books, so next I'm thinking of practice books!

In Japanese

恋こいすてふちょう 我わが名なはまだき 立たちにけり
人ひとしれずこそ 思おもひいそめしか kohisutefu waganahamadaki tachinikeri
hitoshirezukoso omohisomeshika Hyakunin Isshu Poem 41, Mibu no Tadami

In English

They say my name as a lover is already known far and wide, even before the affair began. I had kept it so quietly in my heart.

Ōgiya Uemon's Poem (Kazuhiro Yamaji)

This poem is based on Hyakunin Isshu Poem 99, Retired Emperor Go-Toba.

In Japanese

ならばよし ならば後押あとおし 憂うれひなく
吉原よしわらゆゑえに もの思おもふ身みは narabayoshi narabaatooshi ureinaku
yoshiwarayueni monoomoumiha Ōgiya Uemon (Kazuhiro Yamaji)

In English

Well, if that's the case, fine! I'll back you up, for the Yoshiwara's sake too.

In Japanese

人ひともをし 人ひともうらめし あぢじきなく
世よを思おもふうゆゑえに 物も思おもふう身みは hitomooshi hitomourameshi ajikinaku
yooomofuyueeni monoomofumiha Hyakunin Isshu Poem 99, Retired Emperor Go-Toba

In English

I find people both dear and hateful. It is because I care so deeply about this troubled world that I am always lost in thought.
