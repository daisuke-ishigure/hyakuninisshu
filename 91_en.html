Poem 91
きりぎりす 鳴なくや霜夜しもよの さむしろに
衣ころもかたしき ひとりかも寝ねむん
Author: Gokyōgoku Regent and former Chancellor of the Realm (1169–1206)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
I'm sleeping all alone, and hear The crickets round my head; So cold and frosty is the night, That I across the bed My koromo have spread.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
On a cold, frosty night, the cricket cries. Lying alone with one sleeve folded beneath me — how lonely it is.
Commentary
Kujō Yoshitsune wrote this about sleeping alone on an autumn night after his wife died. Lovers then used each other's robes as pillows, so "spreading only my own robe" meant sleeping alone.
About the Poet
Kujō Yoshitsune was the second son of Kujō Kanezane and grandson of Fujiwara no Tadamichi. He became head of the family after his elder brother died young. He rose to regent and Chancellor, but died two years later at 38, before his father. One rumor says he was killed in his sleep by a spear thrust through the ceiling.

Kujō Yoshitsune was a grandson of Fujiwara no Tadamichi and the second son of Kujō Kanezane, founder of the Kujō family. Because his elder brother, Kujō Yoshimichi, died young, he became the family's heir. He was active in politics from a young age and became Palace Minister in his mid-20s. But when his father Kanezane was removed as Kampaku in the "Political Change of 1196" (Kenkyū shichinen no seihen), Yoshitsune too was kept away from the court for a time.

Later, he returned as Minister of the Left at 30. Trusted by Retired Emperor Go-Toba, he became regent for Emperor Tsuchimikado at 33 and rose to Chancellor of the Realm at 35. But he died suddenly at only 38. Because his death was so sudden, there is a theory that he was stabbed to death in his bedroom.

Yoshitsune's father Kanezane supported Fujiwara no Toshinari and his son Sadaie. Yoshitsune learned waka from Toshinari, led his own poetry circle, and supported Sadaie and other poets of the new style. Sadaie also served as Yoshitsune's keishi (a household official who managed the family's affairs). Yoshitsune was also a gifted calligrapher, founded the Gokyōgoku style of calligraphy, and wrote the kana preface of the Shin Kokin Wakashū.

The Poem He Borrowed From
Yoshitsune borrowed (honkadori) from the following poem. His aim in borrowing was to layer images on top of each other. In the time of the original poem, the Kokin Wakashū, the Lady of Uji Bridge (Uji no Hashihime) was a symbol of "a beloved woman." Later, however, under the influence of The Tale of Genji, she took on the darker image of "a fragile figure wasting away alone." Yoshitsune blended these two opposite images: the classic elegance that came from the original poem, and the loneliness brought by The Tale of Genji.
