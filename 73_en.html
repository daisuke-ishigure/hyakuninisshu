Poem 73
高砂たかさごの 尾おの上への桜さくら 咲さきにけり
外山とやまの霞かすみ 立たたずもあらなむん
Author: Acting Middle Counselor Masafusa (1041–1111)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Spring
English Verse Translation
The cherry trees are blossoming On Takasago's height; Oh may no mountain mist arise, No clouds so soft and white, To hide them from our sight.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
The cherry blossoms on the high peaks of Takasago have already bloomed. Let the mist not rise over the foothills and hide them from view.
Commentary
The Go Shūi Wakashū says this was written at a drinking party at the home of Palace Minister Fujiwara no Moromichi, on the theme "looking at mountain cherry blossoms far away."
About the Poet
Ōe no Masafusa was a great-grandson of Akazome Emon. His family produced scholars, and he became an advanced student of literature at 16. An expert on court customs, he taught Emperors Go-Sanjō, Shirakawa, and Horikawa, advised them on policy, and rose to Acting Middle Counselor.

According to the Sonpi Bunmyaku and the Ōe-shi Keizu (Ōe family tree), the founder of the Ōe clan was Emperor Heizei. But this is said to have been the Ōe family's attempt to make its line look nobler, and it has been pointed out that it may be wrong.

In the time of Ōe no Otondo, the family name was changed from Ōe written as "大枝" (great branch) to "大江" (great river). It is said to carry the wish that the family would prosper for a long time, like a great river (e).

Like the Sugawara clan, the Ōe clan produced many scholars, and the two are mentioned together as the Kanke (the Sugawara family) and the Gōke (the Ōe family).

Celebrating Masafusa's Birth
The next poem by Akazome Emon is in the Go Shūi Wakashū.
She sent it with baby clothes to celebrate Masafusa's birth.

In Japanese

雲くもの上うえに のぼらむまでも 見みてしがな
鶴つるの毛け衣ごろも 年としふとならば kumonoueni noboramumademo miteshigana
tsurunokegoromo toshifutonaraba Go Shūi Wakashū, Akazome Emon

In English

I want to watch you grow until you rise "above the clouds" (to the ranks allowed in the emperor's presence). Wearing white baby clothes like a crane's feathers, you surely will, as the years go by.
A Poem About the Cherry Blossoms on the Peak
in The Tale of Genji
This poem appears in "Wakamurasaki," the 5th chapter of The Tale of Genji. Hikaru Genji wishes to take in the young Murasaki, and her grandmother, who firmly refuses to give her to him, wrote this poem. Just as one's heart is caught by cherry blossoms before the storm scatters them, she fears Genji's love will last only the short time until the blossoms fall.

In Japanese

嵐あらし吹ふく 尾上おのえの桜さくら 散ちらぬ間まを
心こころとめける ほどのはかなさ arashifuku onoenosakura chiranumao
kokorotomekeru hodonohakanasa The Tale of Genji, the nun of Kitayama

In English

Your heart was caught only for the short time before the storm scatters the cherry blossoms on the peak. How fleeting that is!
Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

It shows people enjoying the cherry blossoms on a mountain. But unlike the wish in the poem, haze has risen, and it seems the mountain cherries cannot be seen from far away.
