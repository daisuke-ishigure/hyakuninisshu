Poem 57
めぐり逢あひいて 見みしやそれとも わかぬ間まに
雲隠くもがくれにし 夜半よわの月つきかな
Author: Murasaki Shikibu (c. 970–c. 1014)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
I wandered forth this moonlight night, And some one hurried by; But who it was I could not see,— Clouds driving o'er the sky Obscured the moon on high.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
We met for just a moment, yet I could not be sure it was really you — and then you vanished behind the clouds, like the midnight moon.
Commentary
The Shin Kokin Wakashū says a childhood friend she had not seen for years came to visit, but left again quickly, racing the moon. The poem expresses her disappointment at the friend's quick departure.
About the Poet
Murasaki Shikibu was bright from childhood; it is said she learned Chinese poems faster than her brother just by listening. Fujiwara no Michinaga heard of her gift for stories and brought her to serve Empress Akiko. While teaching Akiko, she finished The Tale of Genji, often called the world's first long novel.

Murasaki Shikibu's husband, Nobutaka, was her second cousin.
Minamoto no Tomoko, the mother of Akiko, whom Murasaki Shikibu served, was also her second cousin.

Murasaki Shikibu tutored Akiko and wrote The Tale of Genji, the world's oldest long novel. Perhaps thanks to this, Emperor Ichijō, who had been devoted only to Teishi, gradually came to love Akiko.

With her husband Nobutaka, Murasaki Shikibu had Fujiwara no Katako (Daini no Sanmi), who also served Akiko.

Murasaki Shikibu's Court Name (Nyōbō-na)
Murasaki Shikibu's real name is unknown. When she began serving Akiko, she was called "Tō Shikibu," because her family name was Fujiwara (written with the character also read tō) and her father had once been a secretary of the Ministry of Ceremonial (Shikibu no jō). Later, when her Tale of Genji became famous, she seems to have come to be called "Murasaki Shikibu," after Murasaki no Ue, the heroine of the tale.
Her Nickname, "Lady of the Chronicles"

In The Murasaki Shikibu Diary, Murasaki Shikibu reveals that she was given the nickname "Nihongi no Mitsubone" (the Lady of the Chronicles of Japan).

When Emperor Ichijō had The Tale of Genji read aloud to him, he praised her deep learning, saying, "This author must have read the Nihongi (Chronicles of Japan)." When an older lady-in-waiting, Saemon no Naishi, heard this, she gave Murasaki Shikibu the nickname "Lady of the Chronicles" to make fun of her.

In fact, Murasaki Shikibu is said to have avoided showing off her learning, even pretending she could not write simple Chinese characters. In her diary she writes that she found the nickname ridiculous. On the other hand, she was secretly teaching Akiko a Chinese text, the New Music Bureau Ballads (Shingafu), and her diary also shows her fear of trouble if Saemon no Naishi found out.

A Poem to Nobutaka Before Their Marriage
While Murasaki Shikibu was living in Echizen Province with her father, the governor, Nobutaka proposed to her. But Nobutaka already had several wives with children, and he was also courting another woman. Around the New Year, he wrote to her: "I would like to show you that when spring comes, your heart will open to me just as the ice melts." So Murasaki Shikibu sent him the next poem.

A Poem Celebrating the Birth of Prince Atsuhira (Emperor Go-Ichijō)
The next poem was written to celebrate the birth of Prince Atsuhira (later Emperor Go-Ichijō), Empress Akiko's son.

In English

The next poem was written to celebrate the birth of Prince Atsuhira (Emperor Go-Ichijō) while looking at the moon reflected in the pond of the Tsuchimikado mansion, Fujiwara no Michinaga's home.

Fujiwara no Michinaga's Poem

In Japanese

すきものと 名なにし立たてれば 見みる人ひとの
折おらで過すぐるは あらじとぞ思おもふ Murasaki Shikibu Diary, Fujiwara no Michinaga

In English

Since you are known as a sukimono (a lover of romance),

"Sukimono" is a pun on "sour plum" (suki mono) and "a person fond of love affairs." "Orade suguru" means passing by without breaking off a branch of the plum, that is, without making her his own.

Rozan-ji Temple

Rozan-ji in Kamigyō Ward, Kyoto, is known as a place connected with Murasaki Shikibu. The mansion of her great-grandfather, Fujiwara no Kanesuke, once stood in this area, and Murasaki Shikibu was born and raised here.

The grounds have a beautiful dry landscape garden of white sand and moss, the "Genji Garden." In summer, when they are at their best, lovely purple bellflowers (kikyō), which match the image of Murasaki Shikibu, color the garden.

Murasaki Shikibu (the Plant)

Murasaki shikibu (Japanese beautyberry) is a deciduous shrub of the genus Callicarpa in the mint family.
It grows wild in woods all over Japan and bears purple berries in autumn.

Ishiyama-dera Temple

In Ōtsu City, Shiga Prefecture. Just as its name ("stone mountain temple") says, it stands on rocks of wollastonite. Murasaki Shikibu is said to have gotten the idea for the "Suma" and "Akashi" chapters while staying at Ishiyama-dera, and the main hall has a "Murasaki Shikibu Room."

The Tale of Genji

The world's oldest long love story, in 54 chapters. It describes the culture and love lives of Heian nobles.

When The Tale of Genji was written, books in Chinese were considered the official ones, and tales written in kana were seen as rather low.

But over time, the literary value of The Tale of Genji came to be highly praised. Today it has been translated into more than 30 languages and is still read around the world after 1,000 years. In Japan, too, many famous writers have made modern Japanese versions of it.

The photo shows a monument to The Tale of Genji near the Asagiri Bridge over the Uji River.

The Opening of The Tale of Genji
In an age when "tales" were generally thought of as reading for children, a story this real, opening with such smooth yet somehow uneasy sentences, came as a shock and greatly stirred the hearts of people at court.

Hikaru Genji's Last Poem in The Tale of Genji
This poem appeared in episode 42 of the NHK drama Hikaru Kimi e (Dear Radiance). In the drama, Fujiwara no Michinaga visits Murasaki Shikibu's room to discuss how Emperor Sanjō does not visit his empress Kenshi (Kiyoko), Michinaga's daughter. Michinaga says, "Between Emperor Ichijō and his empress Akiko there was The Tale of Genji, but between Emperor Sanjō and Kenshi there is nothing. The Tale of Genji is no use." After this talk, Murasaki Shikibu quietly writes down the next poem. It is the last poem Hikaru Genji composes in The Tale of Genji.

In Japanese

もの思おもふと 過すぐる月日つきひも 知しらぬ間まに
年としもわが世よも 今日きょうや尽つきぬる The Tale of Genji, Hikaru Genji

In English

Lost in thought, I did not even notice the months and days go by. Do this year and my life both end today?
A Poem by Toshinari, Who Praised The Tale of Genji
This poem was written by Fujiwara no Toshinari on the theme of "love" at a poetry gathering. Toshinari praised The Tale of Genji as literature that embodies mono no aware (a deep feeling for the sadness of things), and left the words, "A poet who has not read Genji is to be pitied." This poem says that through love, not only joy but also sorrow and pain enrich the heart, so that one can deeply understand mono no aware, the fleeting and painful nature of life.

In Japanese

もののあはれも これよりぞ知しる Shin Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Toshinari

In English

Without love, people would never grow a heart. It is through love that we come to understand mono no aware.
Murasaki Shikibu's Death Poem
The Tale of Genji, written by Murasaki Shikibu, has now been translated into more than 30 languages, and in Japan famous writers keep making new modern versions; it has been read for over 1,000 years. But Murasaki Shikibu herself may never have imagined this.

In Japanese

跡あとは消きえせぬ 形見かたみなれども Shin Kokin Wakashū, Murasaki Shikibu

In English

Murasaki Shikibu, Thought to Have Fallen into Hell

The graves of Murasaki Shikibu and Ono no Takamura, the poet of Poem 11, stand side by side.

At the end of the Heian period, people worried that Murasaki Shikibu, the author of The Tale of Genji, a tale of love and desire, might have fallen into hell after death.

According to a legend, her grave was moved next to Takamura's so that she could be saved with the help of Takamura, who was said to work at the court of Enma, the king of hell. The graves still stand quietly today.
