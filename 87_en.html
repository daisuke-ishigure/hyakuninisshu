Poem 87
村雨むらさめの 露つゆもまだひぬ まきの葉はに
霧きりたちのぼる 秋あきの夕ゆう暮ぐれ
Author: Priest Jakuren (c. 1139–1202)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
The rain, which fell from passing showers, Like drops of dew, still lies Upon the fir-tree needles, and The mists of evening rise Up to the autumn skies.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
After the passing shower, the mist rises slowly through the rain-drenched leaves of the cedar. An autumn evening.
Commentary
Written for the "Fifty Poems of Old and Young" contest held by Retired Emperor Go-Toba. An autumn scene without the colors of autumn leaves, it shows evergreen trees in a stillness like an ink painting. "Murasame" is a passing autumn shower.
About the Poet
Jakuren was adopted by Fujiwara no Toshinari, but is said to have become a monk so that Toshinari's own son Sadaie could be heir. After traveling the country he settled in Saga. Go-Toba chose him with Sadaie to compile the Shin Kokin Wakashū, but he died the next year.

Priest Jakuren was the son of Shunkai, Fujiwara no Toshinari's elder or younger brother. His father Shunkai became a monk at Daigo-ji, and Jakuren is thought to have been adopted by his uncle Toshinari at around age 12.

He rose to Junior Fifth Rank, Upper Grade, and Junior Assistant Minister of Central Affairs (Nakatsukasa no shō), but became a monk in his 30s.

Even after becoming a monk, he was active at poetry gatherings in the capital. Retired Emperor Go-Toba thought highly of him as a poet and chose him, together with Fujiwara no Sadaie, to compile the Shin Kokin Wakashū. But he died before it was finished.

Appendix
The Sanseki no Uta ("Three Evening Poems")
The following three poems in the Shin Kokin Wakashū, all ending with "aki no yūgure" (an autumn evening), are praised as the Sanseki no Uta.

Jakuren

In Japanese

さびしさは その色いろとしも なかりけり
まき立たつ山やまの 秋あきの夕暮ゆうぐれ sabishisaha sonoirotoshimo nakarikeri
makitatsuyamano akinoyuugure Shin Kokin Wakashū, Jakuren

In English

Loneliness does not come only from a color (the color of autumn leaves). It is there in an autumn evening in the mountains, where cedars and cypresses stand (without color).

Saigyō

In Japanese

心こころなき 身みにもあはれは 知しられけり
鴫しぎ立たつ沢さわの 秋あきの夕暮ゆうぐれ kokoronaki minimoahareha shirarekeri
shigitatsusawano akinoyuugure Shin Kokin Wakashū, Saigyō

In English

Though I became a monk and gave up my feelings, I feel the sadness of an autumn evening at the marsh where snipes fly up.

Fujiwara no Sadaie

In Japanese

見渡みわたせば 花はなも紅葉もみじも なかりけり
浦うらの苫屋とまやの 秋あきの夕暮ゆうぐれ miwataseba hanamomomijimo nakarikeri
uranotomayano akinoyuugure Shin Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Sadaie

In English

Looking around, I see neither cherry blossoms nor autumn leaves. Only an autumn evening by the shore, with a poor hut thatched with rush mats, meets my eyes.
A Poem Regretting the End of Spring
The next poem was written for the "Fifty Poems of Old and Young" contest in 1201 (Kennin 1), the year before Priest Jakuren died.
"Haru no minato" (the harbor of spring) is a coined phrase, used to mean the place where spring finally ends up.

In Japanese

暮くれてゆく 春はるの湊みなとは 知しらねども
霞かすみに落おつる 宇治うじの柴舟しばぶね kureteyuku harunominatoha shiranedomo
kasuminiotsuru ujinoshibabune Shin Kokin Wakashū, Priest Jakuren

In English

I do not know where the harbor is where the passing spring will end up, but the brushwood boats of the Uji River drift down as if falling into the haze that hangs over the river below.
A Poem About a Snail
The next poem looks kindly on a small creature, and shows Priest Jakuren's gentle nature.

In Japanese

牛うしの子こに ふまるな庭にわの かたつぶり
角つのありとても 身みをなたのみそ ushinokoni fumarunaniwano katatsuburi
tsunoaritotemo mionatanomiso Jakuren Hōshi-shū, Priest Jakuren

In English

Don't get stepped on by the calf, little snail in the garden. Just because you have horns, don't trust yourself too much.
