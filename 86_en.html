Poem 86
嘆なげけとて 月つきやは物ものを 思おもはわする
かこち顔がおなる わが涙なみだかな
Author: Priest Saigyō (1118–1190)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
O'ercome with pity for this world, My tears obscure my sight; I wonder, can it be the moon Whose melancholy light Has saddened me to-night?
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Did the moon ask me to grieve? These tears on my face — it is as if I am blaming the moon for making me feel this way.
Commentary
This poem was composed on the assigned topic of "love beneath the moon." It captures the feeling of a heart quietly enduring sorrow, shedding tears for a lost love while pretending, almost, that the moon itself is to blame.
About the Poet
Priest Saigyō, born Satō Norikiyo, served Retired Emperor Toba as a Palace Guard and held the post of Left Guards Lieutenant, but at the age of twenty-three he abandoned both his post and his family to become a monk. One theory holds that he took this step to sever his feelings after falling in love with Retired Emperor Toba's consort, Empress Taiken. After taking Buddhist vows, he became a wandering poet, traveling to Tōhoku, the Chūgoku region, Shikoku, and beyond, composing many waka in dialogue with the natural world he encountered on his travels.

The Poet's Feelings (A Personal Interpretation)

The clear moon says nothing at all.
And still, deep inside, my heart quietly stirs.

Unable to speak my longing aloud,
I let my tears fall, hidden in the moonlight.
