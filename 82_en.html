Poem 82
思おもひいわび さても命いのちは あるものを
憂うきにたへえぬは 涙なみだなりけり
Author: Priest Dōin (1090–c. 1182)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
How sad and gloomy is the world, This world of sin and woe! Ah! while I drift along Life's stream, Tossed helpless to and fro, My tears will ever flow.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I am exhausted with sorrow, yet somehow I am still alive. What cannot endure is not my life — but my tears.
Commentary
The poet grieves over a lover's coldness and the pain of lost love. Though overwhelmed by sorrow, he shows his resolve to go on living.
About the Poet
Priest Dōin (Fujiwara no Atsuyori) worked in the court stables and reached the fifth rank. Wishing to write good poems, he walked from Kyoto to Sumiyoshi Shrine every month until he was 70 or 80. After becoming a monk past 80, he still joined poetry gatherings in his 90s, eagerly listening to critiques.

Appendix
A Stingy Man Mocked as "Hadaka no Uma no Suke"
The Kojidan (a collection of tales) tells a story from the time when Priest Dōin was Assistant Head of the Right Bureau of Horses (Uma no suke). He was stingy and kept putting off giving his men, the horse keepers, the clothes he had promised them as a reward. Losing patience, the horse keepers dragged him down from his ox carriage and stripped him of everything, from his court robes to his shoes and even his loincloth. People mocked him as "Hadaka no Uma no Suke" (the Naked Assistant Head of Horses), a pun joining his title with the word for "naked."
His Passion for Waka in Old Age
The Mumyōshō, a book on poetry by Kamo no Chōmei, tells stories about how passionate Priest Dōin was about waka.
Until he was 70 or 80, Dōin walked every month to Sumiyoshi Shrine in Osaka, which honors the god of waka, to pray, "Please let me write fine poems." When he grew hard of hearing, he sat right next to the reader at poetry contests and listened closely. Though he was a monk, he cared a great deal about winning, and when he lost a contest, he later went to the judge's house and wept in earnest. The Mumyōshō even tells what happened after his death: when Fujiwara no Toshinari chose 18 of Dōin's poems for the Senzai Wakashū, Dōin appeared in his dream and wept for joy, so Toshinari added two more, for a total of 20.
