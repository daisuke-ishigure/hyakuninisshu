Poem 25
名なにし負おはば 逢坂山おうさかやまの さねかづずら
人ひとにしられで くるよしもがな
Author: Minister of the Right of Sanjō (873–932)
Source: Gosen Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
Hear thou art as modest as The little creeping spray Upon Mount Ōsaka, which hides Beneath the grass; then, pray, Wander with me to-day.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
If the name of Ōsaka carries its meaning — then let me secretly follow the vine I draw toward me, and come to you unseen.
Note: "Ōsaka" is both a place name and a pun on the verb *au* ("to meet"). The vine (*kazura*) reflects the wordplay of the original poem: to pull or draw the vine (*kuru*) and to come (*kuru*) to one's beloved unseen.
Commentary
As Minister of the Right, the poet was so high-ranking that meeting a lover was not easy, so the poem may hold his wish to meet her secretly. People often sent a poem with the object it named; he may have sent a sanekazura vine with this one.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Sadakata was the father of Middle Counselor Asatada. He was called "the Minister of the Right of Sanjō" because his home was in Sanjō. A relative of the emperor by marriage, he rose to Minister of the Right and supported the poets Ki no Tsurayuki and Ōshikōchi no Mitsune.

The Minister of the Right of Sanjō belonged to the Northern House of the Fujiwara and was a son of Fujiwara no Takafuji. His real name was Fujiwara no Sadakata. His elder or younger sister with the same mother, Taneko, was a consort of Emperor Uda. Sadakata is thought to have risen as high as Minister of the Right because he was a relative of Emperor Daigo through Daigo's mother.

Sadakata's fifth son, Middle Counselor Asatada, also has a poem in the Hyakunin Isshu. A famous story says that he was a big eater: the doctor told him to eat rice with hot or cold water poured over it, but he ate so much of it that it did no good.

However, in the Konjaku Monogatarishū of the late Heian period, the hero of this story was originally Fujiwara no Asahira, Sadakata's sixth son. The author of Hyakunin Isshu Yūwa, written in the late Edo period, seems to have mixed up the two men and told it as a story about the poet Middle Counselor Asatada.

Sadakata's great-grandchildren include Minamoto no Tomoko, Fujiwara no Michinaga's wife, and Fujiwara no Nobutaka, Murasaki Shikibu's husband.

He and Fujiwara no Kanesuke (Murasaki Shikibu's great-grandfather) were cousins. Both served Emperors Uda and Daigo and helped make the poetry world of the court flourish.

Appendix
A Poem That Also Begins "Na ni shi owaba"
Here is another famous poem that begins "Na ni shi owaba."
Ariwara no Narihira wrote it in a boat on the Sumida River, and it appears in "The Journey East" in The Tales of Ise. Later, a dumpling shop on a nearby street began selling Kototoi Dango, named after this poem. The dumplings became popular, and it is said that the nearby bridge was named Kototoi Bridge and the nearby street Kototoi Street. By the way, the miyakodori is the black-headed gull, which is now known as the official bird of Tokyo.
