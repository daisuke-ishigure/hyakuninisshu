Poem 81
ほととぎす 鳴なきつる方かたを ながむれば
ただ有明ありあけの 月つきぞ残のこれる
Author: Later Tokudaiji Minister of the Left (1139–1192)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Summer
English Verse Translation
The cuckoo's echo dies away, And lo! the branch is bare I only see the morning moon, Whose light is fading there Before the daylight's glare.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I looked toward where the cuckoo had just called — and all that remained was the pale moon at dawn.
Commentary
Written on the theme "hearing the hototogisu at dawn." The bird's first call of the year told Heian people that summer had come. Nobles stayed up all night to hear it, and the poet may have been waiting too.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Sanesada was a cousin of Fujiwara no Sadaie, compiler of the Hyakunin Isshu. A fine poet, he was also famous as a musician. His grandfather Saneyoshi had also been Minister of the Left and was called the "Tokudaiji Minister of the Left," so Sanesada was called the "Later" Tokudaiji Minister of the Left.

The Call of the Hototogisu
The Dawn Moon and the Phases of the Moon

The ariake no tsuki (dawn moon) is the moon that is still in the sky after the night ends. From about the 15th day of the lunar month until about the 29th, the moon can be seen in the sky at dawn.

In the Heian period, only privileged people had calendars. So for ordinary people, the changing shape of the moon probably worked as a kind of natural calendar.

Poems About the Hototogisu

For Heian nobles, the first call of the hototogisu (a kind of cuckoo), which announced the coming of summer, was a specially elegant moment.
Here are two summer poems about the hototogisu.

The next poem is by the Mother of the Right Captain Michitsuna and is in her personal collection, the Michitsuna no Haha-shū.

In Japanese

都人みやこびと ねてまつらめや ほととぎす
今いまぞ山辺やまべを 鳴なきていづなり miyakobito netematsurameya hototogisu
imazoyamabeo nakiteizunari Michitsuna no Haha-shū, the Mother of the Right Captain Michitsuna

In English

Would the people of the capital wait for it asleep? Surely they stay awake.
Right now the hototogisu is crying as it sets out from the hills.

The next poem is by Izumi Shikibu and is in the Goshūi Wakashū.

In Japanese

桜色さくらいろに そめし衣ころもを ぬぎかへて
山やまほととぎす 今日きょうよりぞ待まつ sakuraironi someshikoromoo nugikahete
yamahototogisu kyouyorizomatsu Goshūi Wakashū, Izumi Shikibu

In English

I have taken off my robe dyed the color of cherry blossoms,
and from today I wait for the mountain hototogisu.

A Song That Feels Like the End of the Heian Period

After Taira no Kiyomori moved the capital to Fukuhara in 1180, Fujiwara no Sanesada went back to Kyoto and composed the following imayō (a popular song style of the time).

In Japanese

ふるき都みやこを 来きて見みれば 浅茅あさぢが原はらとぞ 荒あれにける
月つきの光ひかりは 隈くまなくて 秋風あきかぜのみぞ 身みにはしむ furukimiyakoo kitemireba asajigaharatozo arenikeru
tsukinohikariha kumanakute akikazenomizo minihashimu The Tale of the Heike, Fujiwara no Sanesada

In English

When I came to see the old capital, it had fallen to ruin, like a field overgrown with low reeds. The moonlight shines everywhere, but only the autumn wind, all the more empty, pierces me to the bone.

The Legend of Naishi-iwa Rock
Sanesada appears in a sad love story handed down on Miyajima, the legend of Naishi-iwa Rock (Naishi-iwa densetsu), so here it is.

When Sanesada was an Upper Counselor, he was not getting the promotions he hoped for and was thinking of becoming a monk. On the advice of an aide, he came to stay and pray at Itsukushima Shrine, which the Taira clan deeply worshipped. There he fell in love at first sight with Ariko, a shrine maiden of Itsukushima and a master of the biwa (a Japanese lute), and wrote the following poem.

In Japanese

山やまの端はに 契ちぎりて出いでむ 夜半よわの月つき
めぐり逢あうべき 折おりを知しらねど yamanohani chigiriteidemu yowanotsuki
meguriaubeki orioshiranedo The Legend of Naishi-iwa Rock, Fujiwara no Sanesada

In English

As if by promise, the midnight moon rises over the mountain ridge.
Though I do not know when I will meet you again.

When Sanesada left for the capital, Ariko ran after his ship, stood on a rock by the shore, and watched until the ship disappeared from sight. After that, Ariko could not forget her love for Sanesada and went to the capital. But the difference in their social standing kept them apart, and she was not even allowed to come near him. In despair, Ariko rowed a boat out onto the sea at Sumiyoshi in Settsu Province and played her biwa as she drifted on the waves. But her tears overflowed and she could no longer play. She composed the following poem, full of grief, and threw herself into the sea.

In Japanese

はかなしや 浪なみの下したにも いりぬべし
月つきの都みやこの 人ひとや見みるとて hakanashiya naminoshitanimo irinubeshi
tsukinomiyakono hitoyamirutote The Legend of Naishi-iwa Rock, Ariko no Naishi

In English

How fleeting! I will go down beneath the waves, thinking that the one in the capital, who wrote of the moon to me, may be watching.

Tsukioka Yoshitoshi's One Hundred Aspects of the Moon

This ukiyo-e print is from the series One Hundred Aspects of the Moon (Tsuki Hyakushi) by Tsukioka Yoshitoshi, an ukiyo-e artist active from the end of the Edo period to the early Meiji period.

It shows the last moments of Ariko from the legend of Naishi-iwa Rock.
