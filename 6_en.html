Poem 6
かささぎの 渡わたせる橋はしに おく霜しもの
白しろきをみれば 夜よぞふけにける
Author: Middle Counselor Yakamochi (c. 718–785)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Winter
English Verse Translation
When on the Magpies' Bridge I see The Hoar-frost King has cast His sparkling mantle, well I know The night is nearly past, Daylight approaches fast.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Looking up at the Milky Way that magpies wing across — its white shimmer seems to be scattered frost. The night deepens.
Commentary
The Tanabata legend came to Japan from China in the Nara period. The poem describes the Milky Way across the winter night sky, sparkling as if frost had settled on it.
About the Poet
Ōtomo no Yakamochi was a Nara-period poet thought to have compiled the Man'yōshū. He clashed with the Fujiwara clan and was demoted and promoted many times before reaching Middle Counselor. After his death he was accused of a plot and stripped of his rank, but some 20 years later Emperor Kanmu, fearing his angry spirit, pardoned him.

In 785, Middle Counselor Fujiwara no Tanetsugu, who was overseeing the building of the new capital Nagaoka-kyō, was shot dead with arrows. Ōtomo no Takeyoshi was arrested as one of the killers, and after questioning, Ōtomo no Yakamochi (Middle Counselor, Junior Third Rank) was named as the leader of the plot, and the Ōtomo clan was punished as a group. Although Yakamochi had died about a month before the murder, he was refused burial and removed from the official records. But in 806, more than 20 years after his death, Yakamochi was cleared and given back the Junior Third Rank.

Emperor Kanmu's younger brother, Prince Sawara, was also said to be involved. He lost his place as crown prince and is said to have died after refusing food on the way to exile in Awaji. Before becoming crown prince, he had been a monk at Tōdai-ji Temple. One reason for moving the capital from Heijō-kyō to Nagaoka-kyō was to get away from the powerful Buddhist temples of Heijō-kyō. Because Prince Sawara had close ties to Tōdai-ji, he may have been seen as against the move and suspected of the murder.

Later, in 866, the Ōtenmon gate of the Heian palace was set on fire (the Ōtenmon Incident). Tomo no Yoshio, an Upper Counselor, was named as the culprit and exiled, and once again his whole clan was punished with him. After this incident, the Ōtomo clan fell into decline.

As the Ōtomo clan declined, the Northern branch of the Fujiwara grew powerful, so some believe the Northern Fujiwara were secretly behind both incidents.

Appendix
Tanabata

Tanabata is a legend that began in China. The Weaver Girl, who wove clothes by order of the Emperor of Heaven (the highest god), married the Cowherd. After that, the two only played and stopped working. The gods' clothes became ragged, and the Cowherd's cattle grew thin and sick. In anger, the Emperor of Heaven separated them on opposite banks of the Milky Way. Then the two did nothing but cry and could not work, so he allowed them to meet once a year, on the night of the seventh day of the seventh month. On that night, magpies form a bridge so the two can meet. This is the story of Tanabata.

In Japan, the Chinese Tanabata story was joined with an old Japanese legend of the weaving maiden (Tanabatatsume), and the Cowherd came to be called Hikoboshi. The custom of writing wishes on strips of paper in five colors and hanging them on bamboo is unique to Japan.

The Man'yōshū
The Man'yōshū, compiled in the 7th–8th centuries, is the oldest surviving collection of Japanese poetry. Hiragana did not yet exist, so it was written in man'yōgana, Chinese characters used for their sounds or meanings, the way hiragana is used today.
Besides about 4,200 tanka (5-7-5-7-7 syllables), it has about 260 chōka, long poems that repeat lines of 5 and 7 syllables and end with 5-7-7, and about 60 sedōka, which repeat 5-7-7 twice.
The Top 4 Poets in the Man'yōshū

This graph shows the top 4 poets in the Man'yōshū, along with the number of poems by unknown authors. The Man'yōshū holds 4,516 poems by people of every class, from farmers to emperors, written all over Japan from Tōhoku to Kyushu. Nearly half of them, about 2,000, are by unknown authors. The poets with the most poems are:

  • Ōtomo no Yakamochi: 473 poems
  • Kakinomoto no Hitomaro: 88 poems
  • Lady Ōtomo no Sakanoue
    (who raised Yakamochi): 84 poems
  • Ōtomo no Tabito
    (Yakamochi's father): 78 poems
Takaoka, Home of the Man'yōshū

The photo shows the statue of Ōtomo no Yakamochi on Mount Futagami (274 m) in Takaoka City, Toyama Prefecture. Yakamochi lived in Etchū Province for about five years, from 746 to 751, as its governor.

Of the 4,516 poems in the Man'yōshū, 473, about one in ten, are by Yakamochi. Nearly half of them, 223 poems, were written while he was in Etchū.

Because Yakamochi wrote so many poems there, Takaoka is called "the home of the Man'yōshū."

A Poem About a Calm Spring Day
Yakamochi went to Etchū Province (present-day Toyama Prefecture) as its governor in 746. He wrote this poem there.

In Japanese

春はるの苑その 紅くれないにほふ 桃ももの花はな
下した照てる道みちに 出いで立たつ娘子おとめ harunosono kurenainihofu momonohana
shitaterumichini idetatsuotome Man'yōshū, Ōtomo no Yakamochi

In English

The spring garden glows with crimson peach blossoms.
On the path lit by their color, a young girl appears and stands.
A Poem of Spring Sadness
Yakamochi wrote the next poem in Yamato Province (present-day Nara Prefecture) in 753.

In Japanese

うらうらに 照てれる春日かすがに
ひばり上あがり
心こころ悲かなしも
独ひとりし思おもへば uraurani tererukasugani hibariagari
kokorokanashimo hitorishiomoheba Man'yōshū, Ōtomo no Yakamochi

In English

On a bright, mild spring day, a skylark soars high into the sky.
Alone with my thoughts, I feel drawn into the sky, and sad.
A Poem for the New Year
Yakamochi went to Inaba Province (present-day Tottori Prefecture) as its governor in 758 and wrote this poem on New Year's Day the next year. It is the very last poem in the Man'yōshū. "Iya shike" means "more and more often," linking the falling snow with good things (yogoto).

In Japanese

新あたらしき 年としの初はじめの 初春はつはるの
今日けふ降ふる雪ゆきの いや重しけ吉事よごと atarashiki toshinohajimeno hatsuharuno
kefufuruyukino iyashikeyogoto Man'yōshū, Ōtomo no Yakamochi

In English

At the start of the new year, in early spring,
may good things pile up more and more, like the snow falling today.

Poems by Lady Kasa, Who Loved Yakamochi in Vain

Lady Kasa (Kasa no Iratsume) loved Yakamochi with all her heart and wrote many poems for him. Twenty-nine of her poems are in the Man'yōshū, and every one is about her love for Yakamochi. But her love was never returned. Here is one of them.

In Japanese

思おもふにし 死しにするものに
あらませば
千遍ちたびそわれは 死しに返かえらまし omofunishi shinisurumononi aramaseba
chitabisowareha shinikaeramashi Man'yōshū, Lady Kasa

In English

If one could die of love,
I would have died a thousand times over.
Mibu no Tadamine's Poem Based on This One
Mibu no Tadamine, who served as an attendant to Fujiwara no Sadakuni, wrote the next poem borrowing from Yakamochi's (honkadori). One night, Sadakuni, drunk, dropped in on Minister of the Left Fujiwara no Tokihira, who asked suspiciously, "Did you just stop by on your way somewhere?" Tadamine then made this poem on the spot, borrowing from Yakamochi's poem. The Yamato Monogatari says his quick wit eased the mood.

In Japanese

かささぎの 渡わたせる橋はしの 霜しもの上うえを
夜半よわにふみわけ ことさらにこそ kasasagino wataseruhashino shimonoueo
yowanifumiwake kotosaranikoso Yamato Monogatari, Mibu no Tadamine

In English

Treading at midnight through the frost on the steps of your hall, like the bridge the magpies make,
I have come here to you on purpose.

Kasasagi Bridge

In Katano City, Osaka Prefecture, flows the Amanogawa River, named after the Milky Way (Amanogawa) of the Tanabata legend. "Kasasagi Bridge" crosses its lowest stretch. The bridge's name also comes from the magpies (kasasagi) of the Tanabata story.

It is about a 5-minute walk from Hirakatashi Station on the Keihan Line.

(Photo taken August 10, 2026)

The Amanogawa River, Visited by Ariwara no Narihira

Going upstream along the Amanogawa, you reach Kisaichi Waterside Plaza (Kisaichi Mizube Plaza), where you can play in the river and walk in rich nature.

In the Heian period, the area around Katano was a hunting ground for the imperial family and nobles. Ariwara no Narihira is known to have come here for falconry, and a stone monument with the poem he is said to have written here stands quietly nearby. Next to it is a charming little bridge, Kisaichi Kasasagi Bridge.

It is about a 5-minute walk from Kisaichi Station on the Keihan Katano Line.

(Photo taken August 10, 2026)

Narihira's Poem About the Amanogawa
This poem by Ariwara no Narihira is carved on the monument at Kisaichi Waterside Plaza. What did Narihira feel about the Tanabata legend?

In Japanese

狩かり暮くらし 棚機たなばたつめに 宿やどからむ
天あまの河原かわらに 我われは来きにけり karikurashi tanabatatsumeni yadokaramu
amanokawarani warehakinikeri Kokin Wakashū, Ariwara no Narihira

In English

I have hunted until evening, so tonight I will ask the Weaver Girl for a place to stay, for I have come to the banks of the River of Heaven.
