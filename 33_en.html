Poem 33
久方ひさかたの 光ひかりのどけき 春はるの日ひに
しづ心こころなく 花はなの散ちるらむん
Author: Ki no Tomonori (c. 845–907)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Spring
English Verse Translation
The spring has come, and once again The sun shines in the sky; So gently smile the heavens, that It almost makes me cry, When blossoms droop and die.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
In the peaceful light of a spring day, why do the cherry blossoms scatter so restlessly?
Commentary
This was likely written for a contest on the theme "falling blossoms." On a calm, windless spring day the cherry blossoms still fall, and the poet asks why they hurry so, as if they had no peace of heart. It shows how quickly blossoms fade and the poet's gentle feeling as he watches.
About the Poet
Ki no Tomonori was a cousin of Ki no Tsurayuki. He was one of the compilers of the Kokin Wakashū but died before it was finished. His poems were highly praised, many appear in imperial anthologies, and he is one of the Thirty-Six Immortal Poets.

Ki no Tomonori was a cousin of Ki no Tsurayuki.
At the order of Emperor Daigo, Ki no Tomonori, Ki no Tsurayuki, Mibu no Tadamine, and Ōshikōchi no Mitsune served as the compilers of the Kokin Wakashū, but Tomonori died while it was still being compiled.

Appendix
A Poem About Plum Blossoms
Ki no Tomonori is famous for his Hyakunin Isshu poem about cherry blossoms, but he also wrote about plum blossoms.
The headnote in the Kokin Wakashū says, "Written when he broke off a branch of plum blossoms and sent it to someone."

In Japanese

君きみならで 誰たれに見みせむ 梅うめの花はな
色いろをも香かをも 知しる人ひとぞ知しる Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tomonori

In English

Who else but you should I show these plum blossoms to?
Only you truly know the beauty of both their color and their scent.

A Poem About the Coming of Autumn
Ki no Tomonori wrote this poem based on the following Chinese story. Su Wu, an envoy of the Han dynasty, was held captive for a long time by the Xiongnu. The Xiongnu insisted that Su Wu was dead, but he tied a letter to the leg of a wild goose and let it fly, and the goose carried the letter to the capital, so he was able to return home safely.

In Japanese

秋風あきかぜに 初雁はつかりが音ねぞ 聞きこゆなる
誰たがたまづさを かけて来きつらむ Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tomonori

In English

On the autumn wind I hear the cries of the first wild geese.
Whose letter have they brought with them?

Ki no Tsurayuki's Poem Mourning
Tomonori's Early Death
Ki no Tomonori died while the Kokin Wakashū was still being compiled. His cousin Ki no Tsurayuki, grieving his death that came far too soon, wrote the next poem.

In Japanese

明日あす知しらぬ 我わが身みと思おもへど
暮くれぬ間まの
今日きょうは人ひとこそ
かなしかりけれ

Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tsurayuki

In English

I do not know if I myself will live to see tomorrow, but today, while I am still alive, I cannot stop grieving for him.

The Kana Preface (Kanajo)
The preface to the Kokin Wakashū written in kana (the Japanese syllabary), as opposed to the preface written in Chinese.
Changing Tastes in Flowers
Until the Nara period, plum blossoms were the most popular, but from the Heian period on, cherry blossoms became more popular.
