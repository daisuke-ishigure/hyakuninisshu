Poem 42
契ちぎりきな かたみに袖そでを しぼりつつ
末すえの松山まつやま 波なみこさじとは
Author: Kiyohara no Motosuke (908–990)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
Our sleeves, all wet with tears, attest That you and I agree That to each other we'll be true, Till Pine-tree Hill shall be Sunk far beneath the sea.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
We promised each other, wringing out our tear-soaked sleeves, that our love would last forever, never to be swept away like the waves beyond Suenomatsu.
Commentary
Sue-no-Matsuyama is a scenic spot near the coast in Tagajō, Miyagi Prefecture. It was said that even a great tsunami could never cross it, and it appears in many poems. Here it stands for love that never changes.
About the Poet
Kiyohara no Motosuke helped compile the Gosen Wakashū. He was the grandson of Kiyohara no Fukayabu, and his daughter was Sei Shōnagon, author of The Pillow Book. She respected him so much that she said she would not write poems for fear of shaming his name.

The Kiyohara clan includes many descendants of Prince Toneri, a son of Emperor Tenmu. Like his grandfather, Kiyohara no Fukayabu, Kiyohara no Motosuke was a master of waka. On the orders of Emperor Murakami, he compiled the Gosen Wakashū together with Ōnakatomi no Yoshinobu, Minamoto no Shitagō, Sakanoue no Mochiki, and Ki no Tokibumi (Ki no Tsurayuki's son). They worked in the Shōyōsha, a building in the women's quarters of the palace. Because pear trees grew in its garden, they were called the "Five Men of the Pear Pavilion" (Nashitsubo no Gonin).

Motosuke had his daughter, Sei Shōnagon, when he was about 58. He lived a very long life for his time, reaching the age of 83.

Appendix
The Source Poem (Honka)
Motosuke's Hyakunin Isshu poem is based on the following poem.
The original is a poem that vows love that never changes.

In Japanese

君きみをおきて あだし心ごころを 我わが持もたば
末すえの松山まつやま 波なみも越こえなむ kimiookite adashigokoroo wagamotaba
suenomatsuyama namimokoenamu Kokin Wakashū, Anonymous

In English

If I should ever give my heart to anyone but you,
the waves would surely cross Sue-no-Matsuyama, which no wave ever crosses.
Sue-no-Matsuyama (Tagajō, Miyagi Prefecture)

A small hill in Tagajō, Miyagi Prefecture.
In 869, the Jōgan Earthquake struck, as large as the Great East Japan Earthquake of 2011. Even then, the tsunami did not go over Sue-no-Matsuyama, so in waka it was often used as an image of something that could never happen. During the Great East Japan Earthquake, the town around Sue-no-Matsuyama was flooded up to 2 meters deep, but news reports said that the people who took shelter on the hill were safe.

Sue-no-Matsuyama (Tagajō):
The Lay of the Land

Motosuke wrote "nami kosaji" (no wave will cross it). Is Sue-no-Matsuyama really a place that waves cannot cross? We used the map website of the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan to show the land around it.

The cross-section and the color-coded elevation map show that the Sue-no-Matsuyama area rises a little higher than the land around it.

Just as its name ("pine hill") suggests, pine trees still grow on Sue-no-Matsuyama today. Perhaps there were pines here in the Heian period too. Pines, a symbol of long life, and a place no wave can cross: perhaps because of these images of things that never change, Sue-no-Matsuyama became an utamakura (a famous place in poetry) for poems about unchanging love.

Sue-no-Matsuyama (Ichinohe, Iwate Prefecture)

Ichinohe, Iwate Prefecture, between Ichinohe and Fukuoka, two post towns on the old Ōshū Kaidō road. Tagajō in Miyagi Prefecture is thought to be the most likely site of Sue-no-Matsuyama, but one theory says it is Namiuchi Pass in Ichinohe.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e print is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period.
It shows a scene to go with the Hyakunin Isshu poem.
Because pine trees are drawn near the sea, Kuniyoshi probably had in mind Sue-no-Matsuyama in Tagajō, Miyagi Prefecture, near Sendai Port.
