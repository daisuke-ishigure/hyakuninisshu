Poem 59
やすらはわで 寝ねなましものを 小夜さよ更ふけて
かたぶくまでの 月つきを見みしかな
Author: Akazome Emon (c. 956–c. 1041)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
Waiting and hoping for thy step, Sleepless in bed I lie, All through the night, until the moon, Leaving her post on high, Slips sideways down the sky.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I waited and waited without sleep, watching the moon sink slowly toward the horizon in the deepening night. How I regret it.
Commentary
The Go Shūi Wakashū says Fujiwara no Michitaka was courting one of Akazome Emon's sisters. One night he promised to come but never did, so Akazome Emon wrote this poem for her sister, gently showing her displeasure.
About the Poet
Akazome Emon's name comes from her father's post in the Right Gate Guards (Uemon). She served Tomoko, wife of Fujiwara no Michinaga, and then Tomoko's daughter Akiko, empress of Emperor Ichijō, knowing Murasaki Shikibu and Izumi Shikibu there. She is known as the author of the main part of A Tale of Flowering Fortunes.

Appendix
A Poem About Autumn Leaves Brought Back from Hatsuse
Akazome Emon wrote this poem about autumn leaves she brought back as a souvenir from a pilgrimage to Hatsuse (Hase-dera Temple).

In Japanese

つとにとて 折おりし紅葉もみぢは 枯かれにけり
嵐あらしのいたく 吹ふきしまぎれに Akazome Emon-shū

In English

The autumn leaves I broke off to bring home as a souvenir (tsuto) have withered, while the storm was blowing so hard.
A Poem Praying for Her Son Takachika's Recovery
Akazome Emon offered this poem at Sumiyoshi Taisha Shrine when her son Takachika was seriously ill and near death. Takachika was then Governor of Izumi, and Akazome Emon is said to have hurried there from Kyoto.

In Japanese

代かはわらむと 祈いのる命いのちは 惜おしからで
さても別わかれむ ことぞ悲かなしき Shika Wakashū, Akazome Emon

In English

I do not mind giving my own life to take his place, but if my prayer is answered, it is sad that I must part from my son.
Poems Exchanged with Her Husband, Ōe no Masahira
When Akazome Emon had a baby, the nurse they hired did not have much milk, and Akazome Emon and her husband, Ōe no Masahira, exchanged the following poems about it.
Poem by Her Husband, Ōe no Masahira

In Japanese

果はかなくも 思おもひけるかな 乳ちもなくて
博士はかせの家いえの 乳母うばせむとは Go Shūi Wakashū, Ōe no Masahira

In English

What a foolish idea, to be the nurse in a scholar's house with no milk!

Ōe no Masahira makes a pun (kakekotoba) on chi (milk) and chi (knowledge): besides having little milk, he hints, "How dare she try to work in a scholar's house with no learning!" Masahira was one of the most learned men of his day, but he may have been a little proud of it.

Akazome Emon's Poem

In Japanese

さもあらばあれ 大和心やまとごころし 賢かしこくば
細乳ほどぢにつけて あらすばかりぞ Go Shūi Wakashū, Akazome Emon

In English

Never mind that. If she has a good Yamato-gokoro, let her stay, even with little milk.

Yamato-gokoro means a graceful, gentle heart.
Akazome Emon defends the nurse as a person with an honest heart.

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures. The picture is said to show women of the pleasure quarters. The woman in the middle seems to twist her body restlessly. Perhaps, like the poet, she is waiting for her lover.

Ukiyo-e by Tsukioka Yoshitoshi

Tsukioka Yoshitoshi was an ukiyo-e artist active from the end of the Edo period to the middle of the Meiji period. Akazome Emon also appears in his One Hundred Aspects of the Moon (Tsuki Hyakushi), a series of scenes connected with the moon.
