Central Government Structure: The Two Offices and Eight Ministries (Nikan-Hasshō)

Imagine a system of government in which religious authority and political power stood side by side under the emperor. This was the structure of the Heian court.

This system was known as 二官八省Nikan-Hasshō — the "Two Offices and Eight Ministries."

Beneath the emperor were two parallel institutions: the 神祇官Jingikan (Office of Shinto), responsible for state religious rites, and the 太政官Dajōkan (Grand Council of State), which directed national governance. Eight ministries operated under the authority of the Dajōkan.

The Two Offices (Nikan)

Jingikan (神祇官) — Office of Shinto

The Jingikan oversaw state religious ceremonies and supervised Shinto priests throughout the country. In the formal ritsuryō system, it stood alongside the Dajōkan as an institution parallel under the emperor. In practice, however, the Dajōkan exercised far greater political influence.

Dajōkan (太政官) — Grand Council of State

The Dajōkan was the central governing body of the Heian state. Unlike modern systems based on the separation of powers, it concentrated executive, legislative, and judicial authority in a single institution.

Its senior officials — including the daijō-daijin, sadaijin, udaijin, as well as senior and middle counselors (dainagon, chūnagon) and councillors (sangi) — met in a policy council known as jin no sadame to deliberate on matters of state.

The Shōnagon originally served as a close aide to the emperor. However, after the establishment of the Kurōdodokoro (Chamberlain's Office) by Emperor Saga in 810, many of these functions were transferred to the chamberlains. The Shōnagon thereafter focused mainly on administrative duties such as managing imperial seals and post-horse bells (ekirei).

The Left and Right Benkan bureaus functioned as the administrative core of the Dajōkan, transmitting decisions to the eight ministries in the form of concrete orders. In addition, extra-statutory offices (ryōgenokan) such as the Naidaijin (Minister of the Interior) played important roles in governance. Titles such as Jun-daijin (Acting Minister) were also used to grant ministerial-level status to individuals outside the standard hierarchy.

In the Heian court, power was organized with remarkable precision. Every official position was tied to rank, and each rank determined the offices a person could hold.

At the center of this system stood the Dajōkan, supported by a clearly defined internal hierarchy.

Structure of the Dajōkan and Its Sub-Organisations

In addition to this basic "ministry → bureau → office" structure, there also existed a category known as gen (監). These were independent agencies, often related to military, diplomatic, or guard functions, and ranked between shiki and ryō.

Many of these organisations disappeared during the early Heian period. For example, the Tonerigen was absorbed into the Ōtoneri-ryō, while the Udoneri-gen came under the control of the Nakatsukasa-shō.

The title gen survived mainly in regional administration, particularly in the Dazaifu, where officials were known as Daigen (大監) and Shōgen (少監).

Regency Power: Sesshō, Kampaku, and Nairan

These three positions stood above the Nikan-Hasshō system and effectively controlled the entire administration of the state.

The Eight Ministries (Hasshō)

Nakatsukasa-shō (中務省) — Ministry of Central Affairs

The Nakatsukasa-shō was the most important of the eight ministries, positioned closest to the emperor.

It handled core state affairs, including the drafting of imperial edicts and the management of official appointments. Because of its proximity to the emperor, it played a central role in coordinating government administration.

Office Function
Taikōtaigō-gū-shiki (太皇太后宮職) Served the empress dowager (grandmother generation) and managed her household affairs.
Kōtaigō-gū-shiki (皇太后宮職) Served the empress dowager (mother generation) and managed her household.
Kōgō-gū-shiki (皇后宮職) Served the empress (the emperor's principal consort).
Chūgū-shiki (中宮職) Served the chūgū (the emperor's principal consort, distinct title).
Ōtoneri-ryō (大舎人寮) Responsible for palace security and ceremonial duties; guards joined imperial processions.
Zusho-ryō (図書寮) Managed books and Buddhist images; also collected and stored texts, produced sutra copies, and made paper and ink.
Kura-ryō (内蔵寮) Managed the emperor's private assets — the "imperial treasury," receiving offerings and managing their disbursement.
Nuidono-ryō (縫殿寮) Made imperial garments and managed the personnel of female palace officials.
Onmyō-ryō (陰陽寮) Handled astronomy, calendrical calculations, and divination — determining auspicious dates and times.
Takumi-ryō (内匠寮) Made palace utensils and ceremonial objects, and decorated buildings.

Shikibu-shō (式部省) — Ministry of Ceremonial

Managed the Daigaku-ryō (Imperial University) and handled the personnel evaluation and appointment of civil officials. Also administered the Sanni-ryō (Bureau of Supernumerary Officials).

Office Function
Daigaku-ryō (大学寮) The national university; trained officials for government service.
Sanni-ryō (散位寮) Maintained lists of officials who held court rank but had no current post. Dispatched personnel to other offices on request. A uniquely Japanese institution with no equivalent in the Tang system; absorbed into the Shikibu-shō in 896.

Daigaku-ryō Curriculum

The Daigaku-ryō offered four specialist courses: Kidendō (Chinese history and literature), Myōgyōdō (Confucian classics), Myōhōdō (law), and Sandō (mathematics). In the Heian period, Kidendō became the dominant course.

Jibu-shō (治部省) — Ministry of Civil Affairs

Broadly responsible for court ceremonies, foreign diplomacy, and the administration of Buddhist and Shinto clergy. Its sub-offices included:

Office Function
Uta-ryō / Gagaku-ryō (雅楽寮) Court music, dance, and related education.
Genba-ryō (玄蕃寮) Administered Buddhist clergy and received foreign envoys. The Kōrokan guest houses in Kyoto, Osaka, and Fukuoka hosted envoys from Silla and Balhae.
Shoryō-ryō (諸陵寮) Managed imperial mausoleums and conducted imperial funeral rites. Elevated from a smaller office (shoryō-shi) to a full bureau during Emperor Saga's reign.

Minbu-shō (民部省) — Ministry of Popular Affairs

Controlled the nation's finances and household registers — combining the functions of a modern ministry of finance and ministry of internal affairs. A common misconception: it was the Minbu-shō, not the Ōkura-shō, that collected taxes. The Ōkura-shō was the guardian of the treasury.

Office Function
Kazue-ryō (主計寮) Compiled the national budget and calculated tax revenues — deciding how taxes (especially chō, local specialties delivered to the capital) would be collected and allocated.
Chikara-ryō (主税寮) Managed so (rice stored in provincial granaries) and audited local finances. The famous Shōsōin repository at Tōdaiji in Nara is similar in construction to the provincial shōsō granaries, though it was a temple storehouse rather than a government one.

Hyōbu-shō (兵部省) — Ministry of Military Affairs

The supreme headquarters for military and national defence — the equivalent of a modern Ministry of Defence. It also managed the Hayato, warriors from southern Kyushu, who served as palace guards and performed traditional dances; maintained the fire-and-smoke signal network (tobhi) for rapid communication; and oversaw state horse-breeding pastures (maki).

Office Function
Hayato-shi (隼人司) Administered and commanded the Hayato warriors from the Ōsumi and Satsuma peninsulas of southern Kyushu.

Gyōbu-shō (刑部省) — Ministry of Justice

The supreme judicial organ, determining the severity of crimes according to the penal codes (ritsuryō) and overseeing the execution of sentences. The five types of punishment were:

Chi (笞)
Light flogging: Beaten with a thin bamboo whip on the back or buttocks; 10–50 strokes in five gradations.
Jō (杖)
Heavy flogging: Beaten with a thicker rod; 60–100 strokes in five gradations.
Zu (徒)
Penal servitude: Forced labour at a government office for 1–3 years.
Ru (流)
Exile: Forced relocation to a distant province; three gradations based on distance (near, middle, far).
Shi (死)
Death: The ultimate penalty; two forms: strangulation (kō) and decapitation (zan).
Office Function
Shūgoku-shi (囚獄司) Managed the prison facilities (gokusha) — the equivalent of a modern correctional institution.

For serious cases or legally complex disputes, the Dajōkan issued the final verdict after reviewing the Gyōbu-shō's findings. From the mid-Heian period, as the Kebiishi (capital police) took over arrests, trials, and prison management, the Gyōbu-shō and its prison office became increasingly hollow.

Ōkura-shō (大蔵省) — Treasury Ministry

Received and safeguarded the goods (chō — cloth and regional produce) and precious metals collected by the Minbu-shō, carefully inspecting quality before storing them. The national treasury.

Office Function
Oribe-no-Tsukasa (織部司) Not merely a storage operation — this office created value by producing premium textiles such as brocade and damask from the yarns and cloth received as tax, using the finest craftsmanship of the day.

Kunai-shō (宮内省) — Ministry of the Imperial Household

The core agency for running the imperial court, managing everything from the emperor's food, clothing, and shelter to medical care and palace maintenance.

Office Function
Daizen-shiki (大膳職) Prepared large-scale ceremonial banquets.
Naizen-shi (内膳司) Prepared the emperor's daily meals and managed food safety (including tasting).
Ōi-ryō (大炊寮) Managed and supplied rice and grains for cooking.
Miki-no-Tsukasa (造酒司) Brewed sake, vinegar, porridge, and other fermented foods for court use.
Moitori-no-Tsukasa (主水司) Managed drinking water, ice and ice cellars, and ritual water supplies.
Moku-ryō (木工寮) Constructed and repaired palace buildings; produced wooden furniture and utensils.
Tonomori-ryō (主殿寮) Managed palace cleaning, lighting, firewood and charcoal, and bathing.
Kamon-ryō (掃部寮) Managed tatami mats and blinds; set up and cleaned ceremony venues.
Ten'yaku-ryō (典薬寮) Provided medical care, procured medicines, and trained physicians and acupuncturists.
Uneme-no-Tsukasa (采女司) Administered and educated the uneme — serving women submitted from the provinces to attend the empress.

Major Bureaus Outside the Eight Ministries

While the Nikan-Hasshō was the administrative backbone, some agencies dealing with military power and ceremonial arms were placed directly under the Dajōkan rather than any of the eight ministries.

Maryō (馬寮) — Bureau of Horses

An independent, high-autonomy organization directly under the Dajōkan, not subordinate to any ministry. Managed the breeding, training, and equipment of official horses, and oversaw horse pastures throughout the country. Divided into Left (Samaryō) and Right (Umaryō) bureaus.

Hyōgo-ryō (兵庫寮) — Bureau of Arms

Likewise directly under the Dajōkan, not subordinate to any ministry. Maintained and managed weapons and ceremonial arms (gijō).

Note: The Four-Tier Office Structure (Shitōkan)

Virtually every government office had four tiers of officials: kami (chief), suke (deputy), jō (controller), and sakan (recorder) — comparable to a modern organisation's president, general manager, section manager, and team leader.

There were exceptions. In the Dazaifu (the regional government of Kyushu) and the Konoefu (imperial guard), the third-tier (jō) post was called gen rather than jō. Likewise, the fourth-tier (sakan) had distinctive names in these offices — daiten and shōten in the Dazaifu, and shōsō in the Konoefu — reflecting the specialised military and diplomatic traditions of those organisations.

Note: Shishō (史生) — Junior Scribes

Below the four-tier managerial officials were shishō (史生), lower-ranking officials who actually drafted official documents and handled clerical work — comparable to modern administrative staff or secretaries. Largely invisible in the historical record, they were the people who kept the mountains of paperwork moving in Heian bureaucracy. They appear occasionally in historical fiction as minor but practically indispensable officials.
