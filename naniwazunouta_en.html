What Is the Naniwazu Poem
(Sakuya Kono Hana)?
The Scholar Wani and
the History of Learning to Write

The Naniwazu Poem ("Sakuya kono hana") is known as the joka (prefatory poem) of competitive karuta. In fact, this poem was also familiar to people of the Heian period as something to practice on when learning to write.

This column looks at the meaning of the poem and the tradition of the scholar Wani, its reputed composer, and then, through records left in wooden tablets (mokkan), the Kokin Wakashū and The Tale of Genji, explores the role the Naniwazu Poem has played in Japanese culture.

In Japanese

難波津なにわづに 咲さくやこの花はな 冬ふゆごもり
今いまは春はるべと 咲さくやこの花はな naniwazuni sakuyakonohana fuyugomori
imahaharubeto sakuyakonohana

In English

At Naniwa harbor the flowers are blooming! They kept to their buds all through the winter, and now, saying spring has come, the flowers are blooming!

About the Poet, the Scholar Wani

According to tradition, the poem was composed by Wani, a scholar who came to Japan from the kingdom of Baekje. The source of this attribution is the Japanese preface (Kanajo) of the Kokin Wakashū. The Nihon Shoki records that Wani came from Baekje in the reign of Emperor Ōjin and taught the classics to the crown prince Uji no Wakiiratsuko. In the Kojiki he appears under the name Wani Kishi, who is recorded as having presented ten volumes of the Analects and one volume of the Thousand Character Classic.

This tradition, however, cannot be taken as history just as it stands. The Thousand Character Classic that Wani is said to have brought was written in the first half of the 6th century by Zhou Xingsi, on the orders of Emperor Wu of Liang in China (r. 502–549). Emperor Ōjin, in whose reign Wani is said to have arrived, is placed around the early 5th century — which would mean Wani brought over a book that did not yet exist. This discrepancy was already noticed in the Edo period by the scholar Arai Hakuseki. Today it is quite a common view that Wani was not a real person, but an ancestor figure handed down by a later clan of immigrant descent who served the court as scribes.

The "Traditional Tomb of Wani" in Fujisaka, Hirakata City, Osaka Prefecture, likewise became known as his tomb after the Edo-period scholar Namikawa Seisho recorded it as such. It cannot at present be confirmed that this is the tomb of a historical Wani, but it is a fascinating site that keeps the tradition of Wani alive today.

"Sakuya Kono Hana" (the Naniwazu Poem),
the Basis of Learning to Write

The Naniwazu Poem played an important part in learning to write. Excavations across Japan have turned up many wooden tablets (mokkan) from the 7th century through the Nara period, and among the tablets with poems written on them, a great many carry this poem. In an age when paper was still precious, wooden slips like these are thought to have been used for writing practice.

The photo below shows one of these tablets.

A wooden tablet inscribed with the Naniwazu Poem, excavated at the site of the Fujiwara Capital (Kashihara, Nara Prefecture). It dates from around 694–710, when Fujiwara-kyō was the capital, and is written in ink on a board of Japanese cypress (hinoki) about 39 cm long.

On the front, the poem is written in man'yōgana (Chinese characters used for their sounds): "奈na尓ni皮haツtsu尓ni佐sa久ku矢ya己ko乃no皮ha奈na布fu由yu己go母mo利ri伊i真ma皮ha々ha留ru部be止to."

In the Heian period, too, this was the first poem people learned when practicing writing. The Kana Jo, the preface to the Kokin Wakashū (presented to the emperor in 905) mentioned above, describes the Naniwazu Poem and the Asakayama Poem as "like the father and mother of poetry, the first poems learned by those learning to write."

And in the "Wakamurasaki" chapter of The Tale of Genji, when Hikaru Genji offers to look after the young Murasaki, her grandmother, a nun, turns him down: "She cannot yet even write out the Naniwazu Poem properly, so it is out of the question." This shows that the poem had become firmly established as the basis of learning to write at the time.

A Melody of Hope That Echoes Across the Ages

Today the Naniwazu Poem is known as the prefatory poem of competitive karuta, but its essence may lie not only in symbolizing the beginning of learning to write and to compose waka, but also in the way it has stayed close to people's hearts across the ages. Even though it was composed long ago in the Kofun period, its universal scene and its message full of hope still speak naturally to us today.

Flowers that "kept to their buds through the winter" beginning to bloom as spring arrives is a scene familiar to everyone, and one that makes us feel hope. Spring, coming after a hard winter has been endured, is truly a new beginning and hope itself. Perhaps it is precisely because the poem stays close to people and lets them feel the joy of living that it has been handed down for so long.
