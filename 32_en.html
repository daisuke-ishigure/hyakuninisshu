Poem 32
山川やまがわに 風かぜのかけたる しがらみは
流ながれもあへえぬ 紅葉もみじなりけり
Author: Harumichi no Tsuraki (dates unknown–920)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
The stormy winds of yesterday The maple branches shook; And see! a mass of crimson leaves Has lodged within that nook, And choked the mountain brook.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
The weir of fallen maple leaves, caught by the mountain wind across the stream — the water cannot flow through.
Commentary
The Kokin Wakashū says this was written while crossing the Shiga pass, on the mountain road from Kyoto to Ōtsu. The poet sees maple leaves piled up in a stream and calls them a beautiful weir built by the autumn wind.
About the Poet
Harumichi no Tsuraki was a descendant of the powerful Mononobe clan and studied literature at the court school when young. Named governor of Iki, he fell ill and died on the way. He left no poetry collection and is little known, but Fujiwara no Sadaie liked this poem enough to borrow from it in his own.

Appendix
A Poem in Which Fujiwara no Sadaie Borrowed from Tsuraki

The phrase kaze no kaketaru shigarami, "a weir the wind has built," is thought to have been a very original expression first used by Harumichi no Tsuraki.
This original expression must be why Sadaie was so drawn to his poem that he borrowed from it (honkadori).

In Japanese

木この葉はもて 風かぜのかけたる しがらみに
さてもよどまぬ 秋あきの暮くれかな konohamote kazenokaketaru shigaramini
satemoyodomanu akinokurekana Shūi Gusō (Sadaie's own collection), Fujiwara no Sadaie

In English

The wind has blown the leaves together into something like a weir, yet even so, the end of autumn flows on without stopping.

A Poem About the Flow of a River

The headnote in the Kokin Wakashū says this poem was written at the end of the year, and it is about how quickly the months and days flow by as a year ends. Tsuraki's Hyakunin Isshu poem is also about the flow of a river, and we can almost see him gazing at a river, lost in many thoughts.

In Japanese

昨日きのうといひ けふと暮くらして 飛鳥川あすかがわ
流ながれてはやき 月日つきひなりけり kinoutoihi kefutokurashite asukagawa
nagaretehayaki tsukihinarikeri Kokin Wakashū, Harumichi no Tsuraki

In English

Saying "yesterday," saying "today," we live day after day until tomorrow (asu) comes.
Like the Asuka River, the months and days flow by so fast.

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures. Below the people working on timber, autumn leaves are gathered in the river as if they were forming a weir.
