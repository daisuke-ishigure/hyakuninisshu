Poem 46
由良ゆらのとを 渡わたる舟人ふなびと かぢじを絶たえ
ゆくへも知しらぬ 恋こいの道みちかな
Author: Sone no Yoshitada (dates unknown)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
The fishing-boats are tossed about, When stormy winds blow strong; With rudder lost, how can they reach The port for which they long? So runs the old love-song.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Like a boatman who has lost his oar on the swift straits of Yura — I drift, not knowing where my love is taking me.
Commentary
The poet, lost on the path of love, compares himself to a sailor who has lost his rudder at the fast-flowing mouth of the Yura River.
About the Poet
Sone no Yoshitada used old Man'yōshū words to widen what waka could say, and was among the first to write sets of a hundred poems. His bold style was seen as odd in his day, but later admired: he has the most poems of anyone in the Shika Wakashū. He served in Tango, so the Yura River was familiar to him.

Notes

Sone no Yoshitada was a skilled poet, but because of his low rank he was shut out by the aristocratic society of his day.

  • Because he served as a junior official (jō) of Tango Province, he was called "Sotango" or "Sotan," names said to have been meant as insults
  • Counted as one of the Later Thirty-Six Immortal Poets (Chūko Sanjūrokkasen)

Ono no Komachi's Poem
About a Boat That Has Lost Its Oar
Like Sone no Yoshitada, Ono no Komachi wrote a poem comparing a vague sense of unease to a boat that has lost its oar.

In Japanese

須磨すまの海女あまの 浦うらこぐ舟ふねの 梶かじを絶たえ
よるべなき身みぞ 悲かなしかりける sumanoamano urakogufuneno kajiotae
yorubenakimizo kanashikarikeru Shoku Kokin Wakashū, Ono no Komachi

In English

Like a diver rowing in the bay of Suma who has lost her oar,
how sad I am, with no one to turn to.
A Poem by Saigyō
About Not Knowing Where He Is Headed
Like Sone no Yoshitada, Saigyō wrote about not knowing where he was headed.
The headnote in the Shin Kokin Wakashū says it was written about Mount Fuji while he was traveling east for religious practice. He seems to see in Fuji's smoke, drifting off who knows where, his own wandering journey with no fixed end.

In Japanese

風かぜになびく 富士ふじの煙けぶりの 空そらに消きえて
ゆくへもしらぬ わが思おもひかな kazeninabiku fujinokeburino soranikiete
yukuhemoshiranu wagaomohikana Shin Kokin Wakashū, Saigyō

In English

Just as the smoke of Mount Fuji, blown by the wind, vanishes into the sky,
I do not know where my thoughts will end up.
A Poem Regretting the Passing of Autumn
"Kirigirisu" in old poems means what we now call a cricket (kōrogi).
In the chirping of crickets in late autumn, the poet feels sorry to see the last of autumn go.

In Japanese

鳴なけや鳴なけ 蓬よもぎが杣そまの きりぎりす
過すぎゆく秋あきは げにぞ悲かなしき nakeyanake yomogigasomano kirigirisu
sugiyukuakiha genizokanashiki Go Shūi Wakashū, Sone no Yoshitada

In English

Sing on, sing on, cricket, living in the mugwort as if it were a forest.
The passing of autumn is truly sad.
