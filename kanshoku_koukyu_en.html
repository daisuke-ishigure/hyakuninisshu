The Inner Palace (Kōkyū, 後宮)

The kōkyū was the section of the palace where the emperor's consorts and ladies-in-waiting lived and supported the emperor's daily life. It included the residential halls of the consorts and the administrative offices (the Twelve Offices of the Inner Palace, kōkyū-jūnishi) staffed by female officials.

Imperial Consorts

Title Description
Nyōgo
(女御)		 One of the emperor's consorts; ranked just below the chūgū or kōgō (empress). Usually the daughter of a powerful noble. Often called by the name of her residential hall — for example, Kokiden-no-nyōgo, who appears in The Tale of Genji. A strong candidate to be elevated to empress.
Kōi
(更衣)		 A consort ranked below the nyōgo. Generally from a family of lower standing, but could wield great influence if she enjoyed the emperor's deep affection.
Miyasudokoro
(御息所)		 Originally an honorific for the mother who lived in the residential hall where imperial children were raised. Later, the term came to refer to any woman who had borne the emperor's child.

The term nyōgo first appears in the Nihon Kōki (815), in an official document setting out clothing regulations by rank and status. The term kōi first appears in the Shoku Nihon Kōki (842), in a record noting that a woman raised from no court rank to Junior Fifth Rank had been made a kōi of the retired Emperor Saga. From the mid-Heian period on, women bearing the title kōi grew increasingly rare, and some scholars hold that the rank had already begun to decline by around the reign of Emperor En'yū. The Tale of Genji opens with the line "In a certain reign there was a lady not of the highest rank, among the many nyōgo and kōi in attendance upon the emperor…," but this is not thought to depict the inner palace of Murasaki Shikibu's own time; rather, the tale is understood to be set against the backdrop of an earlier era.

The Nyōbō (Ladies-in-Waiting) of the Inner Palace

The daily lives of the consorts were directly supported by women known as nyōbō (ladies-in-waiting) and nyokan (female officials). The term nyōbō covered not only female officials belonging to the court's government offices, but also women who served in the household organizations of the consorts. Their duties went beyond attending to their mistress's person — they also handled the drafting and relaying of documents, ceremonial duties, clothing, food, and more.

Title Description
Nyōbō
(女房)		 Women who served close to a noble person — such as the emperor or empress — in the palace or an aristocratic household, handling practical tasks, documentation, and daily assistance. The term could also denote a group of women serving a particular master, as when the emperor's nyōbō and the empress's nyōbō each formed their own distinct organization.
Kami no Nyōbō
(上の女房)		 Ladies-in-waiting who served the emperor directly. As opposed to the Miya no Nyōbō, who served the consorts, this term denotes ladies-in-waiting on the emperor's side.
Miya no Nyōbō
(宮の女房)		 Women who served mainly the empress directly. As opposed to the Kami no Nyōbō, who served the emperor directly, this term denotes ladies-in-waiting on the empress's side.
Nyōbō Sanyaku
(女房三役)
Three Key Posts		 In the mid-Heian period, the Senji (宣旨), Mikushigedono-bettō (御匣殿別当), and Naishi (内侍) held the senior roles within the empress's ladies-in-waiting organization, and the nyōbō organization gradually took shape around these posts.
Senji
(宣旨)		 A senior lady-in-waiting who stood at the top of the nyōbō community, relaying the wishes of the emperor or consorts and overseeing the nyōbō organization.
Mikushigedono-bettō
(御匣殿別当)		 The mikushigedono was the place in the inner palace where female officials in charge of clothing and sewing worked. The woman who oversaw this office was called the Mikushigedono-bettō.
Nyokurodō
(女蔵人)		 Lower-ranking ladies-in-waiting who served in the palace under the naishi and myōbu, attending to miscellaneous duties.

Bō (房) means a private chamber. In the palace and in aristocratic households, women were given living quarters partitioned off with standing screens and curtains and lived in close attendance on their mistress or master; such women were called nyōbō.

The titles above are drawn from different criteria — status, affiliation, role, and so on — so they do not all sit within a single, consistent hierarchy. Their meaning and actual usage also varied over time.

Location of the Inner Palace

The inner palace where these women lived spread across the northern part of the dairi (imperial residence). There stood a cluster of residential halls known collectively as the Shichiden-Gosha (Seven Halls and Five Chambers), each of which served as a residence for the consorts and a place where serving women came and went. The "Kokiden," "Reikeiden," "Kiritsubo," and "Fujitsubo" that appear in The Tale of Genji were among these halls.

The Twelve Offices of the Inner Palace (Kōkyū-jūnishi)

The vast inner palace was run by the kōkyū-jūnishi (Twelve Offices of the Inner Palace). Specialist offices covered every aspect of palace life — from the consorts' food, medicine, and clothing to the management of gate keys and cleaning.

Name Function
Naishi no Tsukasa
(内侍司)		 Conveyed reports and petitions to the throne, relayed imperial commands, and oversaw inner palace ceremonial protocol.
Kura no Tsukasa
(蔵司)		 Managed important items including the imperial seal (shinji), gate passes (kankei), and the clothing of the emperor and empress.
Fumi no Tsukasa
(書司)		 Managed books and writing supplies such as paper, ink, and brushes.
Kusuri no Tsukasa
(薬司)		 Managed medicines and oversaw the administration of medicine to the emperor, including poison-tasting.
Tsuwamono no Tsukasa
(兵司)		 Managed ceremonial weapons and armour used in rites and ceremonies (not combat weapons, as the inner palace was a living space).
Mikado no Tsukasa
(闈司)		 Managed the keys to the inner palace gates.
Tonomori no Tsukasa
(殿司)		 Managed palanquins and their canopies, lighting, charcoal, and bathing supplies for palace life.
Kanimori no Tsukasa
(掃司)		 Maintained palace facilities, set up furnishings, and managed cleaning.
Moitori no Tsukasa
(水司)		 Managed the supply of drinking water and light refreshments such as rice porridge.
Kashiwade no Tsukasa
(膳司)		 Managed the preparation, service, and poison-tasting of the emperor's meals.
Miki no Tsukasa
(酒司)		 Managed the brewing of sake and the care of sake vessels.
Nui no Tsukasa
(縫司)		 Managed the sewing of garments and the making of braided cords.

The Naishi no Tsukasa (Bureau of Handmaids)

Among the Twelve Offices of the Inner Palace, it was the Naishi no Tsukasa that grew particularly important during the Heian period. While the other eleven offices gradually became hollow institutions, the Naishi no Tsukasa alone maintained its role of conveying memorials and messages to the emperor, and became the de facto centre of the female official organisation within the inner palace.

The Naishi no Tsukasa was one of the Twelve Offices established by the legal codes (ryō), serving in close attendance on the emperor and handling memorials and communications. Under the ritsuryō system, twelve offices were placed in the inner palace, but from the mid-Heian period onward the rise of the Kurōdo-dokoro (Imperial Secretariat) caused the practical work of the other eleven offices to gradually decline until they became merely nominal. As a result, the Naishi no Tsukasa became the effective centre of the female official organisation in the inner palace.
