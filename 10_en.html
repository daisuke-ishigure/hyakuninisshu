これ

Poem 10
これやこの 行ゆくも帰かえるも わかれては
しるもしらぬも 逢坂おうさかの関せき
Author: Semimaru (dates unknown)
Source: Gosen Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
The stranger who has travelled far, The friend with welcome smile, All sorts of men who come and go Meet at this mountain stile,— They meet and rest awhile.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
So this is it — the Barrier of Ōsaka, where those who leave and those who return, those who know each other and those who do not, all meet and part.
Commentary
The Gosen Wakashū says Semimaru wrote this while living in a hut at the Ōsaka Barrier, watching people come and go. The barrier was a busy crossing between the capital and the eastern provinces. The poem sounds light and rhythmic, but it rests on the Buddhist idea that all who meet must part.
About the Poet
Semimaru is said to have been the fourth son of Emperor Daigo, abandoned on Mount Ōsaka because he was blind. He made a living as a biwa-playing monk, and one story says he was called Semimaru because his singing to the biwa sounded like a cicada (semi).

Appendix
The Biwa (Japanese Lute)

The biwa is an instrument with a flat, oval wooden body and four or five strings, played by plucking the strings with a bachi (plectrum).
It came to Japan from mainland China around the 7th or 8th century.

Travelers on the Tōkaidō Road Near the Ōsaka Barrier

The Ōsaka Barrier was a checkpoint on the border between Yamashiro Province and Ōmi Province (present-day Kyoto and Shiga).
This picture, from the Ise Sangū Meisho Zue (an illustrated guide to famous places on the pilgrimage to Ise), shows people coming and going on the Tōkaidō road near the site of the Ōsaka Barrier.

The Monument at the Site of the Ōsakayama Barrier

In Ōtani-chō, Ōtsu City, Shiga Prefecture, near the top of the pass on National Route 1, stands a monument marking the site of the Ōsakayama Barrier.

Some point out that the monument is not where the barrier actually stood. Even so, the place helps us picture how it once was. Today cars speed over the pass, and almost no one walks there.

But standing in front of the monument, it is fun to imagine the bustle of travelers and the sound of ox carts carrying loads that Semimaru may have heard.

Izumi Shikibu's Poem About the Ōsaka Barrier
Izumi Shikibu also wrote about the Ōsaka Barrier. She was known for her many love affairs, and once Fujiwara no Michinaga, the most powerful man of the time, wrote "the fan of a fickle woman" on her fan. She answered with the next poem. Because "Ōsaka" (逢坂, "the slope of meeting") calls to mind "au" (to meet), it was also used in waka to suggest meetings and love between men and women.

In Japanese

越こえもせむ 越こさずもあらむ 逢坂おうざかの
関守せきもりならぬ 人ひとな咎とがめそ koemosemu kosazumoaramu ouzakano
sekimorinaranu hitonatogameso Izumi Shikibu Shū, Izumi Shikibu

In English

Whether I cross the road of love or not is my own affair. You are not the guard of the Ōsaka Barrier, so do not blame me.

The Ōsaka Barrier in The Tale of Genji

Chapter 16 of The Tale of Genji, "At the Pass" (Sekiya), is set at the Ōsaka Barrier. Hikaru Genji and Utsusemi, who were once close but had to part, unexpectedly pass each other there after twelve years.

The first poem is one Utsusemi composes in her heart.

In Japanese

行いくと来くと せきとめがたき 涙なみだをや
絶たえぬ清水しみずと 人ひとは見みるらむ ikutokuto sekitomegataki namidaoya
taenushimizuto hitohamiruramu The Tale of Genji, Utsusemi (Murasaki Shikibu)

In English

Going and coming back (and never able to meet you), my tears flow and cannot be held back. Will you see them as just the spring water that never stops flowing at the barrier?

Hikaru Genji sends her the next poem.

In Japanese

わくらばに 行いき逢あふう道みちを 頼たのみしも
なほかひなしや 潮しほならぬ海うみ wakurabani ikiaumichio tanomishimo
nahokahinashiya shihonaranuumi The Tale of Genji, Hikaru Genji (Murasaki Shikibu)

In English

Meeting you by chance on the Ōmi road, I hoped it meant we were fated to meet again. But if I cannot see you, it is all in vain (kai nashi), like a sea without salt (Lake Biwa), where there are no shells (kai).

Utsusemi answers Genji with the next poem. "Ōsaka" is a place name that calls to mind "meeting," while a "barrier" stops people from passing. So in waka, the "Ōsaka Barrier" can stand for both "meeting" and "separation" at once. In The Tale of Genji, too, the Ōsaka Barrier, where Semimaru wrote of "those going and those coming back, parting," is a place where distance between people and the wish to meet again cross paths.

In Japanese

逢坂おうさかの 関せきやいかなる 関せきなれば
しげき嘆なげきの 仲なかをわくらむ ousakano sekiyaikanaru sekinareba
shigekinagekino nakaowakuramu The Tale of Genji, Utsusemi (Murasaki Shikibu)

In English

What kind of barrier is the Ōsaka Barrier, that we must push our way through so much grief?

The Genji Screens of "At the Pass" and "Channel Buoys"

This is part of the folding screens of scenes from The Tale of Genji, "At the Pass" and "Channel Buoys" (Genji Monogatari Sekiya Miotsukushi-zu Byōbu), painted by Tawaraya Sōtatsu, an artist of the Edo period. It shows Chapter 16, "At the Pass": Hikaru Genji's party, on its way from Kyoto to Ishiyama-dera Temple to give thanks, happens to pass Utsusemi's party at the Ōsaka Barrier as she returns to Kyoto with her husband, whose term as Vice-Governor of Hitachi has ended. Genji seems to be in the ox carriage on the right of the screen, and Utsusemi in the carriage stopped by the mountainside on the left.

Semimaru Shrine

Semimaru Shrine, in Ōtsu City, Shiga Prefecture, was founded in 946. It stands right by the monument at the site of the Ōsakayama Barrier. It honors two gods: Semimaru, known as a master of the biwa, and Sarutahiko no Mikoto. Semimaru is worshipped as a god of the performing arts, and Sarutahiko as a god who protects travelers on the road.

(Photo taken August 12, 2025)

Seki Shrine, a Branch Shrine of Ōji Shrine

At Seki Shrine, a branch shrine of Ōji Shrine in Kita City, Tokyo, Semimaru is honored as the ancestral god of hair.

According to a story, Semimaru invented the kamoji (a hairpiece) for his elder sister, who was troubled about her hair, so he came to be worshipped as a god of hair.

On the grounds stands the Kezuka, a memorial built in thanks for hair, and the stone fence around it lists the names of many hair-related businesses, such as wig makers and the hairdressers who tie sumo wrestlers' hair.

It is about a 5-minute walk from the North Exit of Ōji Station on the JR Keihin-Tōhoku Line.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This print is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, one of the greatest ukiyo-e artists of the Edo period.
It shows the lively Ōsaka Barrier, with many travelers hurrying past.

Look at the poor thatched hut: Semimaru is peeking out of its small window. With a calm face, he quietly watches people meeting and parting again and again.

The red cord-like sashes the women wear also catch the eye. One theory says such red cords were thought to ward off evil. To people in Edo, too, this kind of dress must have looked old-fashioned, so it may have helped create the feeling of an earlier age.

Tsukioka Yoshitoshi's One Hundred Aspects of the Moon

Tsukioka Yoshitoshi was a brilliant ukiyo-e artist active from the end of the Edo period into the Meiji period. His late masterpiece, One Hundred Aspects of the Moon (Tsuki Hyakushi), shows Semimaru playing the biwa alone and quietly under the moonlight.

The cracked walls and rough pillars with the bark still on them, and his long, untrimmed hair and beard after cutting himself off from the world, show the striking presence of a hermit who lives freely, unafraid of being alone.

He plays for no one, a deep and mysterious sound that seems to answer the moonlight. Only the rustle of the pampas grass in the wind softly joins the music of his biwa, and it all seems to melt into the quiet darkness of the night.
