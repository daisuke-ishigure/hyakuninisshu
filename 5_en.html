Poem 5
奥山おくやまに 紅葉もみじ踏ふみ分わけ 鳴なく鹿しかの
声こえ聞きく時ときぞ 秋あきはかなしき
Author: Sarumaru Dayū (dates unknown)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
Hear the stag's pathetic call Far up the mountain side, While tramping o'er the maple leaves Wind-scattered far and wide This sad, sad autumn tide.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Deep in the mountains, treading through the scattered autumn leaves, I hear a deer cry. Listening to that voice, I feel the sadness of autumn all the more.
Commentary
The Kokin Wakashū says this poem was written for a poetry contest at the home of Prince Koresada, a son of Emperor Kōkō. Autumn is the deer's mating season, when stags call for mates with a high, piercing cry. People of the time heard this sad cry as a sign of deepening autumn and its loneliness. A wildlife photographer has posted a video on YouTube where you can hear a stag's call. It's a rare recording, so please take a listen.
About the Poet
Nothing is recorded about Sarumaru Dayū's birth, death, or life, and nothing is known of his background or work, so he is called a legendary poet.

Appendix
Autumn Leaves and Deer

The October card of hanafuda (Japanese flower cards) shows "autumn leaves and a deer."
This pairing of seasonal images is a classic, and it appears in many waka poems.

A Haiku by Matsuo Bashō
This haiku was written late in life by Matsuo Bashō, a haiku poet of the early Edo period, at Sarusawa Pond in Nara. "Shirigoe" is a cry that trails on long after it ends. The deer's cry echoing through the autumn evening brings the same loneliness as Sarumaru Dayū's poem.

In Japanese

ぴいと啼なく 尻声しりごえ悲かなし 夜よるの鹿しか piitonaku shirigoekanashi yorunoshika Matsuo Bashō

In English

"Pii!" it cries, and its long, trailing call
makes the night feel lonelier still: a deer in the night.

Poems About the Cry of the Deer
The cry of the deer, a sound of autumn, has appeared in many waka since ancient times.
A stag calling for a doe was seen as a sign of deep love between husband and wife.

In Japanese

秋萩あきはぎを しがらみふせて 鳴なく鹿しかの
目めには見めえずて 音おとのさやけさ akihagio shigaramifusete nakushikano
menihameezute otonosayakesa Kokin Wakashū, author unknown

In English

I cannot see the deer crying as it presses through the tangled autumn bush clover,
but its voice comes clearly to me.

In Japanese

山里やまざとは 秋あきこそことに わびしけれ
鹿しかの鳴なく音ねに 目めを覚さましつつ yamazatoha akikosokotoni wabishikere
shikanonakuneni meosamashitsutsu Kokin Wakashū, Mibu no Tadamine

In English

In a mountain village, autumn is the loneliest season of all.
Woken again and again by the cry of the deer, I cannot sleep.

In Japanese

夕ゆうされば 小倉おぐらの山やまに 鳴なく鹿しかは
今夜こよいは鳴なかず 寝いねにけらしも yuusareba oguranoyamani nakushikaha
koyoihanakazu inenikerashimo Man'yōshū, Emperor Okamoto (Emperor Jomei)

In English

The deer that cries on Mount Ogura every evening
is silent tonight. Has it gone to sleep (with its mate)?
Sarumaru Shrine

In Ujitawara Town, Kyoto Prefecture, stands Sarumaru Shrine, which is said to have the power to cure lumps, warts, and other growths.

This may remind you of the folk tale "The Old Man Who Lost His Lump," but no direct link between the two has been found.

The shrine honors Sarumaru Dayū. Instead of the usual guardian lion-dogs, its grounds have a pair of guardian monkeys.

"The Plant Where Deer Cry" (Bush Clover)

Bush clover (hagi) has another name, shika-naku-gusa, "the plant where deer cry."
The name is said to come from its blooming around the time stags cry. No poem in the Hyakunin Isshu mentions bush clover, but it stands out in the Man'yōshū: it appears in as many as 141 poems, more than cherry or plum blossoms, making it the plant that appears most often in the collection.

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

A deer stands at the top of the mountain, and the fifth person from the left, holding a rake in his left hand, is pointing toward it.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period.
It shows a scene that matches the poem in the Hyakunin Isshu.

A single deer is drawn halfway up the mountain, but there is no sadness here: it shows a relaxed, everyday scene.

And yet the gentle, quiet mood of the whole picture fits strangely well with the autumn feeling of Sarumaru Dayū's poem.

Its touch, quietly cherishing the deepening autumn, seems to show us another side of the poem.
