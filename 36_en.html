Poem 36
夏なつの夜よは まだ宵よいながら あけぬるを
雲くものいづこに 月つきやどるらむん
Author: Kiyohara no Fukayabu (dates unknown)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Summer
English Verse Translation
Too short the lovely summer night, Too soon 'tis passed away; I watched to see behind which cloud The moon would chance to stay, And here's the dawn of day!
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
On a summer night, the day has already dawned while it still feels like early evening. Where in the clouds is the moon hiding?
Commentary
The poet is sad to see the moon set on a short summer night. Asking behind which cloud the moon is hiding has a charm almost like a fantasy. It is a lovely poem full of fondness for the moon as it sinks toward the west.
About the Poet
Kiyohara no Fukayabu was a descendant of Emperor Tenmu but never rose high. He was Sei Shōnagon's great-grandfather; she, too, wrote in The Pillow Book that summer nights, especially moonlit ones, are best. He was friends with Fujiwara no Kanesuke, Ki no Tsurayuki, and Ōshikōchi no Mitsune.

The Kiyohara clan includes many descendants of Prince Toneri, a son of Emperor Tenmu. Kiyohara no Fukayabu's grandson was Kiyohara no Motosuke, and Motosuke's daughter was Sei Shōnagon.

Kiyohara no Fukayabu was a friend of Middle Counselor Kanesuke (Fujiwara no Kanesuke), Ki no Tsurayuki, Ōshikōchi no Mitsune, and others. A poem Fujiwara no Kanesuke wrote on hearing the koto played by Fukayabu, a master of the instrument, was chosen for the Sanjūrokuninsen (Selection of Thirty-Six Poets).

Appendix
A Poem That Sees Falling Snow as Flower Petals
The next poem expresses Fukayabu's longing for the spring soon to come as he watches the snow.
Like his Hyakunin Isshu poem, it is full of fantasy.

In Japanese

冬ふゆながら 空そらより花はなの 散ちりくるは
雲くものあなたは 春はるにやあるらむ fuyunagara sorayorihanano chirikuruha
kumonoanataha haruniyaaruramu Kokin Wakashū, Kiyohara no Fukayabu

In English

Though it is still winter, flower petals are falling from the sky.
Could it be spring beyond the clouds?
A Poem of Longing to the Point of Death
In the next poem, the poet has not been able to see the lover who promised to meet again for a long time, and pleads that he is longing for her to the point of death.

In Japanese

今いまははや 恋こひ死しなましを 逢あひ見みむと
頼たのめしことぞ 命いのちなりける imahahaya kohishinamashio ahimimuto
tanomeshikotozo inochinarikeru Kokin Wakashū, Kiyohara no Fukayabu

In English

By now I would have died of longing long ago, but your promise, "Let's meet," which gave me hope, is the only thing keeping me alive.
The Koto

The koto is a stringed instrument that came from China in the Nara period. You press the strings with your left hand and pluck them with your right to make sounds. There are several kinds: the 琴きん (kin) with 7 strings, the 和琴わごん (wagon) with 6 strings, and the 筝そう (sō) with 13 strings.

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures. This picture shows a pleasure boat on the Sumida River. For the people of Edo, spending a summer night on a pleasure boat must have been a refined way to enjoy themselves. Did Hokusai, too, feel on a pleasure boat that good times pass in no time?
