Poem 4
田子たごの浦うらに うちいでてみれば 白妙しろたえの
富士ふじの高嶺たかねに 雪ゆきは降ふりつつ
Author: Yamabe no Akahito (dates unknown)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Winter
English Verse Translation
I started off along the shore, The sea shore at Tago, And saw the white and glist'ning peak Of Fuji all aglow Through falling flakes of snow.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Going out to Tago Bay and looking around, I see snow falling ceaselessly on the high peak of Fuji, white as freshly woven cloth.
Commentary
Tago Bay is the western shore of Suruga Bay. Mountains come right down to the coast there, so Tago Bay is where the whole of Mount Fuji first comes into view. In Japan, snow has long been treasured along with the moon and flowers. The poem holds the poet's awe at snow falling on Fuji's grand peak.
About the Poet
Yamabe no Akahito was a Man'yōshū poet of the Nara period who served Emperor Shōmu. Though a minor official, he had a gift for nature poetry and wrote many poems while traveling with the emperor. Later ages praised him, with Kakinomoto no Hitomaro, as one of the two "Sages of Poetry."

Appendix
The Original Poem
The original of Akahito's poem in the Hyakunin Isshu is a hanka in the Man'yōshū (Book 3, Poem 317). A hanka is a short poem added after a chōka (long poem). A chōka repeats pairs of 5- and 7-syllable lines three or more times and ends with lines of 5, 7, and 7 syllables.
The Original: The Long Poem (Chōka)

In Japanese

天地あまつちの 分わかれし時ときゆ
神かむさびて 高たかく貴たふとき
駿河するがなる 富士ふじの高嶺たかねを
天あまの原はら ふりさけ見みれば
渡わたる日ひの 影かげも隠かくらひ
照てる月つきの 光ひかりも見みえず
白雲しらくもも い行ゆきはばかり
時ときじくそ 雪ゆきは降ふりける
語かたり継つぎ 言いひ継つぎ行ゆかむ
富士ふじの高嶺たかねは amatsuchino wakareshitokiyu
kamusabite takakutafutoki
suruganaru fujinotakaneo
amanohara furisakemireba
wataruhino kagemokakurahi
terutsukino hikarimomiezu
shirakumomo iyukihabakari
tokijikuso yukihafurikeru

In English

Since heaven and earth were parted,
godlike, high, and noble
stands the lofty peak of Fuji in Suruga.
When I look up at it in the wide sky,
the sun crossing the sky hides behind it,
and the shining moon cannot be seen.
Even the white clouds cannot pass,
and snow falls on it in every season.
Let us keep telling of it,
and pass on its story forever,
this lofty peak of Fuji.

The Original: The Short Poem (Hanka)

In Japanese

田子たごの浦うらゆ うち出いでてみれば 真白ましろにそ
富士ふじの高嶺たかねに 雪ゆきは降ふりける tagonourayu uchiidetemireba mashironiso
fujinotakaneni yukihafurikeru Man'yōshū, Yamabe no Akahito

In English

When I came out onto Tago Bay,
I saw pure white snow had fallen on the high peak of Fuji.
A Poem Akahito Wrote When He Visited Iyo Province
Akahito wrote the next poem when he visited a hot spring in Iyo Province. In 661, when Empress Saimei's party stayed at Nikitatsu on its way to Kyushu to help the kingdom of Baekje, Princess Nukata wrote a poem about Nikitatsu (see Emperor Tenji's page). About half a century later, Akahito visited Iyo and, with her poem in mind, is thought to have written this poem thinking back on the courtiers who once sailed from Nikitatsu.

In Japanese

ももしきの 大宮人おおみやびとの 熟田津にきたつに
船乗ふなのりしけむ 年としの知しらなく momoshikino oomiyabitono nikitatsuni
funanorishikemu toshinoshiranaku Man'yōshū, Yamabe no Akahito

In English

The people of the court must have boarded their ships here at Nikitatsu, but no one knows anymore what year it was.
Poems Written on Imperial Journeys (Man'yōshū)
Yamabe no Akahito was a master of poems describing scenery, and he sometimes wrote poems on the emperor's journeys.
He wrote the next poem when Emperor Shōmu visited Kii Province (present-day Wakayama Prefecture). "Waka no Ura" is the name for a group of scenic spots in the southwest of Wakayama City, recognized in 2017 as a Japan Heritage site, "Waka-no-ura, a Treasure Trove of Scenic Beauty."

In Japanese

若わかの浦うらに 潮満しおみち来くれば 潟かたをなみ
葦辺あしべをさして 鶴たづ鳴なき渡わたる wakanourani shiomichikureba kataonami
ashibeosashite tazunakiwataru Man'yōshū, Yamabe no Akahito

In English

When the tide comes in at Waka no Ura, the tidal flats disappear, and the cranes fly crying toward the reeds.

He wrote the next poem when Emperor Shōmu visited Yoshino.

In Japanese

ぬばたまの 夜よの更ふけゆけば
久木ひさぎ生おふる
清きよき川原かわらに
千鳥ちどりしば鳴なく nubatamano yonofukeyukeba hisagiofuru
kiyokikawarani chidorishibanaku Man'yōshū, Yamabe no Akahito

In English

As the night grows late, on the clear riverbank where the hisagi trees grow,
the plovers cry again and again.

A Poem About the Spring Fields
The next poem, about the fields in spring, is in the Man'yōshū.

In Japanese

春はるの野のに すみれ摘つみにと 来こし我われぞ
野のをなつかしみ 一夜ひとよ寝ねにける harunononi sumiretsuminito koshiwarezo
noonatsukashimi hitoyonenikeru Man'yōshū, Yamabe no Akahito

In English

I came to the spring fields to pick violets, but the fields were so lovely that I ended up staying the night.

A Waka About Mount Fuji
The next poem is by Fujiwara no Tadayuki, a poet of the early Heian period. He served as governor of Tōtōmi Province, a post a bit like today's governor of Shizuoka Prefecture. Mount Fuji then was not the calm mountain we know today, but a wild, active volcano sending smoke high into the sky. Standing there as the local governor, Tadayuki must have seen a Fuji far grander and more full of life than the one we see now.

In Japanese

君きみといへば 見みまれ見めずまれ 富士ふじの嶺ねの
珍めづらしげなく 燃もゆるわが恋こひ kimitoiheba mimaremezumare fujinoneno
mezurashigenaku moyuruwagakohi Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Tadayuki

In English

When it comes to you, whether I see you or not, my love keeps burning, always sending up smoke like the peak of Mount Fuji.

Mount Fuji

Seen from the window of a Shinkansen bullet train, Mount Fuji looks calm today, but it is said that it once showed a wild face, sending up smoke that seemed to scorch the sky.

Seeing that endless smoke, people of the time may have seen in it a passion that burned in their own hearts.

Wakamiya Shrine

This shrine in Kanbara, Shimizu Ward, Shizuoka City, honors Yamabe no Akahito. No one knows exactly when it was founded, but it is said to have been built because of his Hyakunin Isshu poem, written at Fukiage Beach near the Kanbara post town (a little west of the Fuji River).

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

Hokusai seems to have drawn the area around Satta Pass, where the mountains come right down to the sea on the way to Tago Bay. The composition, with travelers crossing the pass in front and Mount Fuji far behind, is very appealing.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period.
It shows a scene that matches the poem in the Hyakunin Isshu.

By Kuniyoshi's time in the Edo period, Mount Fuji had already calmed down, looking much like what we see from the Shinkansen today.

It gives us a glimpse of a Mount Fuji loved across the ages.
