Poem 56
あらざらむん この世よのほかの 思おもひい出でに
いまひとたびの 逢おふうこともがな
Author: Izumi Shikibu (c. 976–death year unknown)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
My life is drawing to a close, I cannot longer stay, A pleasant memory of thee I fain would take away; So visit me, I pray.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I may not be long for this world. As a memory to take beyond — let me meet you just one more time.
Commentary
The Go Shūi Wakashū says Izumi Shikibu sent this to someone while she was ill, so it is read as a poem to a lover from her sickbed. But since the men she loved tended to die young, some read it as a last message to a lover who was himself near death.
About the Poet
After having Koshikibu no Naishi with Tachibana no Michisada, Izumi Shikibu became known for her affairs with Prince Tametaka and Prince Atsumichi, sons of Emperor Reizei. Murasaki Shikibu wrote that she was loose in love but a gifted writer whose every word had charm.

Izumi Shikibu's name comes from her first husband, Tachibana no Michisada, who was Governor of Izumi (Izumi no kami), and from her father's post as a secretary of the Ministry of Ceremonial (Shikibu no jō).

Her father was Ōe no Masamune, born into a family of scholars. Her deep learning seems to owe much to his teaching.

With her first husband, Tachibana no Michisada, she had Koshikibu no Naishi, but her daughter died young, in her twenties. Izumi Shikibu also left poems of mourning (aishōka) for her daughter.

Her love with her second husband, Fujiwara no Yasumasa, is the subject of a famous legend that became one of the floats of the Gion Festival. It is introduced further down this page. Please take a look.

A Poem About the Summer Heat
The Heian capital, surrounded by mountains, has merciless summer heat.
Izumi Shikibu wrote lightly about the heat, using the cicada's song and the cicada's wings as images.

In Japanese

In English

When I hear the cicadas sing, I feel even hotter,

A Poem Mourning Prince Tametaka
In this poem Izumi Shikibu grieves for Prince Tametaka after his death.
It expresses how crushed she felt by the sound of the night wind and the loneliness of no longer having his warmth beside her.

In Japanese

In English

The sound of the wind that blows right through me as I lie awake: in the old days (when I was with Prince Tametaka), I must have heard it as something that had nothing to do with me.
A Poem Mourning Prince Atsumichi
In this poem Izumi Shikibu grieves for Prince Atsumichi after his death. The yobukodori ("calling bird") is the cuckoo.

In English

Cry, cry on, calling bird, together with my weeping,

A Poem Seeing Fireflies as Her Soul
Izumi Shikibu wrote this poem when, troubled by her relationship with her husband, she visited Kifune Shrine at Kurama in Kyoto and saw fireflies flying over the Mitarashi Stream. She seems to have been so worn out that she felt her soul had left her body, but afterwards she and her husband got back together. Because of this, Kifune Shrine is said to have become known as a shrine for bringing couples together.

In English

A Poem Seeking Salvation
Izumi Shikibu lived freely for love, but at the price of much suffering. She also wrote poems seeking salvation.

A Poem Comparing Love to a Color
Izumi Shikibu loved many times and went through painful partings.
Her way of comparing love to a color, lightly and simply, shows her special sensitivity.

The Izumi Shikibu Diary
A diary written by Izumi Shikibu. She is deep in grief over the sudden death of her lover, Prince Tametaka (the third son of Emperor Reizei), and in despair after her father has cut her off because of her love with a man so far above her. The story begins when a branch of tachibana (mandarin orange) flowers arrives for her from Prince Atsumichi (the Sochi no Miya). It tells, from a third-person point of view and in the style of a poem-tale, how the two exchange poems and grow closer.
A Love Legend That Became a Gion Festival Float

When Fujiwara no Yasumasa asked Izumi Shikibu to marry him, she tested how serious he was by asking him to bring her a branch of the plum tree at the Shishinden, the palace's main hall. Yasumasa slipped into the Shishinden at night and, at the risk of being shot by the guards, managed to steal a branch of plum blossoms, and Izumi Shikibu accepted his proposal.

In honor of this legend, a float called "Hōshōyama" parades in the Gion Festival. If you look at the figure of Yasumasa on the float, you can see that he holds a fine branch of plum blossoms. The float's charms are said to be popular with people hoping for a good match.

Seishin-in, a Temple Connected with Izumi Shikibu

A temple connected with Izumi Shikibu, inside the arcade of the Shinkyōgoku shopping street in Nakagyō Ward, Kyoto. According to the temple, it began in 1027 when Kampaku Fujiwara no Michinaga gave Izumi Shikibu, who served his daughter Jōtōmon-in, a small hermitage inside the Tōhoku-in of Hōjō-ji. It was first in another place, but after several moves it was moved to its present site in Shinkyōgoku on the orders of Toyotomi Hideyoshi. The grounds have a stone pagoda (hōkyōintō) said to be Izumi Shikibu's grave and a stone carved with her poem, and the hall holds the main image, Amida Buddha, along with statues of Izumi Shikibu and Fujiwara no Michinaga. In the middle of a busy shopping district, the history of a Heian poet lives on quietly.

Izumi Shikibu Park

A park in Shiota-chō, Ureshino City, Saga Prefecture. It stands on a small hill overlooking the Ariake Sea. Because Izumi Shikibu is said to have been born here and lived here until she was nine, a 5-meter bronze statue of her and a stone carved with her poem stand in the park. It also has an artificial-grass slope for grass skiing, an "athletic forest," and other facilities.
