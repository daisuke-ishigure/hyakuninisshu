Poem 63
いまはただ 思おもひ絶たえなむん とばかりを
人ひとづてならで 言いふうよしもがな
Author: Master of the Left Capital Michimasa (992–1054)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
If we could meet in privacy, Where no one else could see, Softly I'd whisper in thy ear This little word from me— "I'm dying, Love, for thee."
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
If only I could say it directly: all I want is to end this love. But there is no way to tell you in person.
Commentary
Michimasa loved Princess Masako, who had served as the Ise priestess, but her father, Retired Emperor Sanjō, had her closely guarded and forbade him to see her. He put his sorrow at being unable to meet her into this poem.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Michimasa was the eldest son of Fujiwara no Korechika. After his father shot arrows at a retired emperor in the Chōtoku Incident, the family declined. Michimasa held the third rank but had no real power, and grew reckless. His bad behavior earned him the nickname "the wild third rank," and his name was even linked to a murder.

Michimasa's Family Tree

When Michimasa's father, Fujiwara no Korechika, was sent away from the capital after the Chōtoku Incident, the Naka-no-Kanpaku family, which had been at the height of its power, quickly fell. Fujiwara no Michimasa rose to Middle Captain of the Left Palace Guards at 25 and was given Junior Third Rank, but the next year his secret affair with Princess Masako was discovered. Emperor Sanjō punished him, and the lovers were torn apart. His Hyakunin Isshu poem was written at that time. The poem seems to accept the fate he brought on himself, but his life afterward was so wild that he was called "the wild third rank" (Ara-sanmi) and "the wicked third rank" (Aku-sanmi); in his despair he sometimes turned to violence.

Appendix
Poems of Tears for a Love He Could Not Meet
Fujiwara no Michimasa was a man suspected of rough words and deeds, brawls, and even of being behind a murder, and he was called "the wicked third rank."
Yet when his love with Princess Masako was torn apart, even he wrote poems of tragic love full of feeling.

In Japanese

逢坂あふさかは あづま路ぢとこそ 聞ききしかど
心こころづくしの 関せきにぞありける afusakaha azumajitokoso kikishikado
kokorozukushino sekinizoarikeru Go Shūi Wakashū, Fujiwara no Michimasa

In English

I had heard that the Ōsaka Barrier was on the road to the eastern lands, but in fact it was a barrier that wears out the heart (kokorozukushi), like a barrier in faraway Tsukushi.

"Kokorozukushi" also contains "Tsukushi," the old name of Kyūshū (today's Fukuoka Prefecture).

In Japanese

榊葉さかきばの 木綿四手ゆふしでかけし
そのかみに
押おし返かえしても
似にたる頃ころかな sakakibano yufushidekakeshi sonokamini
oshikaeshitemo nitarukorokana Go Shūi Wakashū, Fujiwara no Michimasa

In English

When you were the Ise priestess, you served the gods, hanging yūshide on sakaki branches, and no one was allowed to touch you. These days, when nothing goes as I wish, it is as if we have been pushed back to that time.

Yūshide are shide made of yū (mulberry-bark fiber). Shide are the strips of paper hung on a tamagushi (sacred branch).

In Japanese

みちのくの 緒絶をだえの橋はしや これならむ
ふみみふまずみ 心まどはす michinokuno odaenohashiya korenaramu
fumimifumazumi kokoromadohasu Go Shūi Wakashū, Fujiwara no Michimasa

In English

So this is what they mean by the "Odae Bridge" (the bridge where the cord breaks) in Michinoku. Now that I no longer see your letters (fumi), I am thrown into confusion, afraid our bond will break.

The "Odae Bridge" is Odae-bashi in Ōsaki City, Miyagi Prefecture. A tragic legend says that Princess Odae, loved by Emperor Saga, was driven from the capital and threw herself from this bridge.

In Japanese

涙なみだやは またも逢あふべき つまならん
泣なくよりほかの なくさめぞなき namidayaha matamoafubeki tsumanaran
nakuyorihokano nakusamezonaki Go Shūi Wakashū, Fujiwara no Michimasa

In English

Will shedding tears give me a chance to meet her again? Of course not. Yet I have no comfort but to weep.

"Tsuma," written with the character for "edge," means a chance or a clue.

His Father Korechika's Last Words
His father, Korechika, who had brought about the fall of the Naka-no-Kanpaku family, told Michimasa in his will: "Rather than live by flattering others, become a monk."
Late in life, in 1054, Michimasa became a monk, just as his father had told him.
