Poem 64
朝あさぼらけ 宇治うじの川霧かわぎり 絶たえ絶だえに
あらはわれわたる 瀬々せぜの網代木あじろぎ
Author: Acting Middle Counselor Sadayori (995–1045)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Winter
English Verse Translation
So thickly lies the morning mist, That I can scarcely see The fish-nets on the river bank, The River of Uji, Past daybreak though it be.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
In the early dawn mist over the Uji River, here and there the wooden stakes of fish weirs begin to appear through the haze.
Commentary
The Senzai Wakashū says this was written on a visit to Uji. Light shines into the mist rising from the cold river, and through gaps in the mist the stakes of fish traps (ajirogi) appear, a typical winter sight on the Uji River. Ajirogi held bamboo traps for catching young sweetfish.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Sadayori was the son of Fujiwara no Kintō. Like his father he was skilled at poetry and music, and he was also a fine calligrapher. But the famous story of being outwitted by Koshikibu no Naishi's poem shows he could be careless.

Appendix
Kakinomoto no Hitomaro's Poem About the Fish-Trap Stakes of Uji
This poem is said to have been written by Kakinomoto no Hitomaro on the bank of the Uji River on his way from the ruined province of Ōmi to the Fujiwara capital.
"Mononofu no" is a pillow word (makurakotoba) that leads into "yaso" (many). "Uji" is a pun (kakekotoba) on uji (clans) and the place name Uji.
Having seen the abandoned capital of Ōtsu with his own eyes, Hitomaro is thought to have expressed the impermanence (mujōkan) of a changing world.

In English

Like the waves lingering at the fish-trap stakes of the Uji River, no one knows where all the many officials serving the court will end up.
The Uji River

A river that flows from Uji City, Kyoto Prefecture, out into the Kyoto Basin.

Upstream, the Uji River runs through a deep gorge, and the river mist that forms there flows down the valley to Uji. So river mist often rises at Uji, and the scene of "Asaborake Uji no kawagiri" (the river mist of Uji at dawn) has been a much-loved view of Uji from the Heian period to the present day.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e print is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period.
It shows a scene to go with the Hyakunin Isshu poem.

In those days, every winter, fish traps (ajiro) were set up at the shallows of the Uji River to catch hio (young sweetfish). As the morning mist cleared, the stakes driven into the river appeared one after another, a typical winter scene on the Uji River.

Ajiro fishing has disappeared today, but in summer cormorant fishing (ukai) is held on the Uji River, and you can still see a traditional style of fishing. Cormorant fishing was already done in the Heian period, and it is recorded in the Kagerō Diary.
