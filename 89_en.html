Poem 89
玉たまの緒およ 絶たえなば絶たえね ながらへえば
忍しのぶることの 弱よわりもぞする
Author: Princess Shikishi (1149–1201)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
The ailments of advancing years Though I should try to hide, Some day the thread will break, the pearls Be scattered far and wide; Age cannot be defied.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Let my life end now, if it must. For if I go on living, my power to endure this secret love may weaken.
Commentary
Written on the theme "hidden love." As a Kamo priestess serving the gods, Princess Shikishi could not love openly, so her own life makes the theme of hidden love deeply convincing.
About the Poet
Princess Shikishi was the third daughter of Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa. She served as Kamo priestess from her teens to her early twenties and was never free to love. She lost family one after another and was even suspected of cursing someone. Yet she wrote many beautiful poems, and has 49 in the Shin Kokin Wakashū, the most of any woman.

Princess Shikishi was the third daughter of Emperor Go-Shirakawa and Fujiwara no Shigeko. In 1159 she was appointed Kamo priestess (saiin), and for ten years, until she left the post because of illness in 1169, she lived a life of service to the gods.

After that, in 1171, her younger sister Princess Yasuko died at only 15, and in 1177 her mother Shigeko died. Then, in 1179, Taira no Kiyomori confined her father, Emperor Go-Shirakawa, and his rule as retired emperor was stopped. In response, her younger brother Prince Mochihito raised an army in 1180, but he was hit by a stray arrow in the fighting against the Taira and was killed.

Princess Shikishi also lived in the household of her aunt, Princess Akiko (Hachijō-in). But when someone put a curse on Princess Akiko, Shikishi was treated as the culprit. This led her to become a nun.

Despite her troubled life, Princess Shikishi studied waka under Fujiwara no Toshinari and showed great talent. In the Shin Kokin Wakashū, 49 of her poems were chosen, the most of any woman poet.

Appendix
A Poem from Her Years as Kamo Priestess
The next poem was written early in the morning when, as Kamo priestess, she took part in the Aoi Festival (the Kamo Festival). On the day before the ceremony, the priestess stayed in a temporary hut called the kandachi to purify herself. The poem sings of the pure morning dew on the fields at daybreak.

In Japanese

忘わすれめや 葵あおいを草くさに ひき結むすび
仮寝かりねの野のべの 露つゆのあけぼの wasuremeya aoiokusani hikimusubi
karinenonobeno tsuyunoakebono Shin Kokin Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

How could I forget the sparkle of the dew on the fields at dawn, after a night spent sleeping with aoi leaves tied into my pillow?
Aoi Leaves

At the Aoi Festival (the Kamo Festival), a decoration called aoi-katsura is used, made by twining small branches of katsura (a symbol of purity) with leaves of futaba aoi (wild ginger).

At the festival, you can see the people taking part wearing this decoration in their hair, and ox carriages decorated with it.

Poems of the Four Seasons
Here are some of Princess Shikishi's poems describing scenery.
They capture the beautiful scenes of each season in delicate words.

A Poem About the Coming of Spring

In Japanese

山やま深ふかみ 春はるとも知しらぬ 松まつの戸とに
絶たえ絶だえかかる 雪ゆきの玉水たまみず yamafukami harutomoshiranu matsunotoni
taedaekakaru yukinotamamizu Shin Kokin Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

A mountain home so deep in the hills that it does not even know spring has come. On its door beneath the pines, drops of melting snow fall now and then, beautiful as jewels.

A Poem About the Feeling of Spring

The poem describes the long-awaited blooming of the cherry blossoms and the feeling of spring in the air.

In Japanese

いま桜さくら 咲さきぬとみえて うす曇ぐもり
春はるにかすめる 世よのけしきかな imasakura sakinutomiete usugumori
harunikasumeru yonokeshikikana Shin Kokin Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

Now the cherry trees seem to have bloomed. Thin clouds cover the sky,
and the whole world is wrapped in the haze of spring.

A Poem About a Summer Evening

The poem describes a fresh summer scene after an evening shower.

In Japanese

夕立ゆうだちの 雲くももとまらぬ 夏なつの日ひの
かたぶく山やまに ひぐらしの声こえ yuudachino kumomotomaranu natsunohino
katabukuyamani higurashinokoe Shin Kokin Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

On a summer day, after the clouds that brought the evening shower have passed,
the cry of the higurashi cicada is heard on the mountain where the sun is setting.

A Poem About the End of Summer

It borrows (honkadori) from a poem in The Tales of Ise: "ゆく蛍ほたる 雲くものうへまで 往いぬべくは 秋風吹あきかぜふくと 雁かりに告つげこせ" (Fireflies flying off, if you can go as high as the clouds, tell the wild geese that the autumn wind is blowing).

In Japanese

秋風あきかぜと 雁かりにやつぐる 夕暮ゆうぐれの
雲くも近ちかきまで ゆく蛍ほたるかな akikazeto kariniyatsuguru yuugureno
kumochikakimade yukuhotarukana Fūga Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

Are you going to tell the wild geese that the autumn wind is blowing?
Fireflies, flying up so high in the evening that you almost reach the clouds.

A Poem About the Autumn Moon

In Japanese

風かぜさむみ 木この葉は晴はれゆく 夜よな夜よなに
残のこるくまなき 庭にわの月影つきかげ kazesamumi konohahareyuku yonayonani
nokorukumanaki niwanotsukikage Shin Kokin Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

Night after night the wind is cold and the leaves fall from the trees,
and the moonlight shines on every corner of the garden.

A Poem About the First Snow of Winter

In Japanese

さむしろの 夜半よわの衣手ころもで さえさえて
初雪はつゆき白しろし岡おかの辺べの松まつ samushirono yowanokoromode saesaete
hatsuyukishiroshiokanobenomatsu Shin Kokin Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

Late at night, on a poor straw mat, even the sleeves of my robe are chilled through. Looking out, I see the pines on the hill white with the first snow.
Poems of Hidden Love

In The Tales of Ise there is a poem: "五月待さつきまつ 花橘はなたちばなの 香かをかげば 昔むかしの人ひとの 袖そでの香かぞする" (When I smell the tachibana flowers that wait for the fifth month, I smell the sleeves of someone I once loved). Because of this poem, the scent of tachibana (a kind of citrus) flowers came to be thought of as something that brings back the past. The next poem probably expresses Princess Shikishi's wish that, even if she longed for an old love, she would keep it only within her dreams.

In Japanese

かへり来こぬ 昔むかしを今いまと おもひ寝ねの
夢ゆめの枕まくらに 匂にほふたちばな kaherikonu mukashioimato omohineno
yumenomakurani nihofutachibana Shin Kokin Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

I fell asleep wishing that the past, which will never return, could come back once more, and dreamed. When I woke, the scent of tachibana flowers hung by my pillow.

The next poem tries to keep a love hidden even after death.

In Japanese

君きみゆゑと いふ名なはたてじ 消きえはてむ
夜半よわの煙けむりの すゑまでも見みよ kimiyueto ifunahatateji kiehatemu
yowanokemurino suemademomiyo Shoku Gosen Wakashū, Princess Shikishi

In English

I will not let a rumor spread that I died because of my love for you. I will vanish as smoke, but please watch my smoke rising in the night until the very end.
