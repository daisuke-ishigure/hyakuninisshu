Poem 96
花はなさそふう 嵐あらしの庭にわの 雪ゆきならで
ふりゆくものは わが身みなりけり
Author: Lay Novice and former Chancellor of the Realm (1171–1244)
Source: Shin Chokusen Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
This snow is not from blossoms white Wind-scattered, here and there, That whiten all my garden paths And leave the branches bare; 'Tis age that snows my hair!
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
It is not the snow of blossoms scattered by the storm in the garden — it is I myself who am fading with the years.
Commentary
Written on the theme "falling blossoms." Watching cherry blossoms fall like snow, the poet seems to reflect that even a man at the height of glory, who has gained everything, cannot stop growing old.
About the Poet
Saionji Kintsune married a niece of Minamoto no Yoritomo and built close ties with the Kamakura shogunate. In the Jōkyū War, though on the court's side, he secretly told the shogunate of Go-Toba's plans, and was later rewarded with the post of Chancellor. He built the splendid Saion-ji temple in Kitayama, Kyoto.

Saionji Kintsune married the daughter of Ichijō Yoshiyasu, whose wife Bōmon-hime was Minamoto no Yoritomo's elder or younger sister by the same mother, and so built close ties with the Kamakura shogunate. But he was disliked by Retired Emperor Go-Toba, who aimed to overthrow the shogunate, and because he supported the shogunate's side in the Jōkyū War, he was confined. When the shogunate won and Emperor Go-Horikawa came to the throne, Kintsune was appointed Palace Minister, and in the end he rose to Chancellor of the Realm. Kintsune's daughter Rinshi married Kampaku Fujiwara no Michiie, and their son Fujiwara no Yoritsune later became the fourth shogun of the Kamakura shogunate.

Appendix
Saion-ji Temple

Kintsune rebuilt his mountain villa in Kitayama, Kyoto, and founded the temple Saion-ji.
He changed his family name from Fujiwara to Saionji. The family name comes from this temple, which Kintsune built and which earned him the name Saionji-dono. See it on the map

Kinkaku-ji Temple

The site of Kinkaku-ji (Rokuon-ji) was originally Kitayama-tei, a mountain villa built by Saionji Kintsune. Ashikaga Yoshimitsu took it over and built Kitayama-dono, and after Yoshimitsu's death it became a Zen temple, Rokuon-ji. Today it is widely known as Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Pavilion.
