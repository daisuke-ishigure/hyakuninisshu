Poem 35
人ひとはいさ 心こころも知しらず ふるさとは
花はなぞ昔むかしの 香かに匂におひいける
Author: Ki no Tsurayuki (c. 868–945)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Spring
English Verse Translation
The village of my youth is gone, New faces meet my gaze; But still the blossoms at thy gate, Whose perfume scents the ways, Recall my childhood's days.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I cannot know what is in your heart. But here in the old village, the plum blossoms still carry the same fragrance as before.
Commentary
The Kokin Wakashū says Ki no Tsurayuki returned after a long absence to the inn where he used to stay on visits to Hase. The innkeeper teased him: "The inn has been here all along." He picked a plum blossom nearby and wrote this poem in reply. Whether the innkeeper was a man or a woman is unknown.
About the Poet
Ki no Tsurayuki wrote the Japanese preface to the Kokin Wakashū and the Tosa Diary, an account of his trip home after serving as a provincial governor. Written in kana as if by a woman, the diary greatly influenced later women writers.

Ki no Tsurayuki is said to have come from the Ki clan, an ancient powerful family whose ancestor was Takeuchi no Sukune, a legendary statesman. In the Heian period, as the Fujiwara grew stronger, the political power of the Ki clan gradually weakened. So Tsurayuki's rank was never high.

But Tsurayuki excelled at waka, and at the order of Emperor Daigo he served as a compiler of the Kokin Wakashū together with his cousin Ki no Tomonori, Mibu no Tadamine, and Ōshikōchi no Mitsune. Tsurayuki also wrote the Kana Preface, which explains that waka expresses the human heart. Ki no Tomonori is thought to have died before the Kokin Wakashū was completed.

Tsurayuki also served in a series of provincial posts. After serving as Governor of Tosa, he wrote The Tosa Diary, an account in kana of his boat journey back to Kyoto, pretending to be a woman. The Tosa Diary is said to have had a great influence on later Japanese literature.

His final rank, Junior Fifth Rank, Upper Grade, was not high at all, but he contributed greatly to the growth of waka and kana culture.

Ki no Tsurayuki is born

Born the son of Ki no Mochiyuki.

Becomes a compiler of the Kokin Wakashū

At Emperor Daigo's order, he compiled and presented the Kokin Wakashū, Japan's first imperial anthology of waka, together with Ki no Tomonori, Mibu no Tadamine, and Ōshikōchi no Mitsune. Tsurayuki took the lead among them. He wrote the Kana Preface to the Kokin Wakashū and is thought to have been a driving force behind the waka boom.

Given Junior Fifth Rank, Lower Grade

He was given Junior Fifth Rank, Lower Grade, and appointed Assistant Governor of Kaga.

Becomes Governor of Tosa
and compiles the Shinsen Waka

Just before leaving for the province of Tosa, he was ordered by Emperor Daigo to compile the Shinsen Waka, a selection of poems.
He finished it while in Tosa, but by the time he returned to the capital, Emperor Daigo had died, so he could not present it.

Writes The Tosa Diary

He returned to the capital from Tosa and wrote The Tosa Diary about the 50-day journey home, presented as if a woman had written it in hiragana.

Ki no Tsurayuki dies

His highest rank and post were Junior Fifth Rank, Upper Grade, and Acting Head of the Carpentry Bureau.

The Innkeeper's Reply
The master of the house answered Ki no Tsurayuki's Hyakunin Isshu poem with this reply.

In Japanese

花はなだにも 同おなじ心こころに 咲さくものを
植うゑけむ人ひとの 心こころ知しらなむ Kokin Wakashū, author unknown

In English

Even the plum blossoms bloom with the same heart as before,
so please understand the heart of the one who planted them, too.

A Poem Blaming a Man for His Change of Heart
The next poem is in Ki no Tsurayuki's personal collection. It is said to have been written by a woman blaming Tsurayuki for his change of heart. People have long known that bush clover changes color from its lower leaves first, and the poem compares that "changing color" to a man whose feelings have changed.

In Japanese

秋萩あきはぎの 下葉したばにつけて 目めに近ちかく
よそなる人ひとの 心こころをぞ見みる Tsurayuki-shū, Ki no Tsurayuki

In English

Each time I see the lower leaves of the autumn bush clover changing color, I realize how your heart, though you are right before my eyes, has grown distant and changed completely.

Tsurayuki's Poem Reworked in The Tale of Genji
The next poem borrows from Ki no Tsurayuki's poem above, "Akihagi no shitaba ni tsukete...," and appears in "Wakana I," the 34th chapter of The Tale of Genji. Lady Murasaki, deeply loved by Hikaru Genji for many years, wrote it in her sorrow when Genji told her he would take the much younger Third Princess as his principal wife.

In Japanese

目めに近ちかく 移うつれば変かはる 世よの中なかを
行ゆく末すえ遠とおく 頼たのみけるかな The Tale of Genji, Lady Murasaki

In English

I have watched love between men and women change before my eyes, and yet I trusted that you would stay with me far into the future.

A Poem That Appeared in Episode 23 of Hikaru Kimi e
It is recited in a scene where Emperor Ichijō talks about his memories of Empress Teishi. The poem is in the Kokin Wakashū.

In Japanese

夢路ゆめぢにも 露つゆやおくらむ 夜よもすがら
かよへる袖そでの ひちて乾かわかぬ Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tsurayuki

In English

Does dew fall even on the path of dreams that leads to you?
My sleeves, which travel it all night, are wet and never dry.

A Poem About Falling Cherry Blossoms
The next poem sees falling cherry petals as snow.

In Japanese

桜さくら散ちる 木この下風したかぜは 寒さむからで

A Poem About the Fickle Human Heart
This poem by Ki no Tsurayuki is in the Kokin Wakashū.
Its headnote says he wrote it when someone said, "Nothing falls as quickly as cherry blossoms."

In Japanese

桜花さくらばな とくちりぬとも 思おもほえず
人ひとの心こころぞ 風かぜも吹ふきあへぬ Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tsurayuki

In English

I don't think cherry blossoms fall so quickly.
The human heart changes before the wind has even finished blowing.

His Farewell Poem
The next poem is Ki no Tsurayuki's death poem, found in his personal collection, Tsurayuki-shū.
Knowing he was dying of illness, he is said to have sent it to his friend Minamoto no Kintada.

In Japanese

手てに結むすぶ 水みずに宿やどれる 月影つきかげの
有あるか無なきかの 世よにこそありけれ Tsurayuki-shū, Ki no Tsurayuki

In English

Like the moon reflected in water scooped up in my hands, this world is so fleeting that one can hardly tell if it is there or not.

A Poem by Ki no Tsurayuki's Daughter
The story "Ōshukubai" ("The Plum Tree Where the Warbler Lodges") in the Ōkagami includes a poem by Ki no Tsurayuki's daughter.
When the plum tree planted in front of the Shishinden hall died, Emperor Murakami was very sorry and ordered that a plum tree just as beautiful be found somewhere in the capital. After a while, such a tree was finally found in the capital, and it was to be planted at the Shishinden. The tree had stood at the house of Ki no Tsurayuki, the famous poet of earlier days, and a poem was tied to one of its branches. The poem was by Tsurayuki's daughter, Ki no Naishi. Emperor Murakami was moved by the poem and ordered the tree returned.

In Japanese

勅ちょくなれば いともかしこし
鶯うぐひすの
宿やどはと問とわば
いかが答こたへむ Ōkagami, Ki no Naishi

In English

Since it is the emperor's order, I humbly offer this tree. But if the warbler that comes to the plum tree asks, "Where is my lodging?", how should I answer?

The Kana Preface (Kanajo)
The preface to the Kokin Wakashū written in kana (the Japanese syllabary), as opposed to the preface written in Chinese.
Changing Tastes in Flowers
Until the Nara period, plum blossoms were the most popular, but from the Heian period on, cherry blossoms became more popular.
