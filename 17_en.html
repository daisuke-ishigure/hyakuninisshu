ちは

Poem 17
ちはやぶる 神代かみよもきかず 竜田川たつたがわ
から紅くれないに 水みずくくるとは
Author: Ariwara no Narihira Ason (825–880)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
All red with leaves Tatsuta's stream So softly purls along, The everlasting Gods themselves, Who judge 'twixt right and wrong, Ne'er heard so sweet a song.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Even the ancient gods never heard of this — the Tatsuta River, dyeing its waters a deep crimson with fallen maple leaves.
Commentary
The Kokin Wakashū says this was written about a painted screen showing autumn leaves flowing down the Tatsuta River, at the invitation of Fujiwara no Takaiko. She and Narihira had once been lovers, but she had become a consort of Emperor Seiwa, far above him. He is thought to have hidden in this poem the love he could no longer speak.
About the Poet
Ariwara no Narihira was a son of Prince Abo (a son of Emperor Heizei) but became a commoner. Free-spirited and a gifted poet, he is believed to be the hero of The Tales of Ise.

Ariwara no Narihira was born the fifth son of Prince Abo, a son of Emperor Heizei. His mother was Princess Ito, a daughter of Emperor Kanmu, so both his father and his mother came from the imperial family.

Prince Abo, however, was caught up in the Kusuko Incident and sent away to Dazaifu in Kyushu as its acting governor-general, which took him far from the throne. Later he asked the court to let his children leave the imperial family and become subjects. In 826, Narihira, together with his elder half-brother Yukihira and others, left the imperial family and was given the family name "Ariwara no Ason."

The Tales of Ise describes a deep love between Narihira and Fujiwara no Takaiko, who was later called the "Nijō Empress." When it was decided that Takaiko would enter the palace as a consort of Emperor Seiwa, the two tried to run away together. But on the way, Takaiko's elder brothers caught up with them and took her back.
After that, women who remind readers of Takaiko appear again and again in The Tales of Ise, in stories of love and parting with Narihira.

Appendix
Tatsuta-age
Tatsuta-age is Japanese fried chicken marinated in soy sauce, which turns its surface red when fried. Its name is said to come from this poem, which describes autumn leaves floating on the Tatsuta River.
Prince Abo
The first son of Emperor Heizei, and the father of Ariwara no Yukihira and Ariwara no Narihira.
He is also said to be the real grandfather of Ōe no Chisato.
Why Narihira Left the Imperial Family
After stepping down, Retired Emperor Heizei, urged on by Fujiwara no Kusuko, tried to take back power and clashed with his younger brother, Emperor Saga. Because of this "Retired Emperor Heizei Incident" (also called the "Kusuko Incident"), Heizei's first son, Prince Abo, was also punished and sent away to Dazaifu as its acting governor-general. Later, Prince Abo asked the emperor to let his sons leave the imperial family and become subjects, and Yukihira and Narihira were given the family name "Ariwara."
The Six Poetic Sages (Rokkasen)

When the Kokin Wakashū was compiled, these six poets, including Ariwara no Narihira, were very famous poets of the generation before. In the Japanese preface to the Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tsurayuki picked them out as "people whose names were known in recent times" and judged their poetry. His judgments were quite harsh, but the six later came to be called the Rokkasen, the Six Poetic Sages, and were highly praised as great poets.

See Ki no Tsurayuki's judgments of the six poets.

One of the Six Poetic Sages, Ōtomo no Kuronushi, has no poem in the Hyakunin Isshu, while the other five were all chosen.

Narihira, the Hero of The Tales of Ise
The Tales of Ise is a collection of poem-tales, mostly about love, made in the early Heian period. Each story begins "Long ago, there was a man." Because of this opening line, the hero is called Mukashi-otoko, "the man of long ago." Of the roughly 200 poems he writes in the work, as many as 34 are known to be by Ariwara no Narihira, so Narihira is thought to be the model for the hero.

A Poem from "The Journey East" in The Tales of Ise
The next poem appears in "The Journey East" (Azuma Kudari) in The Tales of Ise. On a journey to the eastern provinces, at the Sumida River between the provinces of Musashi and Shimōsa, the hero learns that the birds he sees are called miyakodori, "birds of the capital," and writes this poem thinking of the woman he loves, whom he left in the capital.

In Japanese

名なにし負おはば いざ言こと問とはむ 都鳥みやこどり
わが思おもふ人ひとは ありやなしやと

The Tales of Ise, Ariwara no Narihira

In English

Bird of the capital,
if you are true to your name, tell me:
is the one I love in the capital
still well, or not?

The miyakodori is the black-headed gull. Today it is known as the official bird of Tokyo.

Kototoi Dango and Kototoi Monaka

Kototoi Dango, named after Narihira's poem "Na ni shi owaba iza koto towamu miyakodori...," was created at the end of the Edo period.

At Mukōjima Kototoi Dango, a long-established shop in Mukōjima on the banks of the Sumida River, these dumplings, served without skewers, have been made in the same place since the shop opened.

The round dumplings come in three colors, white, yellow, and black. Because they are not put together on one skewer, you can enjoy the flavor of each one. Kototoi Monaka, a wafer sweet shaped like a miyakodori, is also recommended: it looks lovely and has the same gentle sweetness as the dumplings.

Both are carefully made from selected Japanese ingredients and have been loved by many people across the ages.

In the shop, you can take your time and enjoy these sweets with fragrant tea.

March 20, 2025

Kototoi Bridge

Kototoi Bridge crosses the Sumida River. Its name is said to come from Narihira's poem. As I looked out over the river from the bridge, black-headed gulls were flying gracefully.

The story goes that when Narihira first saw these birds, he did not know their name and asked the ferryman. The ferryman told him they were called miyakodori, "birds of the capital." Hearing the name reminded Narihira of the woman he loved, whom he had left in the capital, and he wrote the poem "Na ni shi owaba iza koto towamu miyakodori waga omou hito wa ari ya nashi ya to."

Another Poem from "The Journey East" in The Tales of Ise
The next poem also appears in "The Journey East" in The Tales of Ise. On his journey to the eastern provinces, the hero saw kakitsubata (rabbit-ear irises) blooming at Yatsuhashi in the province of Mikawa and wrote this poem. The word tsuma in the poem is a kakekotoba (a word with two meanings): 妻つま, "wife," and 褄つま, "the hem of a robe." The poem also places the five sounds "ka-ki-tsu-ha-ta" at the start of its five lines. This technique is called oriku.

In Japanese

唐衣からころも 着きつつなれにし つましあれば
はるばる来きぬる 旅たびをしぞ思おもふ

The Tales of Ise, Ariwara no Narihira

In English

Like a Chinese robe I have worn so long it feels part of me, I have a dear wife I left in the capital, so I feel deeply how far I have come on this journey.
Ukiyo-e by Katsukawa Shunshō

This ukiyo-e shows Ariwara no Narihira writing the poem "Na ni shi owaba iza koto towamu...." Later, a nearby dumpling shop began selling Kototoi Dango, named after the poem. They became popular, and it is said that this is why the nearby bridge came to be called Kototoi Bridge, and the street Kototoi Street.

The birds floating on the river in the upper left of the print are miyakodori. There are several theories, but the most widely accepted one is that the miyakodori is the black-headed gull, which is now known as the official bird of Tokyo.

Ogata Kōrin's Irises at Yatsuhashi

Irises at Yatsuhashi (Yatsuhashi-zu Byōbu), a folding screen by the Edo-period painter and craftsman Ogata Kōrin, takes as its subject the story of Yatsuhashi in Mikawa from "The Journey East" in The Tales of Ise.

A Poem from "Nagisa-no-in" in The Tales of Ise
The next poem also appears in The Tales of Ise.
Ariwara no Narihira wrote it while viewing cherry blossoms at Nagisa-no-in, the country house of Prince Koretaka (the first son of Emperor Montoku) at Katano-ga-hara (now Hirakata City, Osaka).

In Japanese

世よの中なかに たえて桜さくらの なかりせば
春はるの心こころは のどけからまし

In English

If there were no cherry blossoms at all in this world,
how calm people's hearts would be in spring.
The Site of Nagisa-no-in

Nagisa-motomachi, Hirakata City, Osaka. This was the country house of Prince Koretaka, the first son of Emperor Montoku. The Tales of Ise describes Prince Koretaka, Ariwara no Narihira, and others visiting this place and enjoying the cherry blossoms. Narihira's poem "Yo no naka ni taete sakura no nakariseba haru no kokoro wa nodokekaramashi" is said to have been written at Nagisa-no-in. Today a stone monument with this poem stands at the site.

Folding Screen of Cherry-Blossom Viewing

This folding screen shows a scene from "Nagisa-no-in" in The Tales of Ise. It is a work by Sumiyoshi Gukei, a painter of the early Edo period.

Narihira's Farewell Poem
The next poem is the last one in The Tales of Ise. Its headnote says, "Long ago, a man fell ill and felt that he was about to die." It is Narihira's death poem, written when he fell ill and knew his end was near.

In Japanese

つひに行ゆく 道みちとはかねて 聞ききしかど
昨日きのう今日けふとは 思おもはざりしを

In English

I had long heard it is the road we all must take in the end,
but I never thought it would be so soon, today or tomorrow.
Ukiyo-e by Tsukioka Yoshitoshi

This ukiyo-e is Narihira Ason Carrying the Lady Nijō on His Back as They Flee by Tsukioka Yoshitoshi. The Tales of Ise has a scene in which Narihira runs away with the Nijō Empress (Fujiwara no Takaiko), carrying her on his back, and this print shows that scene.

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures. You can see people on a bridge enjoying the autumn leaves floating down the Tatsuta River, and people fishing on the riverbank. The words mizu kukuru in the poem are usually taken to mean "tie-dyeing," but they are sometimes read as "flowing under the water." Looking at Hokusai's print, which reading do you think it shows?

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period.
It shows a scene that matches the poem in the Hyakunin Isshu.

Narihira's Hyakunin Isshu poem was written while looking at a painted folding screen, but this print shows the autumn leaves spreading over the Tatsuta River as if he were really seeing them.

Perhaps Narihira has come across a scene just like the one in his poem, and is fondly remembering the day he wrote it for the screen.
