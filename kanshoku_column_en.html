How Hyakunin Isshu Poets Got Their Names

Many poets in the Hyakunin Isshu have names that incorporate their court office or rank directly. This was common in Heian aristocratic society, where it was standard practice to refer to a person by their official title or position. The following sections introduce poets whose names are derived from their offices or ranks.

Female poets named after a family member's office

No. 53
Michitsuna no Haha
("Mother of Michitsuna")

Her own name is unknown. Her son Fujiwara no Michitsuna served as Udaishō (Commander of the Right Imperial Guard), so she came to be called "the mother of Michitsuna the Udaishō" — Udaishō Michitsuna no Haha.

U-konoe-fu (Right Guard) Udaishō

No. 54
Gidō-Sanshi no Haha
("Mother of Gidō-Sanshi")

Her own name is Takashine no Takako. Her son Fujiwara no Korechika became Naidaijin (Minister of the Center) and used the Chinese-style honorific Gidō-Sanshi ("equivalent to the Three Senior Ministers") — which became the basis for his mother's name.

Gidō-Sanshi Naidaijin

No. 56
Izumi Shikibu
(和泉式部)

Izumi derives from her husband Tachibana no Michisada, who served as Governor of Izumi Province. Shikibu is said to come from her father, who served as an official in the Ministry of Ceremonial (Shikibu-shō). Women in the Heian court were often identified by the offices held by their male relatives.

Izumi-no-kami Shikibu-shō

No. 57
Murasaki Shikibu
(紫式部)

Shikibu comes from her father Fujiwara no Tametoki, who served in the Ministry of Ceremonial. Murasaki is said to derive from the heroine "Murasaki no Ue" in The Tale of Genji, which she authored.

Shikibu-shō

No. 58
Daini no Sanmi
(大弐三位)

Her husband Takashine no Nariyuki served as Dazai-no-Daini (Senior Deputy Governor-General of Dazaifu). She herself held Senior Third Rank (Sanmi). Her name combines her husband's office and her own rank.

Dazai-no-Daini Sanmi (Third Court Rank)

The Ason Surname and the Hierarchy of Court Ranks (Ikai)

You may notice that many poets in the Hyakunin Isshu carry the surname Ason (朝臣). In ancient Japan, the Eight Hereditary Titles (Yakusa no Kabane) defined a family's social standing. By the Heian period, these had consolidated in practice, and Ason had become the mark of a distinguished noble lineage. Upon that foundation of birth, a nobleman's own achievements and the ruler's favor determined his ikai (court rank) — the numbered ladder of precedence that governed every appointment. Family pedigree set the starting point; personal ability shaped the career. That was the governing principle of the Heian aristocratic world.

Poets Connected to the Imperial Missions to Tang China (Kentōshi)

The missions to Tang China were voyages undertaken at great risk to life. Several poets in the Hyakunin Isshu had their fates intertwined with these legendary expeditions.

No. 7
Abe no Nakamaro
(阿倍仲麻呂)

He traveled to Tang China as a student scholar. On his return voyage he was shipwrecked, and he never succeeded in reaching Japan again, spending the rest of his life in China.

Kentō-ryūgakusei (student scholar)

No. 11
Sangi Takamura
(参議篁)

Appointed as Vice-Ambassador of a Tang mission, he refused to board the ship for various reasons and never made the crossing to China.

Kentō-fukushi (Vice-Ambassador)

No. 24
Sugawara no Michizane
(菅原道真)

Appointed as Ambassador to Tang China, he petitioned for the missions to be suspended, citing the decline of the Tang dynasty and the dangers of the sea voyage. In 894 the suspension was approved, bringing this long-running state enterprise to an end.

Kentō-taishi (Ambassador) Abolition of Kentōshi

Poets with the Prefix Saki no ("Former")

In the Heian period, it was customary to address someone who had retired from an office by prefixing their former title with saki no (前, "former"). This was not merely a label for ex-officials; it carried a respectful weight, acknowledging the prestige of the position they had held.

No. 66
Saki no Daisōjō Gyōson
(前大僧正行尊)

He composed many waka poems during his austere Shugendo mountain asceticism and pilgrimages across the provinces — a monk-poet who balanced rigorous spiritual practice with the art of verse.

Daisōjō Yamabushi asceticism

No. 95
Saki no Daisōjō Jien
(前大僧正慈円)

The monk Jien had served as Daisōjō (Senior High Priest), one of the highest ranks in the Buddhist clergy. He also served four terms as Tendai Zasu (head of the Tendai school at Enryakuji on Mt. Hiei). Saki no indicates he had retired from the post.

Daisōjō (High Priest) Sōkan (Buddhist clergy rank)

Poets Who Renounced the World — Monks and Court Offices

The Hyakunin Isshu also includes poets who had left secular life to become monks.

No. 95
Saki no Daisōjō Jien
(前大僧正慈円)

He served four terms as Tendai Zasu (head of the Tendai school at Enryakuji on Mt. Hiei). He is also known as the author of the historical chronicle Gukanshō.

Daisōjō (High Priest) Tendai Zasu

Fujiwara no Tadamichi served as Regent and Chancellor before taking Buddhist orders at Hōshōji temple — an unusual combination of reaching the pinnacle of secular power and then entering monastic life.

A Warrior Who Became a Monk

No. 86
Saigyō (西行)

He served at court as a Sahyōe-no-jō (officer of the Left Guard) and as one of the hokumen no bushi (warrior attendants of the Retired Emperor Toba). Around the age of 23, he suddenly took Buddhist vows, becoming a wandering monk known as Saigyō.

Hokumen no bushi (imperial guard) Sahyōe-no-jō (Left Guard officer) Buddhist monk
