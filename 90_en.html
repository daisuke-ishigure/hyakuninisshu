Poem 90
見みせばやな 雄島おじまのあまの 袖そでだにも
ぬれにぞぬれし 色いろはかはわらず
Author: Attendant to Empress Inpu (dates unknown)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
The fisher's clothes, though cheap, withstand The drenching they receive; But see I my floods of tears have blurred The colours of my sleeve, As for thy love I grieve.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I wish I could show you — these sleeves of mine, soaked through and through with tears that never change color, never dry.
Commentary
The headnote in the Senzai Wakashū reads, "composed as a love poem at a poetry contest" — this was a poem on an assigned topic. In the poetic conventions of the time, weeping "tears of blood" was a standard expression for overwhelming emotion. This poem never states "tears of blood" outright, but through the phrase "the color of my sleeve," readers of the time would have understood, by unspoken convention, that it meant sleeves stained by tears of blood.
About the Poet
The Attendant to Empress Inpu served Empress Inpu, a daughter of Emperor Go-Shirakawa and the elder sister of Princess Shikishi (Poem 89). Having composed a great many poems in her lifetime, she came to be known as "Senju Taifu" — "Taifu of a Thousand Poems."

The Poet's Feelings (A Personal Interpretation)

Tears no one else can see
quietly stain my sleeves.

I only wish, somehow,
that you could know how deep this pain runs.
