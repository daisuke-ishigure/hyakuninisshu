Poem 90
見みせばやな 雄島おじまのあまの 袖そでだにも
ぬれにぞぬれし 色いろはかはわらず
Author: Attendant to Empress Inpu (c. 1130–c. 1200)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
The fisher's clothes, though cheap, withstand The drenching they receive; But see I my floods of tears have blurred The colours of my sleeve, As for thy love I grieve.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I wish I could show you — these sleeves of mine, soaked through and through with tears that never change color, never dry.
Commentary
Written on the theme "love" for a poetry contest. In poems of the time, "tears of blood" meant extreme grief. The poem does not say it, but readers understood "the color of my sleeves" to mean sleeves stained by tears of blood.
About the Poet
Taifu served Inpumon-in, a daughter of Emperor Go-Shirakawa and the elder sister of Princess Shikishi. She wrote so many poems that she was called "Taifu of a Thousand Poems."

The poet was a lady-in-waiting (nyōbō) who served Inpumon-in (Princess Ryōshi), a daughter of Emperor Go-Shirakawa and the elder sister of Princess Shikishi.

She took part in the Karin'en, the poetry circle led by Priest Shun'e, and knew Fujiwara no Sadaie, Jakuren, Saigyō, Minamoto no Yorimasa, and others. It is also said that she once spent a whole night enjoying linked verse (renga) with Kojijū, who was seen as her rival.

Appendix
Tears of Blood
In waka, they are called "kōrui" or "kurenai no namida" (crimson tears).
The Original Poem (Honka)
The poet's Hyakunin Isshu poem uses honkadori (borrowing from an older poem), based on the following poem by Minamoto no Shigeyuki.
Her poem is said to have been written as a reply to Shigeyuki's poem.

In Japanese

松島まつしまや 雄島おじまの磯いそに 漁あさりせし
海人あまの袖そでこそ かくは濡ぬれしか matsushimaya ojimanoisoni asariseshi
amanosodekoso kakuhanureshika Goshūi Wakashū, Minamoto no Shigeyuki

In English

Would even the sleeves of the fisherfolk who fish on the rocky shore of Ojima at Matsushima
be as wet as my sleeves, soaked with tears?
Poems of Sleeves Wet with Tears
In waka, wetting one's sleeves with tears is a standard way of expressing sorrow or the pain of love.
The next poem appears in Chapter 40, "Minori" (The Rites), of The Tale of Genji. It was written by the Retired Minister (Chiji no Otodo), once the Tō no Chūjō, Hikaru Genji's rival and close friend, for Genji, who had lost his beloved Murasaki. The minister links Murasaki's death to his own grief long ago, when his younger sister Aoi, Genji's first wife, died.

Even that autumn long ago (the autumn when Aoi died) feels like now, and on my sleeves, already wet with tears, more dew seems to fall.

Misebaya

A perennial plant that has been loved in Japan since ancient times as a plant of the Man'yōshū.
Its name comes from "Whom shall I show (miseba ya) these beautiful flowers to?",
and it is also called "tama no o."
