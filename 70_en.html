Poem 70
さびしさに 宿やどを立たち出いでて ながむれば
いづずくも同おなじ 秋あきの夕ゆう暮ぐれ
Author: Priest Ryōzen (dates unknown)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
The prospect from my cottage shows No other hut in sight; The solitude depresses me, Like deepening twilight On a chill autumn night.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Feeling lonely, I stepped outside to gaze at the view — and everywhere I looked, it was the same twilight of autumn.
Commentary
The Shika Wakashū says this was written when Ryōzen, a monk of Mount Hiei, began living in Ōhara, a quiet place with few people. Lonely, he went out of his hut, only to find beauty in the empty autumn dusk. After this poem, ending a waka with "autumn dusk" became popular.
About the Poet
Ryōzen was a monk of Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei and head of Gion Shrine (now Yasaka Shrine). He is said to have later lived quietly in Ōhara.

Appendix
The Three Evening Poems (Sanseki no Uta)
The following three poems in the Shin Kokin Wakashū, each ending with "aki no yūgure" (autumn dusk), are praised as the "Three Evening Poems."

Jakuren

In Japanese

さびしさは その色いろとしも なかりけり
まき立たつ山やまの 秋あきの夕暮ゆうぐれ Shin Kokin Wakashū, Jakuren

In English

Loneliness does not come from any one color (such as the color of autumn leaves). I feel it in the (colorless) autumn dusk on a mountain where evergreens (maki) stand.

Saigyō

In Japanese

心こころなき 身みにもあはれは 知しられけり
鴫しぎ立たつ沢さわの 秋あきの夕暮ゆうぐれ Shin Kokin Wakashū, Saigyō

In English

Even I, who gave up feeling when I became a monk, am deeply moved by the stillness of autumn dusk over the marsh after a snipe (shigi) has flown up.

Fujiwara no Sadaie

In Japanese

見渡みわたせば 花はなも紅葉もみぢもなかりけり
浦うらの苫屋とまやの 秋あきの夕暮ゆうぐれ Shin Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Sadaie

In English

Looking around, I see neither blossoms nor autumn leaves. All that meets my eyes is the autumn dusk on the shore, with only rough huts thatched with rushes (toma).
