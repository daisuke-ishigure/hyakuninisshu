Poem 75
契ちぎりおきし させもが露つゆを いのちにて
あはわれ今年ことしの 秋あきもいぬめり
Author: Fujiwara no Mototoshi (1060–1142)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
It is a promise unfulfilled, For which I humbly sue The dainty little mugwort plant Relies upon the dew, And I rely—on you.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
You promised me, and I staked my life on it like the morning dew on the wormwood. But another autumn is passing, alas.
Commentary
Mototoshi asked Fujiwara no Tadamichi to choose his son Kōkaku, a monk at Kōfuku-ji, as lecturer at the Yuima-e ceremony. Tadamichi answered with a line from an old poem that seemed to promise yes, but Kōkaku was not chosen. Disappointed, Mototoshi wrote this poem.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Mototoshi was a great-grandson of Fujiwara no Michinaga but never rose above a low rank. He valued the traditional style and clashed with the new-style Minamoto no Toshiyori, yet the two were the leading poets of their time. Fujiwara no Toshinari was his student.

Fujiwara no Mototoshi was a great-grandson of Fujiwara no Michinaga. He rose no higher than Junior Fifth Rank, Upper Grade, as Assistant Captain of the Left Gate Guards (Saemon no suke, the deputy head of the Left Gate Guards).

Perhaps because of his low rank, he seems to have had few chances to take part in poetry events at court. His first public poetry event was the Horikawa-in Ontoki Hyakushu Waka (the Horikawa Hundred-Poem Sequence), when he was already about 46. After that he took part in poetry contests as a poet and a judge. Known for his learning in Chinese poetry and prose as well as waka, he was, together with Minamoto no Toshiyori, one of the leading figures of the poetry world in the age of rule by retired emperors.

Late in life he took Fujiwara no Toshinari as his student.

Appendix
The "Shimeji ga Hara" Poem
In answer to Fujiwara no Mototoshi's request for a favor, Fujiwara no Tadamichi quoted a line from the next poem.
This poem is said to have been composed by the Kannon of Kiyomizu.
"Shimeji ga hara no sashimogusa" (the mugwort of Shimeji Field) is an introductory phrase (jokotoba) in which the following words, "sashimo ari tomo" (even if things are so), are left out.

In Japanese

なほ頼たのめ 標茅しめじが原はらの さしも草ぐさ
われ世よの中なかに あらむ限かぎりは nahotanome shimejigaharano sashimogusa
wareyononakani aramukagiriha Shin Kokin Wakashū, the Kannon of Kiyomizu

In English

Rely on me, even if you are troubled enough to burn like the mugwort of Shimeji Field,
for as long as I am in this world.
The Yuima-e Ceremony

A Buddhist service held every year for seven days from the tenth day of the tenth month at Kōfuku-ji, with lectures on the Vimalakirti Sutra (Yuimakyō). Being chosen as the lecturer was an honor for a monk, and it is said that a monk could not be promoted to the temple's leadership without serving as lecturer. Kōfuku-ji was the family temple of the Fujiwara, and Fujiwara no Tadamichi, as head of the Fujiwara clan, was involved in running it and had the power to choose the lecturer. His reply quoting "Nao tanome Shimeji ga hara no..." is thought to have meant that he had accepted the request: "Don't worry; rely on me and wait calmly."
