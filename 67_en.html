Poem 67
春はるの夜よの 夢ゆめばかりなる 手枕たまくらに
かひいなく立たたむん 名なこそをしけれ
Author: Suō no Naishi (c. 1037–c. 1111)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
If I had made thy proffered arm A pillow for my head For but the moment's time, in which A summer's dream had fled, What would the world have said?
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
In the spring night, resting my head on an arm for just a brief dream — how I grieve that my name might spread for such a fleeting thing.
Commentary
One night at the Nijō palace, as people talked till late, Suō no Naishi said she was sleepy and wanted a pillow. Fujiwara no Tadaie slipped his arm under the blinds and said, "Use this as your pillow." She politely turned him down with this poem on the spot. Tadaie was Fujiwara no Sadaie's great-grandfather.
About the Poet
Suō no Naishi's name comes from her father, governor of Suō; her real name was Taira no Chūshi. She served four emperors, Go-Reizei, Go-Sanjō, Shirakawa, and Horikawa, and was a famous court poet with 35 poems in imperial anthologies.

Suō no Naishi served Emperor Go-Reizei, the 70th emperor, as a lady-in-waiting.

Her Hyakunin Isshu poem was written one night when the ladies-in-waiting were talking all night long at the palace of Princess Shōshi, empress (chūgū) of Emperor Go-Reizei. Fujiwara no Tadaie slipped his arm in under the blind, and that led to the poem.

Appendix
Fujiwara no Tadaie's Answer
Fujiwara no Tadaie answered Suō no Naishi's poem "Haru no yo no..." as follows.

In Japanese

契ちぎりありて 春はるの夜よ深ふかき 手枕たまくらを
いかがかひなき 夢ゆめになすべき chigiriarite harunoyofukaki tamakurao
ikagakahinaki yumeninasubeki Senzai Wakashū, Fujiwara no Tadaie

In English

Fate brought me to offer you my arm as a pillow late on this spring night. How could I let it become an empty dream?
The Famous "Shitamoe" Poem
Shitamoe (smoldering underneath) is a word used for burning with love in secret. This poem became famous, so much so that Suō no Naishi came to be called "Shitamoe no Naishi."

In Japanese

恋こひわびて 眺ながむる空そらの 浮雲うきぐもや
わが下萌したもえの 煙けぶりなるらむ kohiwabite nagamurusorano ukigumoya
wagashitamoeno keburinaruramu Kin'yō Wakashū, Suō no Naishi

In English

Are the drifting clouds I gaze at, worn out with love, the smoke from my secret burning, turned into clouds?
A Poem Written on a Pillar
of the House She Had to Give Up
When Suō no Naishi had to give up a house full of memories, she wrote this poem on one of its pillars.

In Japanese

住すみわびて 我われさへ軒のきの 忍草しのぶくさ
しのぶかたがた しげき宿やどかな sumiwabite waresahenokino shinobukusa
shinobukatagata shigekiyadokana Kin'yō Wakashū, Suō no Naishi

In English

It has become hard to live here, and I must leave. O shinobu fern growing on the eaves: this house is thick with things to remember (shinobu).
A Poem Saigyō Wrote
at Suō no Naishi's Ruined House
Saigyō visited the house where Suō no Naishi had once lived and later left, as if visiting a sacred place, and wrote the next poem there.

In Japanese

古いにしえは ついゐし宿やども あるものを
何なにをか今日きょうの しるしにはせん inishieha tsuiishiyadomo arumonoo
naniokakyouno shirushinihasen Sankashū, Saigyō

In English

The house where she lived long ago still stands, but what shall I take as a sign of my visit today?
