Poem 22
吹ふくからに 秋あきの草木くさきの しをるれば
むべ山風やまかぜを 嵐あらしといふうらむん
Author: Fun'ya no Yasuhide (dates unknown)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
The mountain wind in autumn time Is well called "hurricane"; It hurries canes and twigs along, And whirls them o'er the plain To scatter them again.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
The moment the autumn wind blows, the grasses and trees wither. Now I understand why the mountain wind is called "arashi" — storm.
Commentary
This poem applies a technique borrowed from Chinese verse called rigōshi, which plays on splitting and recombining characters — here, the characters for "mountain" (山) and "wind" (風) combine to form the character for "storm" (嵐). Today "arashi" refers to a violent storm, but at the time it meant simply the wind blowing down from the mountains.
About the Poet
Fun'ya no Yasuhide was one of the leading poets of the Heian period, counted among the Six Poetic Sages. He is also known as the father of Fun'ya no Asayasu. His friendship with fellow Sage Ono no Komachi is well documented — when Yasuhide was posted to Mikawa Province as junior secretary, he asked Komachi to accompany him, and she replied with a poem comparing herself to a rootless floating weed, willing to drift along if a stream should invite her. Whether she actually went with him is not recorded, but the exchange shows the literary bond between the two.

The Poet's Feelings (A Personal Interpretation)

In the wind blowing down from the mountains,
the autumn grasses lose their color.
Ah — I see now why "mountain" and "wind"
together make "storm."
