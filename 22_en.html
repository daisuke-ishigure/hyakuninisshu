Poem 22
吹ふくからに 秋あきの草木くさきの しをるれば
むべ山風やまかぜを 嵐あらしといふうらむん
Author: Fun'ya no Yasuhide (dates unknown–c. 885)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
The mountain wind in autumn time Is well called "hurricane"; It hurries canes and twigs along, And whirls them o'er the plain To scatter them again.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
The moment the autumn wind blows, the grasses and trees wither. Now I understand why the mountain wind is called "arashi" — storm.
Commentary
The poem uses a Chinese word game of splitting and joining characters: 山 (mountain) and 風 (wind) make 嵐 (arashi, storm). Today "arashi" means a violent storm, but then it meant wind blowing down from the mountains.
About the Poet
Fun'ya no Yasuhide was a leading Heian poet, one of the Six Poetic Sages, and the father of Fun'ya no Asayasu. His friendship with Ono no Komachi is famous: when he was posted to Mikawa he invited her along, and she answered with a poem saying she might drift along like floating weed. Whether she went is unknown.

Fun'ya no Yasuhide is thought to have been a descendant of Emperor Tenmu. In 752, in the Nara period, the Fun'ya family was given the family name Fun'ya together with the title Mahito. This was the highest of the "Eight Titles" (Yakusa no Kabane) that Emperor Tenmu had set up.

In the Heian period, however, as the Fujiwara took over the central government and reshaped politics, the Fun'ya family lost standing. Yasuhide himself rose no higher than Assistant Director of the Bureau of Sewing (Nuidono no Suke). Despite his noble bloodline, he had to struggle at court as a low-ranking noble.

As a poet, though, he was highly praised. Ki no Tsurayuki chose him as one of the Six Poetic Sages, and he is remembered as a master of clever wording. There is also a charming story: when he was going to take up a post in the province of Mikawa, he invited his friend Ono no Komachi, another of the Six Poetic Sages, to come with him, and Komachi answered with a witty poem, "Wabinureba..." ("I have fallen so low that I will go anywhere I am invited"). See Ono no Komachi's page for more.

In some old manuscripts of the Kokin Wakashū, Yasuhide's Hyakunin Isshu poem is credited to his son Asayasu. So although father and son both have poems in the Hyakunin Isshu, one theory says both poems are really Asayasu's.

Appendix
The Mube Fruit

Do you know mube, a plant of the akebia family? Its name is said to come from an old legend about Emperor Tenji, who had his capital in Ōmi (Shiga Prefecture).

According to the legend, when Emperor Tenji was traveling through the province of Ōmi, he met an old couple living in the mountains. Though old, they were amazingly healthy and had many children. When the emperor asked the secret of their long life, the couple offered him a fruit that grew in the mountains and said, "We eat this every day."

When the emperor tasted the fruit, he was deeply impressed by its goodness and flavor and said, "Mube naru kana" ("No wonder!"). Mube is an old word used when you are fully convinced of something.

The emperor's words left such an impression that people came to call the fruit mube.

Fun'ya no Yasuhide wrote "mube yamakaze o arashi to iuramu." His choice of the word mube may have carried respect for a beautiful word linked to the imperial family, his own ancestors.

"Mube Yamakaze o" in The Pillow Book
In the section "The Day After a Typhoon" (Nowaki no mata no hi koso) of The Pillow Book by Sei Shōnagon, she describes the scene on the day after a typhoon has passed. Sei Shōnagon watches with interest the plants of the garden scattered by the wind and a beautiful woman with her hair tousled, just out of bed. She also writes about gazing thoughtfully at the scene outside, murmuring "mube yamakaze o," a phrase from Fun'ya no Yasuhide's poem, and feeling how elegant it is.
Poems That Play with Kanji

Fujiwara no Suemichi wrote a poem that splits the kanji 愁 ("sorrow") into 秋 ("autumn") and 心 ("heart").

In Japanese

事毎ことごとに 悲かなしかりけり むべしこそ
秋あきの 心こころを 愁うれひと 言いひけれ Senzai Wakashū, Fujiwara no Suemichi

In English

Everything feels sad. No wonder people write "autumn" and "heart" together and call it "sorrow."

Ki no Tomonori wrote a poem that splits 梅 ("plum") into 木 ("tree") and 毎 ("every").

In Japanese

雪ゆき降ふれば 木き 毎ごとに 花はなぞ 咲さきにける
いづれを梅うめと わきて折おらまし Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tomonori

In English

When snow falls, every tree seems to bloom with white flowers.
Since the snow and blossoms look alike, how can I tell which tree is the plum, to break off a branch?

The Six Poetic Sages (Rokkasen)

When the Kokin Wakashū was compiled, these six poets, including Fun'ya no Yasuhide, were very famous poets of the generation before. In the Japanese preface to the Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tsurayuki picked them out as "people whose names were known in recent times" and judged their poetry. His judgments were quite harsh, but the six later came to be called the Rokkasen, the Six Poetic Sages, and were highly praised as great poets.

See Ki no Tsurayuki's judgments of the six poets.

One of the Six Poetic Sages, Ōtomo no Kuronushi, has no poem in the Hyakunin Isshu, while the other five were all chosen.

The Mube-yama Sugoroku

This is a Hyakunin Isshu sugoroku (a board game like snakes and ladders) made in the Edo period, called Shinpan Hyakunin Isshu Mube-yama Sugoroku. Poem 1, by Emperor Tenji, is at the lower right, and the cards are lined up counterclockwise, almost in order. It is said that a player who landed on Poem 22, Fun'ya no Yasuhide's "Fuku kara ni aki no kusaki no shiorureba mube yamakaze o arashi to iuramu," won a prize.
It is about 52 cm tall and 75 cm wide.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period.
It shows a scene that matches the poem in the Hyakunin Isshu.

Yasuhide used the word play "mountain + wind = storm" (山 + 風 = 嵐) to explain, with reasoning, why the storm is so strong: "No wonder (mube)." Kuniyoshi turned that power into a dynamic picture of people tossed about by a fierce wind.

A man desperately chasing his blown-away umbrella, a woman holding down her kimono as it flaps up: Kuniyoshi deliberately turns Yasuhide's clever poem into a playful scene of Edo townspeople in a panic. It is a very interesting picture that, with Kuniyoshi's typical humor, brings the poem of Yasuhide, a descendant of the noble Mahito, into the everyday life of ordinary people.
