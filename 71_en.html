Poem 71
夕ゆうされば 門田かどたの稲葉いなば おとづずれて
蘆あしのまろやに 秋風あきかぜぞ吹ふく
Author: Upper Counselor Tsunenobu (1016–1097)
Source: Kin'yō Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
This autumn night the wind blows shrill, And would that I could catch Its message, as it whistles through The rushes in the thatch And leaves of my rice-patch.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
As evening falls, the breeze comes rustling through the rice stalks by the gate, and the autumn wind blows through the reed-thatched hut.
Commentary
The Kin'yō Wakashū says this was written on the theme "autumn wind at a farmhouse" at a gathering at the Umezu villa of Tsunenobu's relative Minamoto no Morokata. Country life was in fashion, and many nobles built villas outside the capital.
About the Poet
Minamoto no Tsunenobu, a great-grandson of Prince Atsumi (a son of Emperor Uda), excelled at Chinese poetry, waka, and music. When boats for each art were raced on the Ōi River, he arrived late and said any boat would do. Like Fujiwara no Kintō, he was praised for "the talent of three boats."

Minamoto no Tsunenobu was a descendant of the Uda Genji, the Minamoto line founded by Emperor Uda.

A poetry gathering (utakai) was held at the villa of his relative Minamoto no Morokata in Umezu (Umezu, Ukyō Ward, Kyoto, south of Uzumasa on the east bank of the Katsura River). His Hyakunin Isshu poem was written at that gathering.

His son was Minamoto no Toshiyori and his grandson was Priest Shun'e,
so three generations were chosen for the Hyakunin Isshu.

A Poem About the Hio of the Uji River
The next poem by Minamoto no Tsunenobu is in the Kin'yō Wakashū.
Hio are young sweetfish, and ajiro were fish traps for catching them, a winter sight on the Uji River.

In Japanese

月つき清きよみ 瀬々せぜの網代あじろに よるひをは
玉藻たまもに冴さゆる 氷こおりなりけり tsukikiyomi sezenoajironi yoruhioha
tamamonisayuru koorinarikeri Kin'yō Wakashū, Minamoto no Tsunenobu

In English

In the clear moonlight, the hio gathering at the fish traps in the shallows sparkle like ice frozen on the water plants.
A Landscape Poem Written at Sumiyoshi
The next poem by Minamoto no Tsunenobu is in the Go Shūi Wakashū. Sumiyoshi (today's Sumiyoshi Ward, Osaka) was once a beautiful place of white sand and green pines. The poem was written when Retired Emperor Go-Sanjō visited Sumiyoshi.

In Japanese

沖おきつ風かぜ 吹ふきにけらしな 住吉すみよしの
松まつのしづ枝えを あらふ白波しらなみ okitsukaze fukinikerashina sumiyoshino
matsunoshizueo arafushiranami Go Shūi Wakashū, Minamoto no Tsunenobu

In English

The wind must have blown out at sea. White waves rise high enough to reach the lower branches of the pines on the shore of Sumiyoshi.
Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

People look up at a flock of wild geese far in the distance. It shows a farming village where autumn will soon arrive.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e print is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period. It is based on the following story.

Tsunenobu murmured Ki no Tsurayuki's poem "Karakoromo utsu koe kikeba tsuki kiyomi mada nenu hito wo sora ni shiru kana" ("I hear the sound of cloth being beaten. Under the clear autumn moon, someone is still awake. Will that person, too, look up at this moon?").

Then the ogre of the Suzakumon-in, who loved elegant things, appeared and recited a Chinese poem: "North of the Dipper, wild geese fly across; under the moon by the south tower, winter clothes are beaten" ("I see wild geese flying past the Northern Dipper. By the south tower, late at night under the moon, I hear the sound of fulling blocks softening clothes for winter.").

The Ōi River

The Ōi River flows through the beautiful Arashiyama area of Kyoto and is also called the Katsura River. Heian nobles went boating on this river.

Sightseeing on roofed pleasure boats (yakatabune) to enjoy the Arashiyama scenery in every season is still popular today.
