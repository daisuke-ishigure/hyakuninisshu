Waka Techniques:
Jokotoba (Preface Words)

Jokotoba, literally “preface words,” are phrases placed at the start of a poem to lead into the words the poet really wants to say. They paint a vivid scene — usually from nature — and then pivot to the poet’s feelings at a single linking word.

Readers of Homer may recognize the idea from the extended simile, in which a long image from nature, such as falling leaves or a lion in the hills, is laid out before the story resumes. A jokotoba works in much the same way, but in miniature: within just thirty-one syllables (more precisely, morae, the sound units of Japanese verse), the scene often fills the first half of the poem or more.

Unlike makurakotoba (pillow words), which are fixed five-syllable phrases handed down by tradition, a jokotoba is created by the poet for that particular poem, and is seven syllables or longer.

A jokotoba connects to the words it leads into in one of three ways:

  • Simile: the scene is a comparison: as long as the trailing tail of the mountain bird, so long is this night (Poem 3).
  • Kakekotoba (pivot word): the scene leads into a word with a double meaning (Poem 13).
  • Sound: the scene ends with a word that sounds just like the next one, much like a pun or a rhyme: yoru (the waves draw near) → yoru (night) (Poem 18).

Poem 3: Kakinomoto no Hitomaro

Type: Simile

As long as the trailing tail feathers of the mountain bird — through these long, long nights, must I sleep alone?

The “long, long night” is likened to the long, drooping tail feathers of the mountain bird.

Poem 13: Retired Emperor Yōzei

Type: Kakekotoba

Just as the streams from the peak of Tsukuba flow down to fill the deep pools of the Mina River, so my love for you has gathered deep within me.

It leads into kohi, a kakekotoba on 恋 (love) and 水 (water).

Poem 14: Minister of the Left of Kawara

Type: Simile

For whose sake has my heart become as disordered as the tangled patterns of Shinobu-mojizuri cloth of Mutsu? It is certainly not my own doing.

The poet’s tangled heart (midare) is likened to the tangled dye pattern of shinobu-mojizuri cloth from Michinoku.

Poem 18: Fujiwara no Toshiyuki Ason

Type: Sound

Even in my dreams, I find myself longing to go to you along the path of dreams — but do I hide from others' eyes even in the night?

The sound of yoru (寄る, the waves draw near) is repeated in yoru (夜, night).

Poem 19: Ise

Type: Kakekotoba

In this short life, even as brief as a joint of reed at Naniwa Bay, must I pass through this world without ever meeting you?

It leads into fushi no ma, a kakekotoba on “the short space between two joints of a reed” and “the briefest moment.”

Poem 27: Middle Counselor Kanesuke

Type: Sound

Like the Izumi River that wells up and flows through Mika Plain — when did I first see you, that I should long for you so?

The sound of izumi (泉, spring) is repeated in itsu mi (いつ見, “when did I see”).

Poem 39: Councillor Hitoshi

Type: Sound

Though I try to hide it, my longing spills over. Why is it that I cannot stop thinking of that person?

The sound of shino (篠, bamboo grass) is repeated in shino(bu) (忍ぶ, to endure in secret).

Poem 46: Sone no Yoshitada

Type: Simile

Like a boatman who has lost his oar on the swift straits of Yura — I drift, not knowing where my love is taking me.

Not knowing where one is headed (yukue mo shiranu) is likened to a boatman crossing the Yura strait who has lost his oar and drifts.

Poem 48: Minamoto no Shigeyuki

Type: Simile

Like the waves crashing against the rocks in the fierce wind, I alone am broken to pieces by this sorrow.

Being the only one heartbroken (onore nomi) is likened to waves dashed against a rock by a fierce wind.

Poem 49: Ōnakatomi no Yoshinobu Ason

Type: Simile

Like the fire tended by the palace guards, I burn at night and fade in the day — lost in endless longing.

Burning by night (yoru wa moe) is likened to the fires kindled by the guards (eji) who watch the palace gates.

Poem 51: Fujiwara no Sanekata Ason

Type: Sound

I cannot even tell you how I feel — like the smoldering fire of ibuki wormwood, my hidden love quietly burns.

Repeating sashimogusa and sashimo emphasizes the sound of the words.

Poem 58: Daini no Sanmi

Type: Sound

When the wind blows through the bamboo groves of Arima and Ina — of course I have not forgotten you.

From the bamboo grass rustling (soyo) in the wind, it leads into the word soyo.

Poem 77: Retired Emperor Sutoku

Type: Simile

Like the river that is split by rocks and flows apart — though we are separated now, I believe we will meet again in the end.

The wish to meet again someday, even after parting, is likened to a swift stream that is split by a rock but flows back together again.

Poem 88: Attendant to Empress Kōka

Type: Kakekotoba

For just one brief night together, like the cut end of a reed at Naniwa — must I go on longing for you forever?

It leads into karine, a kakekotoba on 刈り根 (the stubble of cut reeds) and 仮寝 (a brief sleep).

Poem 92: Sanuki, attendant to retired Emperor Nijō

Type: Simile

My sleeves are like a rock hidden beneath the tides — unseen by others, they are never dry.

“No one knows it, but my sleeves are never dry” is likened to a rock far out at sea that stays hidden even at low tide.

Poem 97: Acting Middle Counselor Sadaie

Type: Simile

Waiting for someone who will not come, at the quiet evening shore — like salt burned from the seaweed, I too am consumed by longing.

The poet’s burning longing is likened to seaweed burned for salt at Matsuho Bay in the evening calm.

Other Waka Techniques

  • Makurakotoba (Pillow Words): Fixed five-syllable phrases such as “ashibiki no” and “hisakata no”
  • Kakekotoba (Pivot Words) and Engo (Associated Words): One sound carrying two meanings, and words that echo one another
