Poem 69
嵐あらし吹ふく 三室みむろの山やまの 紅葉もみじ葉ばは
竜田たつたの川かわの 錦にしきなりけり
Author: Priest Nōin (988–death year unknown)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Autumn
English Verse Translation
The storms, which round Mount Mimuro Are wont to howl and scream, Have thickly scattered maple leaves Upon Tatsuta's stream; Like red brocade they seem.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
The storm blows through Mimuro Mountain, and the scattered maple leaves become a brocade on the Tatsuta River.
Commentary
This poem was composed for a poetry contest held at Emperor Go-Reizei's court, on the set topic of "autumn leaves."
About the Poet
Priest Nōin was originally a student of literature, but took Buddhist vows at the age of twenty-six. He had a deep passion for utamakura — place names celebrated in classical poetry — and composed many poems while traveling throughout the country, earning him the nickname "a man of refined taste." Many anecdotes survive about him. In one, he is said to have deliberately sunburned himself before presenting a poem featuring a distant utamakura, to make it seem as though he had composed it on a journey, when in fact he had written it in Kyoto all along. In another, he is said to have dedicated a rain-prayer poem at a shrine on behalf of drought-stricken farmers, and brought rain for three days and three nights.

The Poet's Feelings (A Personal Interpretation)

After the storm has passed,
the river is scattered with autumn leaves like brocade.

Even in the beauty of their falling,
I find my heart completely captivated.
