Poem 97
来こぬ人ひとを 松帆まつほの浦うらの 夕ゆうなぎに
焼やくやもしほの 身みもこがれつつ
Author: Acting Middle Counselor Sadaie (1162–1241)
Source: Shin Chokusen Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
Upon the shore of Matsu-hō For thee I pine and sigh; Though calm and cool the evening air, These salt-pans caked and dry Are not more parched than I!
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Waiting for someone who will not come, at the quiet evening shore — like salt burned from the seaweed, I too am consumed by longing.
Commentary
The poem borrows from a long poem in the Man'yōshū, in which a man in Akashi loves a young fisher girl at Matsuho Bay across the sea. Sadaie turns it around: here it is the girl at Matsuho Bay who waits, burning with longing, for her lover.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Sadaie compiled the Hyakunin Isshu and was a compiler of the Shin Kokin Wakashū, a giant of waka. He also studied the classics, copying The Tale of Genji, the Sarashina Diary, and The Tales of Ise, and wrote a commentary on the Kokin Wakashū. Many classics survive today thanks to him.

Fujiwara no Sadaie's father was Fujiwara no Toshinari (Poem 83 of the Hyakunin Isshu). Toshinari was also a great master of waka and taught poetry to Retired Emperor Go-Toba, Princess Shikishi, Ietaka, and others. Sadaie and his father Toshinari belonged to a famous family of poets known as the Mikohidari family.

When Sadaie was about 74, Utsunomiya Yoritsuna, the father of his son's wife, asked him to choose and copy out one hundred poems to decorate the sliding doors of his country villa, and Sadaie is said to have compiled the Hyakunin Shūka. The Hyakunin Shūka became the original version of the Hyakunin Isshu.

In English

At Matsuho Bay on Awaji Island,
which can be seen from the harbor of Nakisumi,
I hear there is a fisher girl
who cuts seaweed in the morning calm
and burns seaweed for salt in the evening calm.
But I have no way to go and see her,
and with no manly courage,
my heart gives way like a frail woman's;
wandering, I can only long for her,
for I have neither boat nor oar.

The Poem That Made Go-Toba Notice His Talent
Retired Emperor Go-Toba had poets each present a hundred poems in a project called the Shōji Shodo Hyakushu (the First Hundred-Poem Sequences of the Shōji Era), and Sadaie presented a hundred poems as well. The next poem is one of them and is also in the Shin Kokin Wakashū. It is said that Go-Toba discovered Sadaie's talent at this time and thought highly of him.

In Japanese

駒こまとめて 袖そでうちはらふ かげもなし
佐野さののわたりの 雪ゆきの夕ゆう暮ぐれ komatomete sodeuchiharafu kagemonashi
sanonowatarino yukinoyuugure Shin Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Sadaie

In English

There is no shelter where I can stop my horse and brush the snow from my sleeves: a snowy evening at the Sano ferry crossing.
The Poem That Angered Go-Toba
Retired Emperor Go-Toba recognized Sadaie's talent, but once, because Sadaie wrote the next poem, he angered Go-Toba and was ordered to stay at home in disgrace. The poem was based on a poem by Sugawara no Michizane, and it expresses the frustration of Sadaie, a man eager for promotion, at not being promoted. Here it is together with the poem by Michizane that Sadaie based it on.

In Japanese

道みちのべの 野原のはらの柳やなぎ したもえぬ
あはれ歎なげきの 煙けぶりくらべに michinobeno noharanoyanagi shitamoenu
aharenagekino keburikurabeni Shūi Gusō, Fujiwara no Sadaie

In English

By the roadside, under the willows of the field, the grass is sprouting,
as if competing with the smoke of grief smoldering in my heart.

Shūi Gusō is Sadaie's personal poetry collection.

This is the poem by Sugawara no Michizane that Sadaie based his poem on. Michizane wrote it when he arrived in Tsukushi (today's Fukuoka Prefecture), after being sent away because of Fujiwara no Tokihira's false accusations. There are several theories about why Go-Toba ordered Sadaie into disgrace, but if Sadaie wrote his poem based on this poem full of Michizane's bitterness, it is no wonder Go-Toba was angry.

In Japanese

夕ゆうされば 野のにも山やまにも 立たつ煙けぶり
なげきよりこそ 燃もえまさりけれ yuusareba nonimoyamanimo tatsukeburi
nagekiyorikoso moemasarikere Ōkagami, Sugawara no Michizane

In English

When evening comes, smoke rises over the fields and the mountains. It burns all the more fiercely, fed by the wood of my grief (nageki, which also means "throwing on wood").
A Famous Poem with the World of The Tale of Genji
The next poem includes "Yume no Ukihashi" (The Floating Bridge of Dreams), the name of the last chapter of The Tale of Genji. It calls to mind the dawn sky seen after lovers part, and leaves a lingering feeling like the ending of a story. It is famous as one of Sadaie's masterpieces and is in the Shin Kokin Wakashū.

In Japanese

春はるの夜よの 夢ゆめの浮橋うきはし とだえして
峰みねに別わかるる 横雲よこぐもの空そら harunoyono yumenoukihashi todaeshite
mineniwakaruru yokogumonosora Shin Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Sadaie

In English

When the fleeting spring night's dream, like a floating bridge, broke off, what I saw was the dawn sky, where trailing clouds were pulling away from the mountain peak.
A Poem About the Autumn Leaves of Mount Ogura
The next poem was written at the Ogura mountain villa, where Sadaie is said to have compiled the Hyakunin Isshu. Because of this poem, the villa also came to be called "Shigure-tei" (the Pavilion of Autumn Showers).

In Japanese

小倉山おぐらやま しぐるるころの 朝あさな朝あさな
昨日きのうはうすき 四方しほうのもみぢ葉ば ogurayama shigururukorono asanaasana
kinouhausuki shihounomomijiba Shūi Gusō, Fujiwara no Sadaie

In English

In the season of autumn showers on Mount Ogura, the leaves all around grow deeper in color day by day, and each morning I realize that yesterday's colors were paler than today's.
Ogura Shikishi (Poem Cards)

The Ogura shikishi are the poem cards on which Fujiwara no Sadaie, at the request of Utsunomiya Yoritsuna, chose one hundred poems and wrote them out himself.
This card has the poem by Semimaru.

Sadaie's distinctive handwriting became popular in the Edo period. It is still popular today, and Adobe offers a font called "Kazuraki SP2N," inspired by his handwriting.

Meigetsuki

The Meigetsuki is Fujiwara no Sadaie's diary. Sadaie himself called it "Guki" (my foolish record), but later generations named it Meigetsuki. It is kept by the Reizei family, Sadaie's descendants.

Sadaie kept the diary almost every day from age 19 until about age 74. He collected a wide range of knowledge, not only about poetry, and he also recorded astronomical events.

For example, he recorded in detail a "supernova explosion" (today's Crab Nebula) that happened in 1054, before he was born, noting that at its brightest it could be seen even in the daytime, and that it stayed visible for about two years after it appeared. Such records are valuable for astronomy, and in 2019 the Astronomical Society of Japan named the Meigetsuki one of the first "Japan Astronomical Heritage" items.

In addition, in 2000 the Meigetsuki was designated a National Treasure.

Teikakazura (Asian Star Jasmine)

Teikakazura is an evergreen climbing shrub of the dogbane family. It is named after a legend that Fujiwara no Sadaie (whose name can also be read "Teika") loved Princess Shikishi deeply, and after her death became this plant and wound itself around her grave.

An Ukiyo-e Print by Katsushika Hokusai

This is from Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki (The Hyakunin Isshu Explained by the Nurse) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The series was made so that a nurse could explain the poems of the Hyakunin Isshu simply, with pictures. People boiling down salt water, people carrying seawater in buckets, people piling up bundles of straw: Hokusai shows people making moshio (salt from seaweed). Just looking at the picture, you can feel how hard the real salt makers worked, but the nobles seem to have found it elegant. The Minister of the Left of Kawara even built a salt kiln at his own mansion and had seawater carried all the way from the coast to make salt for fun.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

An ukiyo-e print by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period.
In the Edo period, Sadaie's Hyakunin Isshu poem seems to have been known as a charm to bring back a lost cat.

Instead of the poem's content, the print shows a family giving dried bonito flakes to a cat that has come home.

Enri-an (Shigure-tei)

A temple in Ukyō Ward, Kyoto. Fujiwara no Sadaie's mountain villa is said to have stood here. However, there are several theories about the site of Shigure-tei, and it is also said to have been at Jōjakkō-ji or Nison-in. See it on the map

How to Read the Name 定家
The name 定家 is read both "Sadaie" and "Teika." Both readings are correct.
Reading a name in the Chinese-style way, like "Teika," is called yūsoku-yomi.
