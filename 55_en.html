Poem 55
滝たきの音おとは たえて久ひさしく なりぬれど
名なこそ流ながれて なほお聞きこえけれ
Author: Upper Counselor Kintō (966–1041)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
This waterfall's melodious voice Was famed both far and near; Although it long has ceased to flow, Yet still with memory's ear Its gentle splash I hear.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
The sound of the waterfall has long since faded, but its name flows on and is still heard by the world.
Commentary
This poem was composed at the ruins of the waterfall pavilion in the garden of Daikaku-ji Temple. Daikaku-ji had originally been Emperor Saga's villa, and its garden once had a beautiful waterfall. By the time Kintō visited, the waterfall had long since ceased flowing, but he wrote that its fame still lived on, widely spoken of even then.
About the Poet
Upper Counselor Kintō, Fujiwara no Kintō, was a man of such broad learning that he was praised as possessing the "talent of the three boats" — mastery of Chinese poetry, waka, and music all at once. Yet he also had a careless side: when his elder sister, Fujiwara no Junshi, became Emperor En'yū's empress, he thoughtlessly needled another of the emperor's consorts, Fujiwara no Senshi, asking her, "And when will this lady become empress?" — earning himself lasting resentment. In his later years, dissatisfied with his own rank, he withdrew from political life and chose a life of seclusion.

The Poet's Feelings (A Personal Interpretation)

The sound of the waterfall
can no longer be heard anywhere.

And yet, in the stillness,
its echo from long ago still remains.
