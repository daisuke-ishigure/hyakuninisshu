Poem 93
世よの中なかは つねにもがもな 渚なぎさこぐ
あまの小舟こぶねの 綱手つなでかなしも
Author: Minister of the Right of Kamakura (1192–1219)
Source: Shin Chokusen Wakashū, Travel
English Verse Translation
I love to watch the fishing-boats Returning to the bay, The crew, all straining at the oars, And coiling ropes away; For busy folk are they.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I wish the world could stay just as it is forever. How moving it is to watch the small fishing boats being towed along the shore.
Commentary
Minamoto no Sanetomo, the third Kamakura shogun, wrote this around age 20 while looking at a beach near Kamakura. In an age that felt how fleeting life is, it shows a ruler's fond care for the everyday lives of ordinary people.
About the Poet
Sanetomo was the son of Minamoto no Yoritomo, founder of the Kamakura shogunate, and Hōjō Masako. He followed his brother Yoriie as shogun at only 12. Though he led the warriors, he loved waka and wished for peace. At 28 he was killed by his nephew Kugyō, probably caught up in a power struggle.

Minamoto no Sanetomo was the fourth son of Minamoto no Yoritomo, the first shogun of the Kamakura shogunate, and the second son of his mother, Hōjō Masako. Because his elder brother Yoriie was driven out in a power struggle among the shogun's vassals (gokenin), Sanetomo became shogun at only 12. But real power was held by his grandfather Hōjō Tokimasa, his mother Hōjō Masako, and her younger brother Hōjō Yoshitoki.

At the same time, Sanetomo built ties with Retired Emperor Go-Toba and became the first warrior to be appointed Minister of the Right. He was also interested in court culture and loved waka so much that he held poetry gatherings at the shogun's residence, and he studied waka under Fujiwara no Sadaie.

But at 28, while visiting Tsurugaoka Hachimangū Shrine, he was assassinated by his nephew Kugyō. His life, lived amid struggles for power, was full of upheaval, yet his poem "Yo no naka wa tsune ni mo ga mo na..." carries his wish for peace.

Appendix
A Poem Pledging Loyalty to Retired Emperor Go-Toba
In the next poem, Sanetomo pledged his loyalty to Retired Emperor Go-Toba. Though he was the third shogun of the Kamakura shogunate, Sanetomo had close ties with the imperial court. This poem is at the end of the Kinkai Wakashū.

In Japanese

山やまは裂さけ 海うみは褪あせなむ 世よなりとも
君きみにふた心こころ わがあらめやも yamahasake umihaasenamu yonaritomo
kiminifutakokoro wagaarameyamo Kinkai Wakashū, Minamoto no Sanetomo

In English

Even if the world should come to a time when the mountains split and the seas dry up,
could I ever betray you?
No, never.
The Poem Monument in Kamakura Seaside Park

Below Kamakura Seaside Park stretches Yuigahama Beach. Its beautiful sands are known as a swimming beach that is crowded with people in summer. In the park stands a monument with Sanetomo's poem, shaped like a boat.

According to the Azuma Kagami, Sanetomo planned to sail to Song China himself and tried to have a Chinese-style ship built. On this calm beach, Sanetomo watched fishermen pulling their boats and wished for lasting peace. His feelings still seem to watch gently over this place.

From Hase Station on the Enoshima Electric Railway, walk south for about 10 minutes to reach Yuigahama Beach. From there, walk to the right along the beach for about 5 minutes to reach Kamakura Seaside Park.
(Photos taken February 22, 2025)

Tsurugaoka Hachimangū Shrine

Minamoto no Sanetomo, the third shogun of the Kamakura shogunate, was appointed Minister of the Right at 28. He visited Tsurugaoka Hachimangū Shrine to report it, and as he was coming down the great stone steps, he was attacked and killed by Kugyō, his nephew and also his adopted son.

Kugyō was the son of Minamoto no Yoriie, who had been driven from the post of shogun. He killed Sanetomo to avenge his father, but was himself killed soon after, and so the Minamoto line of shoguns ended after three generations.

Near the Kamakura Museum of National Treasures in the shrine grounds stands a monument with Sanetomo's poem:

山はさけ うみはあせなむ 世なりとも
君にふた心 わがあらめやも

This poem is widely known as a pledge of loyalty to Retired Emperor Go-Toba.

Tsurugaoka Hachimangū Shrine is about a 10-minute walk from Kamakura Station on the JR and Enoshima Electric Railway lines, but it is often crowded with tourists, so it is best to allow plenty of time.
