Poem 65
恨うらみわび ほさぬ袖そでだに あるものを
恋こいにくちなむん 名なこそをしけれ
Author: Sagami (c. 998–after 1061)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
Be not displeased, but pardon me, If still my tears o'erflow; My lover's gone, and my good name, Which once I valued so, I fear must also go.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
My sleeves are never dry — and I grieve not for myself, but that my name as a lover will be ruined before it is known.
Commentary
Written on the theme "love" for the palace poetry contest of 1051, it won. A woman who loved a cold man grieves that she has lost his love and that her name is now the subject of gossip.
About the Poet
Sagami's name comes from her husband, Ōe no Kinsuke, governor of Sagami, but they later parted. She then served Princess Shūshi, a daughter of Emperor Ichijō, and lived a life of free love. Highly praised as a poet, she has 40 poems in the Go Shūi Wakashū. She was the adopted daughter of Minamoto no Yorimitsu, who in legend slew the demon of Mount Ōe.

She became the wife of Ōe no Kinsuke, Governor of Sagami, and so her court name (nyōbō-na) was "Sagami."

But she parted from that husband after about four years, and is said to have begun a relationship with Fujiwara no Sadayori, who wrote the poem "Asaborake Uji no kawagiri taedae ni...."

Later, Sagami served Princess Shūshi, the first daughter of Emperor Ichijō.

Appendix
A Poem Written When Her Husband Forgot Her
The headnote to this poem says it was written "when Ōe no Kinsuke forgot me."

In Japanese

夕暮ゆうぐれは 待またれしものを 今いまはただ
行ゆくらむ方かたを 思おもひこそやれ yuugureha matareshimonoo imahatada
yukuramukatao omohikosoyare Go Shūi Wakashū, Sagami

In English

I always used to wait for you at dusk, but now all I can think of is where you must be going, and to whom.
A Poem Giving Up on Her Husband, Yet Missing Him
The husband who left Sagami went to his new post in Tōtōmi Province (today's western Shizuoka Prefecture) with another woman.
This poem expresses her mixed feelings for the husband who crossed the Ōsaka Barrier and went away: she has given up on him, yet still cannot let go.

In Japanese

逢坂おうさかの 関せきに心こころは かよはねど
見みし東路あづまぢは なをぞ恋こいしき ousakano sekinikokoroha kayohanedo
mishiazumajiha naozokoishiki Go Shūi Wakashū, Sagami

In English

My heart no longer reaches you as you cross the Ōsaka Barrier, but I still remember fondly the eastern road we once saw together.
Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e print is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period.
It shows a scene to go with the Hyakunin Isshu poem.

In this picture, a woman quietly gazes into the distance from the veranda. Lost in thought, she seems to reflect the feelings of Sagami, troubled by love.
