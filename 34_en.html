Poem 34
誰たれをかも 知しる人ひとにせむん 高砂たかさごの
松まつも昔むかしの 友ともならなくに
Author: Fujiwara no Okikaze (dates unknown)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
Gone are my old familiar friends, The men I used to know; Yet still on Takasago beach The same old pine trees-grow, That I knew long ago.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Who can I call a true friend now? Even the old pine of Takasago knew the friends of long ago no longer.
Commentary
With its evergreen needles, the pine has long stood for long life, and Takasago (Hyōgo Prefecture) was famous for pines. A long life means outliving the friends who understood you, and the poem speaks of that loneliness of old age. The poet is said to have written it at 74, when 40 already counted as old.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Okikaze held a low rank but was highly praised as a poet and left a personal collection, the Okikaze Shū. He was also known as a skilled koto player.

Fujiwara no Okikaze is the only poet from the Capital House (Kyōke) of the Fujiwara chosen for the Hyakunin Isshu. The Capital House was the line founded by Fujiwara no Maro, and it got its name because Maro served as Master of the Left Capital (Sakyō no Daibu).

Okikaze's ancestor, the Councillor Fujiwara no Hamanari, was caught up when the plot of his son-in-law Hikami no Kawatsugu to rebel against Emperor Kanmu (the Hikami no Kawatsugu Incident) came to light, and he was removed from his post as Councillor. The Capital House did not play a large part in politics.

Appendix
A Spring Poem
In this poem, Fujiwara no Okikaze asks the bush warbler, whose song tells of spring's arrival, to sing more.

In Japanese

声こえ絶たえず 鳴なけや鶯うぐいす ひと年とせに
再ふたたびとだに 来くべき春はるかは Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Okikaze

In English

Bush warbler, sing on without a pause.
Spring does not come even twice a year.

A Poem That Appeared in Hikaru Kimi e
This poem by Fujiwara no Okikaze, from the Kokin Wakashū, appeared in Episode 10, "The Moonlit Plot," of the NHK historical drama Hikaru Kimi e (Dear Radiant One).
Michinaga sends it to Mahiro (Murasaki Shikibu).

In Japanese

死しぬる命いのち 生いきもやすると こころみに
玉たまの緒おばかり 逢あはむと言いはなむ Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Okikaze

In English

My life is about to end. It might come back to life,
so just to try, please say you will meet me, even for a moment.

The Koto

The koto is a stringed instrument that came from China in the Nara period. You press the strings with your left hand and pluck them with your right to make sounds. There are several kinds: the 琴きん (kin) with 7 strings, the 和琴わごん (wagon) with 6 strings, and the 筝そう (sō) with 13 strings.
