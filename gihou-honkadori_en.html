Waka Techniques:
Honkadori (Allusive Variation)

Honkadori, literally “taking from a base poem,” is a technique in which a poet borrows a phrase or two from a famous older poem (the honka, or “base poem”) and builds a new poem around them. The older poem stays in the background, so the new poem carries two layers of meaning at once.

To modern readers this may look like copying, but it was the opposite. Educated readers knew the famous poems by heart, so the moment they heard the borrowed words, the old poem came to mind, and the pleasure lay in spotting what had been kept and what had been changed. Think of a jazz musician who quotes a well-known melody and then improvises on it, or of T. S. Eliot weaving lines from Shakespeare and Dante into The Waste Land: the reader is meant to recognize the source, and the new work gains depth from it.

The technique flourished most in the age of the Shin Kokin Wakashū (early 13th century). Fujiwara no Sadaie (Teika), who compiled the Hyakunin Isshu, set out guidelines for it in his books on poetry, Kindai Shūka and Eiga no Taigai:

  • Borrow only a little: no more than two of the five lines (ku), or two lines plus a few syllables if you move them to a different place in the poem.
  • Change the theme: if the base poem is about one of the seasons, write about love or another subject instead.
  • Borrow from the classics: not from poems by poets of your own day.

Some of the poems below were written long before Sadaie’s time, and even later poets did not always follow his guidelines to the letter.

In each diagram, the base poem is on the left and the new poem on the right, one line (ku) per row. Words taken from the base poem are highlighted, and lines connect the rows where they appear: a level line means the words stayed in the same place, and a slanting line means they were moved. Hyakunin Isshu Poems That Later Poets Borrowed From

Poem 1: Emperor Tenji → Izumi Shikibu

Base poem
By the rice fields, in a makeshift hut with a loosely woven roof, my sleeves are wet with the night dew.
New poem
The roof of my hut by the autumn rice field is loosely woven, so how could I sleep with the dew dripping through? (Shoku Gosen Wakashū)
What changed
In Emperor Tenji’s poem, the dew quietly soaks the speaker’s sleeves as the night goes on. Izumi Shikibu keeps the same hut and the same rough roof, but turns the ending into a frank complaint: with the dew dripping in, how could anyone sleep?

Poem 1: Emperor Tenji → Master of the Left Capital Akisuke

Base poem
By the rice fields, in a makeshift hut with a loosely woven roof, my sleeves are wet with the night dew.
New poem
The farmer who builds a hut by the autumn rice field has a rough, loosely woven roof, so he will keep watch all night long, together with the moonlight shining in. (Shin Kokin Wakashū)
What changed
Akisuke (Poem 79) replaces the dew with moonlight, which leaks in through the gaps in the roof. The word mori can be heard two ways: moru, “to leak in” (the moonlight), and moru, “to keep watch” (the farmer). And while Emperor Tenji speaks as “I,” Akisuke pictures the farmer from outside: “he will keep watch.”

Poem 2: Empress Jitō → Fujiwara no Yoshitsune

Base poem
Spring has passed and summer seems to have come — white robes are said to be hung out to dry on the heavenly Mount Kagu.
New poem
On heavenly Mount Kagu, where white robes are said to be hung out to dry in summer, the clouds have now cleared, and the light of the snow shines like those white robes. (Akishino Gesseishū)
What changed
Yoshitsune (Poem 91) keeps the whole second half of the empress’s poem but moves it from summer to winter: the “white robes” are now the snow shining on Mount Kagu after the clouds clear. By borrowing a summer poem for a winter scene, he may have wanted to give his poem a feeling that goes beyond time and season.

Poem 3: Kakinomoto no Hitomaro → Retired Emperor Go-Toba

Base poem
As long as the trailing tail feathers of the mountain bird — through these long, long nights, must I sleep alone?
New poem
Cherry blossoms bloom on the distant mountains. Even if I gaze at them all day long, a day as long as the drooping tail of the copper pheasant, I never tire of their color. (Shin Kokin Wakashū)
What changed
In Hitomaro’s poem, the long tail measures a long, lonely night. Go-Toba (Poem 99) uses the same image for a long spring day spent gazing at cherry blossoms without ever tiring of them. He wrote it for a folding screen celebrating Fujiwara no Toshinari’s 90th birthday, and it is thought to carry a wish for his teacher’s long life.

Poem 6: Middle Counselor Yakamochi → Mibu no Tadamine

Base poem
Looking up at the Milky Way that magpies wing across — its white shimmer seems to be scattered frost. The night deepens.
New poem
Treading at midnight through the frost on the steps of your hall, like the bridge the magpies make, I have come here to you on purpose. (Yamato Monogatari)
What changed
Yakamochi’s poem is a quiet look at the winter night sky. Tadamine borrowed it on the spot: when Minister of the Left Fujiwara no Tokihira suspiciously asked whether Tadamine’s drunk master had just stopped by on his way somewhere, Tadamine turned the magpie bridge into the steps of Tokihira’s hall and answered, “We came on purpose.” The Yamato Monogatari says his quick wit eased the mood.

Poem 12: High Priest Henjō → Princess Shikishi

Base poem
Heavenly wind, please close the path through the clouds! I want to keep the celestial maidens here a little longer.
New poem
On a winter night when the wind of heaven blows cold and clear as ice, the moonlight shines white on the maidens’ sleeves. (Shin Chokusen Wakashū)
What changed
Princess Shikishi keeps “wind of heaven” and “maidens.” Henjō compared the Gosechi dancers to heavenly maidens and asked the wind to keep them there a little longer. She moves the scene to a winter night, cold and clear as ice, where the moonlight shines on a maiden’s sleeves.

Poem 23: Ōe no Chisato → Kamo no Chōmei

Base poem
When I look at the moon, a thousand thoughts rise up and fill me with sadness. Yet this autumn is not mine alone.
New poem
As I gaze at the moon that fills me with a thousand thoughts, the lonely wind in the pines on the peak blows, as if for me alone. (Shin Kokin Wakashū)
What changed
Chōmei, the author of An Account of My Hut (Hōjōki), wrote on the theme “Wind in the Pines Before the Moon.” He keeps Chisato’s “a thousand thoughts” and “I alone,” moves the moon to the third line, and adds something new at the end: the wind in the pines on the peak, which seems to blow for him alone.

Poem 32: Harumichi no Tsuraki → Fujiwara no Sadaie

Base poem
The weir of fallen maple leaves, caught by the mountain wind across the stream — the water cannot flow through.
New poem
The wind has blown the leaves together into something like a weir, yet even so, the end of autumn flows on without stopping. (Shūi Gusō)
What changed
“A weir the wind has built” was Tsuraki’s own fresh image, which is why Sadaie (Poem 97), the compiler of the Hyakunin Isshu, was drawn to it. In Tsuraki’s poem the weir of leaves holds back the stream; in Sadaie’s, even such a weir cannot hold back the end of autumn.

Poem 35: Ki no Tsurayuki → Lady Murasaki (The Tale of Genji)

Base poem
Each time I see the lower leaves of the autumn bush clover changing color, I realize how your heart, though you are right before my eyes, has grown distant and changed completely. (Tsurayuki Shū)
New poem
I have watched love between men and women change before my eyes, and yet I trusted that you would stay with me far into the future. (The Tale of Genji)
What changed
This is not a Hyakunin Isshu poem, but it shows a poem by Ki no Tsurayuki, the poet of Poem 35, being borrowed in The Tale of Genji. Tsurayuki compares the turning leaves to a man’s change of heart. Lady Murasaki keeps “before my eyes” and writes of her sorrow when Hikaru Genji tells her he will take the Third Princess as his principal wife.

Poem 51: Fujiwara no Sanekata Ason → Izumi Shikibu

Base poem
I cannot even tell you how I feel — like the smoldering fire of ibuki wormwood, my hidden love quietly burns.
New poem
Will you say such cold things again today? Then I alone will keep burning, like the mugwort of Ibuki. (Shin Kokin Wakashū)
What changed
Sanekata’s poem pours out his overflowing love directly. Izumi Shikibu keeps the burning mugwort of Ibuki but writes with a generous heart, ready to keep the fire burning alone until the one she loves turns to her.

Poem 56: Izumi Shikibu → Attendant to Empress Inpu

Base poem
I may not be long for this world. As a memory to take beyond — let me meet you just one more time.
New poem
What shall I do? I want to meet you once more, even if only in a dream, and never wake from that sleep. (Shin Chokusen Wakashū)
What changed
Izumi Shikibu asks to meet her lover once more as a memory to take beyond this world. The Attendant to Empress Inpu (Poem 90) moves “once more” and “meeting” up to the second and third lines, keeps the wishful ending -mogana (“if only”), and adds a new twist: she would meet him in a dream and never wake.

Hyakunin Isshu Poems Built on an Older Poem

Poem 26: Fujiwara no Tadafusa → Teishin-kō

Base poem
O autumn leaves on the banks of Mount Yoshino, if you have a heart, change your color and wait for the rare imperial visit. (Kokin Rokujō)
New poem
O maple leaves of Ogura Mountain, if you have a heart — hold on a little longer, until the emperor’s visit.
What changed
Both poems speak to the autumn leaves, saying “if you have a heart,” and ask them to wait for an imperial visit. Teishin-kō moves the scene from Mount Yoshino to Mount Ogura. He wrote his poem after Retired Emperor Uda, moved by the leaves there, said he wanted his son, Emperor Daigo, to see them too.

Poem 28: Fujiwara no Okikaze → Minamoto no Muneyuki Ason

Base poem
When autumn comes, I find myself crying along with the insects, for I know that people will drift away and the grass and leaves will wither.
New poem
In a mountain village, loneliness deepens in winter. Not only people disappear — the grass too withers away.
What changed
Muneyuki takes “people,” “grass,” and karenu from Okikaze’s poem. Okikaze cries with the insects in autumn. Muneyuki moves the scene to a mountain village in winter, where visitors and grass alike are karenu. Here the word is a pivot word (kakekotoba) meaning both “drifted away” and “withered.” The sorrow of autumn becomes the loneliness of a winter village.

Poem 39: Kokin Wakashū → Councillor Hitoshi

Base poem
Like the low bamboo grass of the moor, I hide my love. But how would that person ever know, when no one is there to tell them? (Kokin Wakashū)
New poem
Though I try to hide it, my longing spills over. Why is it that I cannot stop thinking of that person?
What changed
Hitoshi keeps the opening, “the low bamboo grass of the moor,” which leads into the same sound in shinobu (to hide). The older poem says that person will never know of a hidden love. Hitoshi changes the ending: his love can no longer be hidden, and it spills over.

Poem 42: Kokin Wakashū → Kiyohara no Motosuke

Base poem
If I should ever give my heart to anyone but you, the waves would surely cross Sue-no-Matsuyama. (Kokin Wakashū)
New poem
We promised each other, wringing out our tear-soaked sleeves, that our love would last forever, never to be swept away like the waves beyond Suenomatsu.
What changed
Motosuke keeps Sue-no-Matsuyama and the image of waves crossing it. The hill was said to be safe from even a great tsunami, so waves crossing it stand for something that can never happen. The older poem makes this a vow of faithful love. Motosuke turns it into a reminder spoken to the other person: “We promised, didn’t we?”

Poem 90: Minamoto no Shigeyuki → Attendant to Empress Inpu

Base poem
Would even the sleeves of the fisherfolk who fish on the rocky shore of Ojima at Matsushima be as wet as my sleeves, soaked with tears? (Goshūi Wakashū)
New poem
I wish I could show you — these sleeves of mine, soaked through and through with tears that never change color, never dry.
What changed
Her poem is said to have been written as a reply to Shigeyuki’s. He said only the fisherfolk’s sleeves could be as wet as his; she answers that even those sleeves, soaked through, never changed color. The poem does not say it, but readers understood that her own sleeves had changed color, stained by “tears of blood,” which in poems of the time meant the deepest grief.

Poem 91: Kokin Wakashū → Gokyōgoku Regent and former Chancellor of the Realm

Base poem
Spreading only her own robe on a straw mat, is she waiting for me tonight too, lonely and alone, the Lady of Uji Bridge? (Kokin Wakashū)
New poem
On a cold, frosty night, the cricket cries. Lying alone with one sleeve folded beneath me — how lonely it is.
What changed
Yoshitsune borrowed in order to layer images on top of each other. In the Kokin Wakashū, the Lady of Uji Bridge was a symbol of “a beloved woman”; later, under the influence of The Tale of Genji, she took on the darker image of “a fragile figure wasting away alone.” His poem blends the classic elegance of the base poem with that loneliness.

Poem 92: Izumi Shikibu → Sanuki, attendant to retired Emperor Nijō

Base poem
My sleeves are like a stone under the water. Wet with the tears I shed in secret, they never have time to dry. (Izumi Shikibu-shū)
New poem
My sleeves are like a rock hidden beneath the tides — unseen by others, they are never dry.
What changed
Izumi Shikibu likens her tear-soaked sleeves to a stone under the water. Sanuki keeps the beginning and the end, and places the stone far out at sea, where it stays hidden even at low tide. Written on the theme “love compared to a stone,” her poem became so famous that she was called “Sanuki of the stone in the offing.”

Poem 93: Fufuki no Toji → Minister of the Right of Kamakura

Base poem
As no grass grows on the sacred rocks by the river, may you stay always as you are, a maiden forever. (Man’yōshū)
New poem
I wish the world could stay just as it is forever. How moving it is to watch the small fishing boats being towed along the shore.
What changed
Sanetomo keeps tsune ni mogamo na, “may it stay forever.” The older poem wishes that a young woman may always stay young. Sanetomo wishes the same for the whole world. The second half of his poem comes from another older poem, shown next.

Poem 93: Kokin Wakashū → Minister of the Right of Kamakura

Base poem
Michinoku has many fine places, but the sight of boats being towed along the bay at Shiogama is what moves me most. (Kokin Wakashū)
New poem
I wish the world could stay just as it is forever. How moving it is to watch the small fishing boats being towed along the shore.
What changed
Sanetomo keeps the last line, tsunade kanashimo, and turns “boats rowing in the bay” into “small fishing boats rowing along the shore.” The older poem is about the bay at Shiogama in the far north. Sanetomo is said to have written his while looking at a beach near Kamakura.

Poem 94: Sakanoue no Korenori → Councillor Masatsune

Base poem
Snow must be piling up on the Yoshino mountains, for the cold grows ever sharper here in the old capital. (Kokin Wakashū)
New poem
On an autumn night, the mountain wind of Yoshino blows cold. In the old village, I can hear the sound of someone beating cloth.
What changed
Masatsune keeps “the Yoshino mountains” and “cold in the old capital.” The older poem is about winter snow. He moves it to autumn and, on the theme of beating cloth, writes of the sound of a mallet striking cloth late at night.

Poem 95: Saichō → Former Senior High Priest Jien

Base poem
O Buddhas of supreme wisdom (anokutara sanmyaku sanbodai), grant your unseen blessing (myōga) to this mountain where I stand. (Shin Kokin Wakashū)
New poem
How bold it is of me — yet I spread my ink-black sleeves to shelter the people of this troubled world.
What changed
Saichō, the founder of the Tendai school, is said to have written his poem when he built the main hall of Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei, praying for the Buddhas’ blessing on “this mountain where I stand.” Jien, who headed the Tendai school at Enryaku-ji four times, takes up the founder’s words and turns them into a vow: to shelter the people of a troubled world with his black monk’s sleeves.

Poem 96: Ono no Komachi → Lay Novice and former Chancellor of the Realm

Base poem
How the beauty of the cherry blossoms faded away, soaked by the long spring rains. And just like them, my own looks have faded too, lost in a haze of endless longing. (Kokin Wakashū)
New poem
It is not the snow of blossoms scattered by the storm in the garden — it is I myself who am fading with the years.
What changed
Both poets look at cherry blossoms and think of growing old. Komachi sees her own looks in the fading flowers. The newer poem changes furu to furiyuku, which means both “falling” and “growing old,” and sets the blossoms falling like snow against the poet’s own aging.

Poem 97: Kasa no Kanamura → Acting Middle Counselor Sadaie

Base poem
At Matsuho Bay, in the morning calm they cut the seaweed, and in the evening calm they burn it to make salt. (Man’yōshū)
New poem
Waiting for someone who will not come, at the quiet evening shore — like salt burned from the seaweed, I too am consumed by longing.
What changed
The poem borrows from a long poem in the Man’yōshū, in which a man in Akashi loves a young fisher girl at Matsuho Bay across the sea. Sadaie turns it around: here it is the girl at Matsuho Bay who waits, burning with longing, for her lover.

Poem 98: Minamoto no Yoritsuna → Junior Second Rank Ietaka

Base poem
When the leaves of the oaks rustle on the summer mountain at dusk, it feels as if autumn has come again this year. (Goshūi Wakashū)
New poem
The breeze rustles through the leaves of the sacred oak trees by the river at dusk. The purification rite is the only sign that summer is here.
What changed
Ietaka keeps the oaks (nara), the rustling, and the dusk. The older poem feels autumn in a summer evening. Ietaka lays nara over the name of the Nara Stream and says that even with autumn in the air, the purification rite shows it is still summer. His poem has a second older poem behind it, shown next.

Poem 98: Yashiro no Ōkimi → Junior Second Rank Ietaka

Base poem
In the river breeze of the Nara Stream, where the purification is held, I keep on praying that what I feel in my heart will never end. (Kokin Rokujō)
New poem
The breeze rustles through the leaves of the sacred oak trees by the river at dusk. The purification rite is the only sign that summer is here.
What changed
Ietaka keeps the purification rite, the Nara Stream, and the wind. The older poem is a prayer made during the rite, asking that love may never end. Ietaka wrote his poem to go with a painting of the rite on a folding screen, and the rite becomes a sign of summer’s end.

Princess Shikishi (Poem 89): a poem based on The Tales of Ise

Base poem
Fireflies flying off, if you can go as high as the clouds, tell the wild geese that the autumn wind is blowing.
New poem
Are you going to tell the wild geese that the autumn wind is blowing? Fireflies, flying up so high in the evening that you almost reach the clouds. (Fūga Wakashū)
What changed
This is not Princess Shikishi’s Hyakunin Isshu poem but another of hers. She uses almost every key word of the base poem — fireflies, clouds, autumn wind, wild geese, telling — but turns their order upside down, so the lines in the diagram cross. The base poem asks the fireflies to carry a message to the geese; she watches the fireflies rise toward the clouds at dusk and wonders whether they are on their way to deliver it.

Other Waka Techniques

  • Makurakotoba (Pillow Words): Fixed five-syllable phrases such as “ashibiki no” and “hisakata no”
  • Kakekotoba (Pivot Words) and Engo (Associated Words): One sound carrying two meanings, and words that echo one another
  • Jokotoba (Preface Words): Scenes from nature that lead into the poet’s feelings
