Poem 2
春はる過すぎて 夏なつ来きにけらし 白妙しろたえの
衣ころもほすてふちょう 天あまの香具山かぐやま
Author: Empress Jitō (645–703)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Summer
English Verse Translation
The spring has gone, the summer's come, And I can just descry The peak of Ama-no-kagu, Where angels of the sky Spread their white robes to dry.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Spring has passed and summer seems to have come — white robes are said to be hung out to dry on the heavenly Mount Kagu.
Commentary
Mount Kagu is a low hill in present-day Kashihara, Nara Prefecture. From Fujiwara-kyō, where Empress Jitō ruled, it could be seen to the east. In early summer, white robes were hung out to dry on this mountain, a sight that told people summer had come.
About the Poet
Her personal name was Uno no Sarara, and she was a daughter of Emperor Tenji. In the Jinshin War, the largest civil war of ancient Japan, she fought beside her husband, Emperor Tenmu, and won. After he died and her son died young, she took the throne herself in her mid-40s, building Fujiwara-kyō and a system of written law as he had wished.

Empress Jitō was the daughter of Emperor Tenji (Poem 1).

After Emperor Tenji died, a fight over the throne — the Jinshin War — broke out in 672. Empress Jitō fought alongside her husband, Prince Ōama (Emperor Tenmu), against the court army led by Prince Ōtomo (Emperor Kōbun), and they won.

In 686, when Emperor Tenmu died, Prince Ōtsu was charged with plotting a rebellion and took his own life. There are different views about this. One says that Empress Jitō wanted her own son, Prince Kusakabe, to take the throne, so she removed Prince Ōtsu, who was well liked and could have become Prince Kusakabe's rival.
Prince Ōtsu's farewell poem is introduced below.

In 689, Prince Kusakabe fell ill and died at just 27. Empress Jitō wanted his son, Prince Karu (later Emperor Monmu), to take the throne, but Prince Karu was only 7 years old, so she took the throne herself.

In 697, when Emperor Monmu took the throne, Empress Jitō became the first retired sovereign (Daijō Tennō).

Appendix
Timeline of Empress Jitō
(Click to open or close.)

Empress Jitō is born

She was born to Emperor Tenji and Ochi no Iratsume. Her personal name was Uno no Sarara.

In the same year, her father, Emperor Tenji, killed Soga no Iruka together with Nakatomi no Kamatari and others in the Isshi Incident.

Soga no Ishikawa-maro takes his own life

Her grandfather on her mother's side, Soga no Ishikawa-maro, was falsely accused by his half-brother, Soga no Himuka, of plotting a rebellion, and took his own life.

Marries Prince Ōama (Emperor Tenmu)

At 13, she married Prince Ōama, Emperor Tenji's younger brother, who later became Emperor Tenmu.

Gives birth to Prince Kusakabe

She and Emperor Tenmu had a son, Prince Kusakabe.

Her father, Emperor Tenji, dies (age 27)

After Emperor Tenji died, she withdrew to Yoshino with Prince Ōama.

The Jinshin War breaks out

Learning that Prince Ōtomo was moving to attack her husband, Prince Ōama, she and her husband started the Jinshin War and defeated the court army led by Prince Ōtomo.

Her husband takes the throne as Emperor Tenmu

At Asuka Kiyomihara Palace, her husband, Prince Ōama, became Emperor Tenmu. One theory says the name "Kiyomihara" came from a wish for Emperor Tenmu to recover from an illness.

Her grandson Prince Karu is born

Her son, Prince Kusakabe, had a son, Prince Karu.

Her husband, Emperor Tenmu, dies

She held funeral rites called mogari for more than two years, using the time to prepare for her son Prince Kusakabe to take the throne. She ruled as the real head of the country without formally taking the throne, a form of rule called shōsei.

She drives Prince Ōtsu to take his own life

She had Prince Ōtsu arrested for plotting a rebellion and drove him to take his own life. Some say she was removing a rival to her son Prince Kusakabe; others say it was a hard but necessary political choice to keep the country from splitting apart.

April: Her son Prince Kusakabe dies

The cause of his death is not clear. Some say he was weak from birth; others say the forced death of Prince Ōtsu and the pressure of being heir to the throne put too much stress on him.

June: The Asuka Kiyomihara Code is put into effect

Japan's first organized code of law, begun by Emperor Tenmu, was put into effect by Empress Jitō. Under this code, the country's name was officially set as "Nihon" (Japan).

She takes the throne as Empress Jitō

The Kōin Nenjaku family register is made

Building on the Kōgo Nenjaku made by Emperor Tenji, she had the Kōin Nenjaku made — the first family register based on the ritsuryō law codes. It included a rule to remake the registers every six years, and this rule was actually put into practice.

The capital moves to Fujiwara-kyō

She led the move to Fujiwara-kyō that her husband, Emperor Tenmu, had planned. Eight years after his death, she finally completed Japan's first true planned capital.

Empress Jitō steps down

Emperor Monmu takes the throne

Her grandson Prince Karu became Emperor Monmu at age 15. Empress Jitō became the first retired sovereign and helped him rule.

Empress Jitō dies

As she had wished, she became the first emperor to be cremated. She was given the posthumous name "Takamanohara Hironohime no Sumeramikoto," which shows her as an heir of the sun goddess Amaterasu.

Her tomb is robbed

Fujiwara no Sadaie's diary, the Meigetsuki, records that Hinokuma no Ōuchi no Misasagi, the tomb of Emperor Tenmu and Empress Jitō, was robbed. The silver urn holding Empress Jitō's remains was stolen, and her remains were thrown away near the tomb.

The Original Poem
This poem is taken from the Shin Kokin Wakashū, but its original version is in the Man'yōshū, Japan's oldest poetry collection (Book 1, Poem 28). The original simply describes what the poet sees: "white robes are hung out to dry." The Hyakunin Isshu version changes this to something heard from others: "white robes are said to be hung out to dry."

Spring has passed, and summer seems to have come.

A Poem That Borrows Across Time and Seasons
This poem by Fujiwara no Yoshitsune (Kujō Yoshitsune) borrows from Empress Jitō's poem in the Hyakunin Isshu, a technique called honkadori. In the Hyakunin Isshu he appears as Poem 91, under the name Gokyōgoku Regent and Former Chancellor of the Realm. By borrowing a summer poem to describe a winter scene, Yoshitsune may have wanted to give his poem a feeling that goes beyond time and season.

In English

On heavenly Mount Kagu, where white robes are said to be hung out to dry in summer,

Who Was Forced to Take His Own Life
The next poem is by Prince Ōtsu, who was driven to take his own life — perhaps by Empress Jitō's scheming.
Did he leave this world feeling the brightness of life in the cries of the ducks?
This is the farewell poem of a tragic prince who was forced to die at just 24.

A Poem Empress Jitō Wrote When Her Husband,
Emperor Tenmu, Died
When Emperor Tenmu died, the palace was Asuka Kiyomihara Palace, in Asuka, Asuka Village, Nara Prefecture. Mount Kagu lies to the north of the palace, so the "northern mountain" in the poem is thought to mean Mount Kagu. Empress Jitō seems to have seen her husband's spirit in the blue clouds trailing over Mount Kagu.

The blue clouds trailing over the northern mountain are drifting far away,

In English

There are many mountains in Yamato,
but the finest of all is heavenly Mount Kagu.
When I climb to its top and look out over the land,

"Akizushima" is an old poetic name for Yamato (Japan).

A Poem About Yamato

The next poem was written by Yamato Takeru no Mikoto, thinking of Yamato (present-day Nara Prefecture). Empress Jitō, too, lived in an age when Yamato was the center of the country. The character 倭 can be read "Wa," but from early times it was also read "Yamato." 大和 is also read "Yamato," and in ancient times the name was also used for Japan as a whole.

The late 7th century, when Empress Jitō lived, was a time when a state run by written law was being built on the base of ancient Wa (Yamato). There are different views on how the country's name changed from "Wa" to "Nihon" (Japan), but some say the name "Nihon" was established under Emperor Tenmu and Empress Jitō.

In Japanese

大和やまとは 国くにの真秀まほろば 畳たたなづく青垣あおがき
山籠やまごもれる 大和やまとしうるはし yamatoha kuninomahoroba tatanazukuaogaki
yamagomoreru yamatoshiuruhashi Kojiki, Yamato Takeru no Mikoto

In English

Yamato is the finest place in the land. Wrapped in green mountains that rise layer upon layer like a fence, Yamato is beautiful.

The Jinshin War

The Jinshin War was the largest civil war of ancient Japan. It was a fight over the throne between Emperor Tenji's younger brother, Prince Ōama, and Emperor Tenji's son, Prince Ōtomo. When Emperor Tenji died, Prince Ōama felt his life was in danger. He became a monk and withdrew to Yoshino (present-day Yoshino Town, Nara Prefecture) with only a few followers. Meanwhile, in the capital, the Ōmi court led by Prince Ōtomo took control.

When Prince Ōama learned that Prince Ōtomo's side had begun gathering soldiers to attack him, he rose up against the court in the 6th month of 672. This was the start of the Jinshin War.

Prince Ōama's side had fewer soldiers, but he blocked the Fuwa road (in present-day Sekigahara Town, Gifu Prefecture) and gathered troops from eastern Japan, and this let him fight back. The plan worked, and his army surrounded the capital at Ōmi (present-day Shiga Prefecture) from the east and the west.

Cornered, Prince Ōtomo took his own life at Yamasaki (in present-day Yamashina Ward, Kyoto) on the 23rd day of the 7th month, and Prince Ōama won.

Empress Jitō was with Prince Ōama from the time he became a monk and went to Yoshino. She helped plan the strategy and contributed to the victory.

Akitsumikami: Empress Jitō as a Living God

Empress Jitō's enthronement was different from those before her. Until then, the support of the court officials had mattered a great deal, but Empress Jitō is said to have taken the throne as an akitsumikami — a living god who received her authority from the gods.

The Nihon Shoki describes the ceremony: a sword and a mirror were presented to her, the officials clapped their hands to worship her as they would a god, and Empress Jitō appeared from the Takamikura, the imperial throne.

This ceremony was handed down to later ages and became part of the 8th-century ritsuryō system. It is thought that by making the right to the throne clear in this way, the country built a system that made fights over the throne less likely.

Fujiwara-kyō and Fujiwara Palace

Fujiwara-kyō, built across present-day Kashihara City and Asuka Village in Nara Prefecture, was Japan's first true capital city planned on the Chinese model. It was also the first in Japan to use a grid of streets laid out like a go board (the jōbō system).

It was a grand capital where about 30,000 people are thought to have lived. But in 710, about 15 years after it was completed, the capital moved to Heijō-kyō. The area of Fujiwara-kyō became rice fields, and even its size was forgotten.

Its size only became clear in the 1990s. Many years of digging showed where the main buildings, such as the Great Hall of State (Daigokuden) and the State Halls (Chōdōin), stood and how they were built. This showed that Fujiwara Palace covered 1 km2, and that Fujiwara-kyō as a whole was a huge capital, about 5.2 km from east to west and 4.8 km from north to south.

The photo shows the site of the south gate of the Great Hall of State. Replica pillars mark where the gate stood. The hill-like mound in the back is the site of the Great Hall itself (the Ōmiya earthen platform).

(Photo taken August 15, 2025)

Kamokimi Shrine (Site of the Great Hall of State)

Kamokimi Shrine, which stands on the site of the Great Hall of State, honors Empress Jitō's throne. This land was originally home to the Kamo, an ancient powerful clan, and it is said that the Kamo clan offered it to Empress Jitō when Fujiwara Palace was built. "Kamokimi" in the shrine's name seems to have been a title showing high rank within the Kamo clan.

The Kamo clan is also known for having been in charge of the emperor's religious ceremonies. Their name lives on today, in changed forms, in places like the Kamo River in Kyoto and the Kamigamo and Shimogamo Shrines.

(Photo taken August 15, 2025)

The Three Mountains of Yamato and Fujiwara Palace

Mount Ama-no-Kagu (152 m), Mount Miminashi (140 m), and Mount Unebi (199 m) are called the Three Mountains of Yamato. They are in Kashihara City, Nara Prefecture, and Fujiwara Palace is surrounded by them.

Empress Jitō was given the posthumous name "Takamanohara Hironohime no Sumeramikoto," which shows her as an heir of the sun goddess Amaterasu. In myth, Mount Kagu was said to have come down from Takamanohara, the heavenly world. By building Fujiwara-kyō at the foot of this sacred mountain, she may have made her husband Emperor Tenmu's dream come true: bringing the world of myth to life on earth.

(Photo taken August 15, 2025)

A Poem by Emperor Tenji About
a Fight Over a Woman
The next poem was written by Emperor Tenji when he was still Prince Naka no Ōe. It is about the Three Mountains of Yamato: Kagu, Unebi, and Miminashi. There are different views on which mountains were male and which female, but in the age of myth, these mountains were said to be caught in a love triangle.

In Japanese

香具山かぐやまは 畝傍うねびををしと
耳梨みみなしと 相争あひあらそひき
神代かみよより かくにあるらし
古いにしへも 然しかにあれこそ
うつせみも 妻つまを 争あらそふらしき kaguyamaha unebiooshito miminashito ahiarasohiki
kamiyoyori kakuniarurashi inishihemo shikaniarekoso
utsusemimo tsumao arasofurashiki Man'yōshū, Prince Naka no Ōe

In English

Mount Kagu, not wanting to lose Mount Unebi, fought with Mount Miminashi.
It seems it has been this way since the age of the gods.
Because it was so in ancient times, people today also fight over wives.

Empress Jitō's Tomb

Empress Jitō died in 703 and was cremated about a year later — the first cremation of any emperor. Her ashes were placed in a silver urn and laid next to the coffin of her husband, Emperor Tenmu, who had already been buried.

However, according to records from the Kamakura period, the tomb was robbed in 1235, and Empress Jitō's silver urn was taken away. Because this robbery was recorded, we know for certain that she was cremated.

There are different views on why she was cremated. One says that she cared about her people and wanted a simple funeral. It is truly sad that the tomb of such an empress was robbed. The records tell of the people who robbed the tomb, but no one knows what became of the stolen silver urn. Its fate has been lost to history.

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

It shows workers carrying clothes, probably washed in the river, up the mountain. Taking wet clothes all the way up a mountain to dry is a lot of work. Mount Kagu was thought to be sacred, so this was probably for some religious ceremony, but the details are not known.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This ukiyo-e is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an artist of the Edo period. It shows a scene that matches the poem in the Hyakunin Isshu.

In the picture, Mount Kagu is a rugged peak with bare rock, but the real mountain looks very different. The real Mount Kagu is a low hill, only 152 m high, with gentle slopes.

Empress Jitō is wearing a splendid jūni-hitoe (twelve-layered robe). This is probably because the Hyakunin Isshu was loved as a symbol of Heian court culture, so the artist chose to dress her in the formal wear of the Heian period.

Ukiyo-e by Suzuki Harunobu

This work is by Suzuki Harunobu, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. It moves Empress Jitō's poem into the everyday life of Edo.

White summer clothes are hung out to dry, showing that summer has come in Edo. The water in the tub looks cloudy: at the time, people are said to have washed clothes with aku, an alkaline water made by mixing ashes into water.
