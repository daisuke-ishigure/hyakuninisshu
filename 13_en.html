Poem 13
筑波嶺つくばねの 峰みねより落おつる みなの川がわ
こひぞつもりて 淵ふちとなりぬる
Author: Retired Emperor Yōzei (869–949)
Source: Gosen Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
The Mina stream comes tumbling down From Mount Tsukuba's height; Strong as my love, it leaps into A pool as black as night With overwhelming might.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Just as the streams from the peak of Tsukuba flow down to fill the deep pools of the Mina River, so my love for you has gathered deep within me.
Commentary
The Gosen Wakashū says this was sent to the Princess of Tsuridono. Retired Emperor Yōzei wrote it to propose to Princess Suishi, a daughter of Emperor Kōkō, and they later married. Mount Tsukuba has a "woman peak" and a "man peak," so it often appears in love poems.
About the Poet
Yōzei became emperor at 9 and stepped down at 17 because of mental illness. Stories of violent acts survive, but the reasons for his stepping down are debated; one says Fujiwara no Mototsune arranged it. He then lived as a retired emperor for over 60 years, the longest in history.

Retired Emperor Yōzei became emperor at the age of 9, but he is said to have behaved strangely. The year after his foster brother (raised by the same nurse), Minamoto no Masaru, was killed inside the palace, the regent Fujiwara no Mototsune pressed him to give up the throne. Mototsune was the elder brother of Yōzei's mother, Fujiwara no Takaiko. Although they had the same mother, Mototsune and Takaiko were rivals in a struggle for power. So the reasons given for Yōzei's stepping down may have been made up.

After leaving the throne at 17, Yōzei married Princess Suishi. He lived 65 years as a retired emperor and died at the age of 80.

His son, Prince Motoyoshi, is known for his secret love affair with Fujiwara no Hōshi, a daughter of Mototsune's son Tokihira and a consort of Emperor Uda.

Appendix
Mount Tsukuba

An 877-meter mountain at the northern end of Tsukuba City, Ibaraki Prefecture. It has two peaks: Nantaisan, the "Male Peak" (871 m), on the west, and Nyotaisan, the "Female Peak" (877 m), on the east. Mount Tsukuba was known as a place for utagaki (or kagai), gatherings where unmarried young men and women sang love poems to each other and danced, and many love poems were written about it. The tradition seems to live on today: events such as hikes and treks for people looking for a marriage partner are sometimes held there.

The Minano River

Today the Minano River is written 男女川, "Male-and-Female River," but long ago it was sometimes written 水無川, "River without Water."

Mount Tsukuba was formed from magma that hardened deep underground and came to the surface over a very long time. Its rocks are full of cracks, so rainwater easily sinks into the ground, and probably little water flowed on the surface. This may be why it was written "River without Water."

We don't know how much Yōzei knew about the land around Mount Tsukuba. Still, the quiet nature of the water flowing down the mountain seems to live in his poem "Tsukubane no..."
