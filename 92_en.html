Poem 92
わが袖そでは 潮干しおひに見みえぬ 沖おきの石いしの
人ひとこそ知しらね かわくまもなし
Author: Sanuki, attendant to Retired Emperor Nijō (c. 1141–c. 1217)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
My sleeve is wet with floods of tears As here I sit and cry; 'Tis wetter than a low-tide rock,— No one, howe'er he try, Can find a spot that's dry!
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
My sleeves are like a rock hidden beneath the tides — unseen by others, they are never dry.
Commentary
Written on the theme "love compared to a stone," the poem became so famous that she was called "Sanuki of the stone in the offing." Her father, Minamoto no Yorimasa, had compared a tear-soaked pillow to a rock under the tide, and the poem also borrows from a poem by Izumi Shikibu.
About the Poet
Sanuki served Emperor Nijō and later Gishūmon-in, empress of Go-Toba. She was ranked with Princess Shikishi as a master of waka. In 1180 her father and brothers joined Prince Mochihito's revolt against the Taira and died, and she became a nun.

Sanuki served Emperor Nijō as a lady-in-waiting. After Emperor Nijō died, she is said to have married Fujiwara no Shigeyori (there are several theories) and had Shigemitsu and Ariyori, and later to have served Gishūmon-in (Kujō Taeko), the empress of Emperor Go-Toba.

Her father, Minamoto no Yorimasa, joined Prince Mochihito's uprising together with his sons, but they were defeated by the Taira; some died in battle and others killed themselves.

Because of this, Sanuki is said to have gone to live in Miyagawa Village, Onyū District (now the Miyagawa area of Obama City), which had been her father's land.

That place is Tagarasu in Fukui Prefecture, on the coast of Wakasa Bay. With little flat land there, it is famous for the Tagarasu terraced rice fields, cut into the hills. In one corner of the terraces stands a stone marking "the site of Lady Nijō-in Sanuki's residence."

It is said that Sanuki wrote her Hyakunin Isshu poem, "Waga sode wa shiohi ni mienu oki no ishi no...," while gazing from there at a rocky reef called "Oki no Ishi" (the stone in the offing), 2 km off the shore.

If this story is true, the "stone in the offing" in her poem was not under the sea, but a reef that is always being hit by waves and never has time to dry.

It is said that Sanuki may have been inspired by the next poem when she wrote her poem about the stone in the offing.

Minamoto no Yorimasa's Death Poem
Sanuki's father, Minamoto no Yorimasa, joined Prince Mochihito's uprising but was defeated by the Taira.
The next poem was written by Yorimasa just before he killed himself, cornered by the Taira.

In Japanese

埋木うもれぎの 花はな咲さくことも なかりしに
身みのなる果はてぞ 悲かなしかりける The Tale of the Heike, Minamoto no Yorimasa

In English

Like buried wood that never blooms, I, an old man, joined an uprising with no hope of winning, and how sad that it has ended like this, against my wishes.
The Slaying of the Nue

The nue is a monster with the head of a monkey, the body of a raccoon dog, the limbs of a tiger, and a snake for a tail. In the late Heian period, a nue appeared every night at the hour of the ox (around 2 a.m.) over the Seiryōden, the emperor's living quarters, and troubled the emperor with its eerie cries.

Sanuki's father, Minamoto no Yorimasa, a master archer, was ordered to get rid of it, and he is said to have shot and killed the nue. It is thought that the nue was really a scaly thrush (toratsugumi), a bird that calls at night in the breeding season.
