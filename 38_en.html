わすら

Poem 38
忘わすらるる 身みをば思おもはず 誓ちかひてし
人ひとの命いのちの 惜おしくもあるかな
Author: Ukon (dates unknown)
Source: Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
My broken heart I don't lament, To destiny I bow; But thou hast broken solemn oaths,— I pray the Gods may now Absolve thee from thy vow.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I do not grieve for myself, though I am forgotten. But I worry for you — you who swore an oath. What will become of you?
Commentary
A lover who swore by the gods to love her has left her, and she worries the gods will punish him. Some read it as deep love, but he may have been frightened. The Yamato Monogatari says he was Acting Middle Counselor Atsutada, who died young at 38. Divine punishment?
About the Poet
Ukon was a lady-in-waiting to Empress Onshi, wife of Emperor Daigo. Her name comes from her father's post in the Right Palace Guards (Ukon'e). The Yamato Monogatari tells of her loves with many men.

Ukon's name comes from the post of her father, Fujiwara no Suenawa, who was a Minor Captain of the Right Palace Guards (Ukon'e no Shōshō).

She served Empress Onshi, the consort of Emperor Daigo, as a lady-in-waiting, and was known as a poet who had many loves.

Her poem "Wasuraruru..." is said to have been sent to Fujiwara no Atsutada (Acting Middle Counselor Atsutada). It is not clear whether she truly meant to worry about him or to curse him for leaving her. Katsushika Hokusai made an interesting ukiyo-e print for Atsutada's Hyakunin Isshu poem. Please take a look.

Appendix
Sei Shōnagon's "Wasuraruru" Poem
Sei Shōnagon also wrote a poem that begins with "Wasuraruru." Its headnote says it was sent "to a man whose heart had changed," so it too was written to a man who had lost interest in her. Its feelings are easy for people today to understand.

In Japanese

忘わすらるる 身みのことわりと 知しりながら
思おもひあへぬは 涙なみだなりけり wasuraruru minokotowarito shirinagara
omohiahenuha namidanarikeri Shika Wakashū, Sei Shōnagon

In English

I understand why you have forgotten me,
and yet my tears will not stop.
Ukon's Poem in The Tale of Genji
Ukon's Hyakunin Isshu poem is used to great effect in The Tale of Genji, in a scene that shows how a man and a woman can fail to understand each other. Murasaki no Ue waited in the capital, trusting Hikaru Genji's promise, made as he left for Suma, that he would stay with her as long as he lived. But Genji had an affair with the Akashi Lady, who became pregnant, and then came back to the capital. Murasaki no Ue was deeply hurt. At that moment she murmured to Genji, "mi wo ba omowazu" ("I do not think of myself"), part of Ukon's poem. Behind these words was probably her sadness at being betrayed, but Genji took them as jealousy and found her charming. The quotation of Ukon's poem makes the gap between their hearts stand out strongly.
Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from Hokusai's One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse, made for the poem by Fujiwara no Atsutada, who is said to have been Ukon's lover.

The woman here is doing ushi no koku mairi, a kind of curse in which a person goes to a shrine at around 1 to 3 a.m. and nails a straw doll, standing for the hated person, to the shrine's sacred tree. She may be meant as Ukon, who wrote a poem worrying that the lover who swore eternal love might lose his life as punishment from the gods. Of course, this is not a confirmed historical fact.

In the Heian period, a woman who was not a man's principal wife, even if they were married, faced an unfair situation: the relationship simply ended when he stopped visiting her. By the way, ushi no koku mairi seems to have been done only by women.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi's Hyakunin Isshu no Uchi

This print is by Utagawa Kuniyoshi, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. It is one of a series that pictures the Hyakunin Isshu poems in his own way, and it shows Ukon, who shone in the court salons of the Heian period.

Ukon is called a gifted woman not just because she wrote good poems. As a lady-in-waiting to Empress Onshi, consort of Emperor Daigo, she had deep learning and a witty way with words that let her hold her own with leading cultural figures such as Ki no Tsurayuki.

Her intelligence shows best in her Hyakunin Isshu poem "Wasuraruru...." Rather than simply grieving over a betrayed love, she seems to have added a deliberate, ironic twist: "I am worried that you, who swore to the gods that you loved me, will be punished by them for breaking that promise."
