Poem 26
小倉山おぐらやま 峰みねのもみぢじ葉は 心こころあらば
今いまひとたびの みゆき待またなむん
Author: Teishin-kō (880–949)
Source: Shūi Wakashū, Autumn Miscellany
English Verse Translation
The maples of Mount Ogura, If they could understand, Would keep their brilliant leaves, until The Ruler of this land Pass with his royal band.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
O maple leaves of Ogura Mountain, if you have a heart — hold on a little longer, until the emperor's visit.
Commentary
Retired Emperor Uda was so moved by the autumn leaves of Mount Ogura that he said he wanted his son, Emperor Daigo, to see them too. Teishin-kō, who was with him, wrote this poem. It conveys the wish of father and son to share the sight.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Tadahira, youngest son of Fujiwara no Mototsune, served as regent and chief adviser and helped build the Fujiwara clan's power. He was gentle by nature and stayed friends with Sugawara no Michizane, whom his brother Tokihira had exiled. He was named Teishin-kō after death, and was Kentoku-kō's grandfather.

Teishin-kō (Fujiwara no Tadahira) was born into the Northern House of the Fujiwara. Fujiwara no Michinaga was his great-grandson.

When his elder brother, the Minister of the Left Fujiwara no Tokihira, died at 39, Tadahira came to lead the government. He also completed the Engi Kyakushiki (detailed rules for carrying out the law codes), which Tokihira had been compiling on Emperor Daigo's orders.

In the reign of Emperor Suzaku, Tadahira served as Regent and Kampaku (chief adviser). But the reign was far from peaceful: large revolts such as those of Fujiwara no Sumitomo and Taira no Masakado broke out one after another, and order in the provinces broke down badly.

His elder brother Tokihira is infamous for bringing down Sugawara no Michizane, but Tadahira, by contrast, is known as a gentle man. He kept in touch with Michizane even after Michizane was sent away, and stories of his kind nature have come down to us.

Tokihira's sudden death was said to be Michizane's curse, and Tokihira's son Yasutada is said to have lived in fear of Michizane's vengeful spirit. After Tokihira died, the main line of the Northern House of the Fujiwara passed to Tadahira, and his sons flourished: Saneyori as the Ononomiya line and Morosuke as the Kujō line.

Appendix
Cherry Blossoms That Bloomed in Ink-Black
The next poem is a poem of mourning written by Kamitsukeno no Mineo, a friend of the Chancellor Fujiwara no Mototsune, when Mototsune died. Bokusen-ji Temple in Fushimi Ward, Kyoto, takes its name from this poem. There is a strange legend that, after this poem was written, the cherry tree there bloomed in sumizome color (a pale gray, like ink), as if sharing the sorrow. Today the temple is a little-known spot for cherry blossoms. The name "Sumizome" is used not only for the temple but also for the nearby town and station, and the story is still lovingly told.

In Japanese

深草ふかくさの 野辺のべの桜さくらし 心こころあらば
今年ことしばかりは 墨染すみぞめに咲さけ Kokin Wakashū, Kamitsukeno no Mineo

In English

O cherry trees in the fields of Fukakusa, if you have a heart,
just this year, bloom in the gray of mourning.
Sadaie's Poem on the Autumn Leaves of Mount Ogura
Fujiwara no Sadaie also wrote about the autumn leaves of Mount Ogura. He wrote the next poem at his Ogura mountain villa, where he is thought to have compiled the Hyakunin Isshu.
Because of this poem, the villa also came to be called the Shigure-tei, "the Pavilion of Autumn Showers." The autumn leaves of Mount Ogura that Sadaie must have gazed at still charm many people today.

In Japanese

小倉山おぐらやま しぐるるころの 朝あさな朝あさな
昨日きのうはうすき 四方しほうのもみぢ葉ば Shūi Gusō (Sadaie's own collection), Fujiwara no Sadaie

In English

In the season of autumn showers on Mount Ogura, the autumn leaves all around grow deeper day by day, and each morning I notice that yesterday's colors were paler than today's.

Ogura-an (Sweet Bean Paste)

Ogura-an is a sweet bean paste made by mixing smooth bean paste with dainagon azuki (large red beans) simmered in sugar. Its name is said to come from an interesting chain of ideas. First, "Ogura" comes from Mount Ogura, famous for its autumn leaves, as in Teishin-kō's poem. Next, autumn leaves and deer have long been loved as a tasteful pair, as in Sarumaru Dayū's Hyakunin Isshu poem. The spotted pattern on a deer's back is called kanoko moyō ("fawn pattern"), and it calls to mind grains of sweet simmered beans, that is, dainagon azuki. Mixed into smooth bean paste, these beans make ogura-an. Mount Ogura → autumn leaves → deer → the fawn pattern → large red beans → ogura-an: this is said to be where the name comes from.

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

This picture shows what happens after the poem. In the poem, Retired Emperor Uda wishes to show the beautiful autumn leaves of Mount Ogura to his son, Emperor Daigo, and the picture shows Emperor Daigo actually coming to see them. On the right is a pine tree (matsu), which is said to carry the meaning that the autumn leaves' waiting (matsu) was worth it.
