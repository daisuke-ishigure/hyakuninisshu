Why Did Lower-Ranked Nobles Aim to Become Local Officials?

When you hear the term "local official" — or kokushi, provincial governor — you might picture someone banished from the glamorous life of the capital.
But the salary data from the period tells a very different story. The lower an official's court rank, the more they actually earned by working in the provinces rather than in the capital. Using salary comparison data by court rank and office for ritsuryō officials, this page explores why lower-ranked nobles were so strongly drawn to provincial governor posts.

What the Data Shows

Upper nobility (Senior 1st–Junior 3rd Rank)　At the ranks held by the highest nobility — Chancellor- and Minister-level officials — capital salaries overwhelmingly exceed those of local officials, averaging about 13 times higher. Nobles at this level rarely actually served as local officials, which is why almost no local-post data exists for these ranks. For the highest nobility, simply staying in the capital was the most profitable option.

Middle-ranking nobility (Senior 4th–Junior 5th Rank)　Here the picture starts to change. On average, Dazaifu officials (15,518 bundles) actually earned more than capital officials (10,350 bundles) — a posting to the Dazaifu, a key hub for diplomacy and military affairs, was a genuinely attractive option at this rank. Local officials outside the Dazaifu (6,215–6,063 bundles), on the other hand, earned less than capital officials, showing just how much pay could vary depending on the posting.

Sixth Rank and below (lower nobles and jigebito)　This trend is most pronounced at Sixth Rank and below. Capital officials at this level earned an average of just 281 bundles, while Dazaifu officials earned 1,166 bundles (about 4.1 times as much), and local officials outside the Dazaifu still earned 524–498 bundles (about 1.9 times as much). For officials of Sixth Rank and below — known as jigebito — a provincial posting paid far better than grinding away in a junior post in the capital.

What this data shows is that, for lower-ranked nobles, a posting as a local official — a kokushi — was a far more lucrative and attractive prospect than remaining a junior official in the capital.

In later periods, hands-on provincial governors came to be known as zuryō, well known for amassing wealth in the provinces they governed — and this underlying difference in salary structure likely explains why. For lower nobles with no real hope of advancement in the capital, becoming a kokushi was a genuine chance to change their fortunes.
