Poem 30
有明ありあけの つれなく見みえし 別わかれより
あかつきばかり 憂うきものはなし
Author: Mibu no Tadamine (c. 860–c. 920)
Source: Kokin Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
I hate the cold unfriendly moon, That shines at early morn; And nothing seems so sad and grey, When I am left forlorn, As day's returning dawn.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Ever since that cold dawn when we parted, I have come to dread the break of day more than anything in the world.
Commentary
The Kokin Wakashū places this among poems of "returning without meeting." A man visited a woman at night and left before dawn. Two readings exist: he regrets leaving because she was cold to him, or he finds the dawn moon "heartless" for ending the night as he grieves the parting.
About the Poet
Mibu no Tadamine was a low-ranking palace guard, but a gifted poet who often joined court poetry gatherings. He was skilled enough to be chosen as a compiler of the Kokin Wakashū. He was the father of Mibu no Tadami.

Mibu no Tadamine was one of the compilers of the Kokin Wakashū. He and his son, Mibu no Tadami, were both chosen for the Hyakunin Isshu.

Tadamine's son, Mibu no Tadami, competed against Taira no Kanemori at the Poetry Contest of the Imperial Palace in 960 (Tentoku 4) and lost, but both poems were good, and it is said to have been a close contest.

Tadamine served as a bodyguard of Fujiwara no Sadakuni. The Tales of Yamato, a collection of poem-tales, tells a story about it: one night Sadakuni, drunk, visited the Minister of the Left, Fujiwara no Tokihira, and surprised him, but when his bodyguard Tadamine recited a poem, the mood eased.

Appendix
High Praise from Sadaie
In Kenchū Mikkan, a commentary on the Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Sadaie, the compiler of the Hyakunin Isshu, praised the poem "Ariake no tsurenaku mieshi wakare yori..." highly: "If I could write even one poem as fine as this, it would be a memory to treasure all my life."
The Ariake Moon and the Phases of the Moon

The ariake no tsuki is the moon that is still in the sky after dawn. From about the 15th to the 29th day of the lunar month, the moon can be seen in the early-morning sky.

In the Heian period, only privileged people could have calendars. So for ordinary people, the changing shape of the moon probably served as a kind of natural calendar.

The Compilers of the Kokin Wakashū

The Kokin Wakashū was the first imperial anthology of waka, compiled at the order of Emperor Daigo. Four poets, Ki no Tsurayuki, Ki no Tomonori, Mibu no Tadamine, and Ōshikōchi no Mitsune, served as its compilers and gathered about 1,100 poems, including poems not in the Man'yōshū and poems of their own time. It was completed around 913, but Ki no Tomonori died before it was finished.

A Quick-Witted Poem That Borrows
from Ōtomo no Yakamochi
Mibu no Tadamine served as a bodyguard of Fujiwara no Sadakuni. One night Sadakuni, drunk, went to visit the Minister of the Left, Fujiwara no Tokihira. Surprised, Tokihira asked, "Did you drop by on your way somewhere else?" The Tales of Yamato says that Tadamine, the bodyguard, quickly recited the next poem and eased the mood. The poem is based on Ōtomo no Yakamochi's poem.

In Japanese

かささぎの 渡わたせる橋はしの 霜しもの上うえを
夜半よわにふみわけ ことさらにこそ The Tales of Yamato, Mibu no Tadamine

In English

Treading at midnight over the frost on the palace steps,
like the frost on the magpies' bridge, I have come here to you on purpose.

A Poem of Love at First Sight
Mibu no Tadamine attended the Kasuga Festival in Nara. From the window of his ox carriage, he glimpsed a woman who was watching the festival, fell in love, found her house, and sent her the next poem. Did he follow her ox carriage to find where she lived? Today this would be called stalking and would not be allowed, but in the Heian period it was common to fall in love with a woman seen by chance and send her poems like this.

Izumi Shikibu's Poem of Love at Dawn
Izumi Shikibu wrote this poem after spending the whole night waiting for a lover who never came.

In Japanese

夢ゆめにだに 見みで明あかしつる 暁あかつきの
恋こいこそ恋こいの かぎりなりけれ Shin Chokusen Wakashū, Izumi Shikibu

In English

The love of a dawn after a night spent thinking of you, without meeting you even in a dream: that was love at its very deepest.
