Waka Poems Featured in
Hikaru Kimi e

Here are the waka poems quoted in the 2024 NHK historical drama Hikaru Kimi e, which follows the life of Murasaki Shikibu (called Mahiro in the drama).

Episode 2: "Encounters"

A poem Mahiro (Murasaki Shikibu) was copying out. It was composed by her great-grandfather, Fujiwara no Kanesuke, and is quoted many times in The Tale of Genji.

In Japanese

人ひとの親おやの 心こころは闇やみに あらねども
子こを思おもふ道みちに まどひぬるかな hitonooyano kokorohayamini aranedomo
kooomofumichini madohinurukana Gosen Wakashū, Fujiwara no Kanesuke

In English

A parent's heart is not darkness itself, yet on the path of caring for a child, it wanders as if lost in the dark.

A poem Mahiro was writing while she ghostwrote love letters at a painter's workshop.
It is a poem by Hikaru Genji in the "Yūgao" (Evening Faces) chapter of The Tale of Genji.

In Japanese

寄よりてこそ それかとも見みめ たそかれに
ほのぼの見みつる 花はなの夕顔ゆふがほ yoritekoso sorekatomomime tasokareni
honobonomitsuru hananoyufugaho The Tale of Genji, Chapter 4, "Yūgao"

In English

Come closer and see whether it is so: the evening-face flower you glimpsed faintly in the twilight.

Episode 3: "The Mysterious Man"

This poem was quoted in a scene at the poetry-study gathering hosted by Minamoto no Tomoko, where a lady-in-waiting recited a poem from the Kokin Wakashū as if it were her own and was gently corrected by Akazome Emon.

In Japanese

見みてもまた またも見みまくの ほしければ
なるるを人ひとは 厭いとふべらなり mitemomata matamomimakuno hoshikereba
naruruohitoha itofuberanari Kokin Wakashū, author unknown

In English

Because I long to see you again even after we meet, you must be avoiding getting close to me.

This poem was quoted in a scene at the poetry-study gathering hosted by Minamoto no Tomoko, where Akazome Emon, said to know the whole Kokin Wakashū by heart, recited it as an example at Tomoko's urging.

In Japanese

秋あきの夜よも 名なのみなりけり 逢あふといへば
ことぞともなく 明あけぬるものを akinoyomo nanominarikeri afutoiheba
kotozotomonaku akenurumonoo Kokin Wakashū, Ono no Komachi

In English

The long autumn night was long in name only: when I met you, dawn came all too soon.

Episode 6: "Two Talented Women"

A poem Fujiwara no Michinaga sent to Mahiro (Murasaki Shikibu).
It adapts a poem from The Tales of Ise, changing "people of the court" (ōmiyabito) to "the one I love" (koishiki hito).

In Japanese

ちはやぶる 神かみの斎垣いかきも 越こえぬべし
恋こいしき人ひとの 見みまくほしさに chihayaburu kaminoikakimo koenubeshi
koishikihitono mimakuhoshisani The Tales of Ise, Section 71

In English

I feel I could even cross the sacred fence of the gods, so much do I long to catch a glimpse of the one I love.

Episode 10: "A Plot on a Moonlit Night"

Fujiwara no Michinaga sent Mahiro (Murasaki Shikibu) the following poems, one at a time. As time goes on, you can feel from the poems how his love grows stronger.

In Japanese

思おもふには 忍しのぶることぞ 負まけにける
色いろには出いでじと 思おもひしものを omofuniha shinoburukotozo makenikeru
ironihaidejito omohishimonoo Kokin Wakashū, author unknown

In English

My heart that tried to hide my love has lost. I thought I would never let it show on my face.

In Japanese

死しぬる命いのち 生いきもやすると こころみに
玉たまの緒おばかり 逢あはむと言いはなむ shinuruinochi ikimoyasuruto kokoromini
tamanoobakari ahamutoihanamu Kokin Wakashū, Fujiwara no Okikaze

In English

My life is about to end. It might come back to life, so just try telling me you will meet me, even for a moment.

In Japanese

命いのちやは なにぞは露つゆの あだものを
逢あふにしかへば 惜おしからなくに inochiyaha nanizohatsuyuno adamonoo
afunishikaheba oshikaranakuni Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tomonori

In English

"Life": what is it, after all? Is it not as fleeting as the dew? If I can meet you, I would not regret losing it.

Episode 14: "Even After the Star Falls"

Just before his death, Fujiwara no Kaneie looked back on his life and recited a poem by Yasuko (the Mother of Michitsuna). This poem was chosen for the Hyakunin Isshu.

In Japanese

嘆なげきつつ ひとり寝ぬる夜よの 明あくる間まは
いかに久ひさしき ものとかは知しる nagekitsutsu hitorinuruyono akurumaha
ikanihisashiki monotokahashiru Shūi Wakashū, the Mother of Michitsuna

In English

Do you know how long a night is, spent sleeping alone and grieving that you do not come, until the dawn finally breaks?

Episode 17: "Change"

Just before his death, Fujiwara no Michitaka recited a poem by his wife Takako (the Mother of the Honorary Grand Minister) and confessed, "It was this poem that made me choose Takako." This poem was also chosen for the Hyakunin Isshu.

In Japanese

忘わすれじの ゆく末すえまでは かたければ
今日きょうを限かぎりの 命いのちともがな wasurejino yukusuemadeha katakereba
kefuokagirino inochitomogana Shin Kokin Wakashū, the Mother of the Honorary Grand Minister

In English

"I will never forget you" is a hard promise to keep, so I wish my life would end today, the day I heard those words.

Episode 23: "When the Snow Dances"

This poem was quoted, in a scene where Emperor Ichijō talks with Fujiwara no Yukinari, as a poem that Empress Teishi loved.

In Japanese

夢路ゆめぢにも 露つゆやおくらむ 夜よもすがら
かよへる袖そでの ひちて乾かわかぬ yumejinimo tsuyuyaokuramu yomosugara
kayoherusodeno hichitekawakanu Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tsurayuki

In English

Does dew settle even on the path of dreams that leads to you? My sleeves, crossing it all night long, are wet and never dry.

A poem Mahiro (Murasaki Shikibu) composed after coming to Echizen, longing for the capital.

In Japanese

ここにかく 日野ひのの杉すぎむら 埋うづむ雪ゆき
小塩をしほの松まつに 今日けふやまがへる kokonikaku hinonosugimura uzumuyuki
oshihonomatsuni kefuyamagaheru Murasaki Shikibu Shū

In English

Here in Echizen, the cedar groves of Mount Hino are all but buried in snow. Is snow piling up today on the pines of Mount Oshio in the capital, too?

Episode 24: "The Unforgettable One"

This poem was in a letter telling Mahiro (Murasaki Shikibu) that her friend Sawa had died.

In Japanese

行ゆきめぐり 逢あふを松浦まつらの 鏡かがみには
誰たれをかけつつ 祈いのるとか知しる yukimeguri afuomatsurano kagaminiha
tareokaketsutsu inorutokashiru Murasaki Shikibu Shū

In English

Do you know for whom I pray at the Kagami Shrine of Matsura, hoping that, after all our wanderings, we will meet again?

Episode 25: "Resolve"

Sei Shōnagon added the upper verse to a lower verse composed by Fujiwara no Kintō. She based her upper verse on a passage from Bai Juyi's poem "Snow in Southern Qin." See here for details.

In Japanese

空そら寒さむみ 花はなにまがへて 散ちる雪ゆきに
少すこし春はるある 心地ここちこそすれ sorasamumi hananimagahete chiruyukini
sukoshiharuaru kokochikososure The Pillow Book

In English

Because the sky is cold, the snow that scatters like falling blossoms gives me a slight feeling of spring.

Episode 26: "The Sacrificial Princess"

A poem by Fujiwara no Nobutaka, Mahiro's (Murasaki Shikibu's) husband, composed during a quarrel between the couple.

In Japanese

たけからぬ 人ひとかずなみは わきかへり
みはらの池いけに 立たてどかひなし takekaranu hitokazunamiha wakikaheri
miharanoikeni tatedokahinashi Murasaki Shikibu Shū

In English

For someone as ordinary as me, nothing great, there is no point in getting angry.

Episode 27: "A Life Bound by Fate"

When Akiko, daughter of Fujiwara no Michinaga, entered the court of Emperor Ichijō, a splendid folding screen was prepared as part of her trousseau. Michinaga asked the court nobles to compose poems to go with the pictures painted on the screen. This poem, by Fujiwara no Kintō, was added to the picture of "a place where wisteria blooms." It likens the wisteria, the symbol of the Fujiwara, to purple clouds, an auspicious sign, and wishes for the prosperity of Michinaga's line.

In Japanese

紫むらさきの 雲くもとぞ見みゆる 藤ふじの花はな
いかなる宿やどの しるしなるらむ murasakino kumotozomiyuru fujinohana
ikanaruyadono shirushinaruramu Shūi Wakashū, Fujiwara no Kintō

In English

The wisteria blooms in glory like purple clouds. What good fortune can it be foretelling for this house?

Episode 28: "One Emperor, Two Empresses"

A poem by Teishi, Emperor Ichijō's empress, expressing her gratitude to Sei Shōnagon, who always looked out for her.

In Japanese

みな人ひとの 花はなや蝶ちょうやと いそぐ日ひも
わが心こころをば 君きみぞ知しりける minahitono hanayachouyato isoguhimo
wagakokorooba kimizoshirikeru The Pillow Book, Fujiwara no Teishi

In English

Even on days when everyone hurries to chase flowers and butterflies, you alone understand my heart.

The death poem that Teishi, Emperor Ichijō's empress, sent to Emperor Ichijō. See here for details.

In Japanese

夜よもすがら 契ちぎりしことを 忘わすれずは
こひむん涙なみだの 色いろぞゆかしき yomosugara chigirishikotoo wasurezuha
kohinnamidano irozoyukashiki Goshūi Wakashū, Fujiwara no Teishi

In English

If you have not forgotten the promises you made me all through the night, I long to see the color of the tears you will shed in missing me.

Episode 30: "Words That Connect"

A poem by Ki no Tsurayuki that Mahiro discussed at the poetry-study gathering hosted by Toshiko, wife of Fujiwara no Kintō.

In Japanese

人ひとはいさ 心こころも知しらず ふるさとは
花はなぞ昔むかしの 香かに匂におひいける hitohaisa kokoromoshirazu furusatoha
hanazomukashino kaninihohikeru Kokin Wakashū, Ki no Tsurayuki

In English

People's hearts change, so I cannot know your feelings. But in this old village, the plum blossoms bloom as beautifully as ever and give off the same fragrance as before.

A poem Akane (Izumi Shikibu) composed on the spot when she heard cicadas singing.

In Japanese

声こえ聞きけば 暑あつさぞまさる 蝉せみの羽はの
薄うすき衣ころもは 身みに着きたれども koekikeba atsusazomasaru seminohano
usukikoromoha minikitaredomo Izumi Shikibu Shū

In English

When I hear the cicadas, I feel all the hotter, even though I am wearing a robe as thin as a cicada's wing.

A poem Mahiro (Murasaki Shikibu) found herself reciting when she was at a loss raising her child. It is by her great-grandfather, Fujiwara no Kanesuke.

In Japanese

人ひとの親おやの 心こころは闇やみに あらねども
子こを思おもふ道みちに まどひぬるかな hitonooyano kokorohayamini aranedomo
kooomofumichini madohinurukana Gosen Wakashū, Fujiwara no Kanesuke

In English

A parent's heart is not darkness itself, yet on the path of caring for a child, it wanders as if lost in the dark.

Episode 31: "Under the Moon"

Akane (Izumi Shikibu) said she could not feel the charm of The Pillow Book. Asked why by Mahiro, she answered that The Pillow Book is witty but lacks the warmth of human skin, and recited the following poem.

In Japanese

黒髪くろかみの 乱みだれも知しらず うち伏ふせば
まづかきやりし 人ひとぞ恋こいしき kurokamino midaremoshirazu uchifuseba
mazukakiyarishi hitozokoishiki Goshūi Wakashū, Izumi Shikibu

In English

Lying down in turmoil, not caring that my black hair is tangled, I long for the one who used to brush it aside for me.

Episode 35: "The Empress's Tears"

A poem by Akane (Izumi Shikibu), sunk in deep grief after losing Prince Atsumichi.

In Japanese

ものをのみ 乱みだれてぞ思おもふ 誰だれにかは
いまは嘆なげかむん むばたまの筋すじ monoonomi midaretezoomofu darenikaha
imahanagekan mubatamanosuji Izumi Shikibu Zokushū, Izumi Shikibu

In English

Lost in thought, my heart in disarray and my black hair in disarray too: to whom can I now pour out my grief?

This poem was composed by Fujiwara no Nobunori, Murasaki Shikibu's younger brother, when he was caught after slipping in to visit Saiin no Chūjō, a lady-in-waiting to the Kamo Priestess (Princess Senshi). It draws on the story of Emperor Tenji, who, while crown prince, had those who came before him give their names first. Moved by the poem, the Kamo Priestess forgave Nobunori.

In Japanese

神垣かみがきは 木きの丸まる殿どのに あらねども
名なのりをせねば 人ひととがめけり kamigakiha kinomarudononi aranedomo
nanorioseneba hitotogamekeri Konjaku Monogatarishū, Fujiwara no Nobunori

In English

The sacred fence of the shrine is not the Log Palace (Ki no Marudono), yet here, too, one is challenged for not giving one's name.

In the scene where Mahiro shows Michinaga The Tale of Genji, the story of Hikaru Genji's secret affair with Fujitsubo is introduced in Mahiro's narration. The narration is based on the following poems in the "Wakamurasaki" (Young Murasaki) chapter of The Tale of Genji.

In Japanese

見みてもまた 逢あふ夜よまれなる 夢ゆめのうちに
やがて紛まぎるる 我わが身みともがな mitemomata afuyomarenaru yumenouchini
yagatemagiruru wagamitomogana The Tale of Genji, Chapter 5, "Wakamurasaki," Hikaru Genji

In English (narration)

Even though we meet like this, there is no telling when we can meet again. I wish I could vanish within this dream, just as I am.

In Japanese

世語よがたりに 人ひとや伝つたへむ たぐひなく
憂うき身みを覚さめぬ 夢ゆめになしても yogatarini hitoyatsutahemu taguhinaku
ukimiosamenu yumeninashitemo The Tale of Genji, Chapter 5, "Wakamurasaki," Fujitsubo

In English (narration)

Will people not pass it on as gossip, even if I make this matchlessly unhappy life a dream from which I never wake?

Episode 36: "The Long-Awaited Day"

A poem Tō Shikibu (Murasaki Shikibu) composed while looking at the moon on the night Empress Akiko gave birth to a prince.

In Japanese

めづらしき 光ひかりさしそふ 盃さかずきは
もちながらこそ 千代ちよもめぐらめ mezurashiki hikarisashisofu sakazukiha
mochinagarakoso chiyomomegurame Goshūi Wakashū, Murasaki Shikibu

In English

This sake cup, lit by a wondrous new light, will pass from hand to hand and go round for a thousand ages, never waning, like the full moon.

In the drama, Tō Shikibu (Murasaki Shikibu) explained the meaning of the poem: "This cup, in which the new light of the prince has joined the moonlight of the Empress, will go round for a thousand ages, as splendid as tonight's full moon."

On the night of Prince Atsuhira's ika no gi (a ceremony held on the fiftieth day after birth, like a first-meal ceremony), Tō Shikibu (Murasaki Shikibu) was urged by Michinaga at the banquet to compose a poem, and she composed the following.

In Japanese

いかにいかが かぞへやるべき 八千歳やちとせの
あまり久ひさしき 君きみが御代みよをば ikaniikaga kazoheyarubeki yachitoseno
amarihisashiki kimigamiyooba The Diary of Murasaki Shikibu, Murasaki Shikibu

In English

How on earth could I count them: the years of the prince's reign, which will last as long as eight thousand ages?

In reply to Tō Shikibu's (Murasaki Shikibu's) poem, Michinaga composed the following.

In Japanese

あしたづの よはひしあらば 君きみが代よの
千歳ちとせの数かずも 数かぞへとりてむ ashitazuno yohahishiaraba kimigayono
chitosenokazumo kazohetoritemu The Diary of Murasaki Shikibu, Fujiwara no Michinaga

In English

If only I had the lifespan of a crane, I could count all thousand years of the prince's life.

Episode 37: "Ripples" — Travel Segment: Higashiōmi, Shiga Prefecture

In the segment introducing Higashiōmi City, once a place where the gromwell plant (murasaki) was grown, a poem from the age of the Man'yōshū was introduced. It is by Princess Nukata, a wife of Emperor Tenji. She is said to have first been the wife of Emperor Tenmu and even had a daughter with him, but Emperor Tenmu's elder brother, Emperor Tenji, fell in love with her, and she left Emperor Tenmu to become Emperor Tenji's wife. This poem is said to have been sent by her to Emperor Tenmu after she had become Emperor Tenji's wife.

In Japanese

あかねさす 紫野むらさきの行ゆき 標野しめの行ゆき
野守のもりは見みずや 君きみが袖そで振ふる akanesasu murasakinoyuki shimenoyuki
nomorihamizuya kimigasodefuru Man'yōshū, Princess Nukata

In English

You go through the fields of gromwell glowing madder-red, through the imperial preserve. Won't the field guard see you waving your sleeve to me?

Episode 38: "Dazzling Darkness" — Travel Segment: Kobe, Hyōgo Prefecture

For the crime of shooting arrows at the Cloistered Emperor Kazan in the Chōtoku Incident, Fujiwara no Korechika was exiled to Harima and Takaie to Tajima. Korechika expressed his feelings as the close brothers were torn apart in the following poem.

In Japanese

かたがたに 別わかるる身みにも 似にたるかな
明石あかしも須磨すまも おのが浦々うらうら katagatani wakaruruminimo nitarukana
akashimosumamo onogauraura Eiga Monogatari, Fujiwara no Korechika

In English

How like our fate, parted from each other: Akashi and Suma, each its own bay, so close and yet so far apart.

Korechika's poem above is thought to borrow from the following poem by Kakinomoto no Hitomaro (honkadori). "Tatsu" (to rise / to cut cloth) and "ura" (bay / lining) are pivot words (kakekotoba).

In Japanese

白浪しらなみは たてど衣ころもに かさならず
明石あかしも須磨すまも おのが浦々うらうら shiranamiha tatedokoromoni kasanarazu
akashimosumamo onogauraura Shūi Wakashū, Kakinomoto no Hitomaro

In English

Cloth, once cut, can be sewn so that its face and lining overlap, but white waves, though they rise, never overlap: Akashi and Suma are separate bays.

Episode 39: "Unbroken Bonds"

Fujiwara no Nobunori, Murasaki Shikibu's younger brother, fell ill with stomach pains on the journey to Echigo with his father, Tametoki, who had been appointed Governor of Echigo, and died there. A poem expressing his wish to recover and return to the capital became his death poem. It is said that Nobunori ran out of strength after writing "omo" of the last word, "omofu," and that his father Tametoki added the final "fu."

In Japanese

都みやこにも 恋こいしき人ひとの 多おおかれば
なほこのたびは いかむとぞ思おもふ miyakonimo koishikihitono ookareba
nahokonotabiha ikamutozoomofu Goshūi Wakashū, Fujiwara no Nobunori

In English

There are so many people I love in the capital, too, that I want to live and go back, whatever it takes.

Episode 39: "Unbroken Bonds" — Travel Segment: Ōtsu, Shiga Prefecture

Fujiwara no Nobunori wrote of how, on his way to Echigo, he had only just crossed the Ōsaka Barrier when he already began to miss the capital. The Ōsaka Barrier was like a gateway on the east side of the capital, so we can tell he composed this poem soon after setting out.

In Japanese

逢坂おうさかの 関せきうちこゆる ほどもなく
今朝けさは都みやこの 人ひとぞ恋こいしき ousakano sekiuchikoyuru hodomonaku
kesahamiyakono hitozokoishiki Goshūi Wakashū, Fujiwara no Nobunori

In English

I have barely crossed the Ōsaka Barrier, yet this morning I already miss you, back in the capital.

Episode 40: "Leaving You Behind"

Emperor Ichijō, who had stepped down and lay ill in bed, composed the following poem for Empress Akiko. He had shaved his head, so this is thought to have been after he became a monk. For the last line (the last seven syllables), Fujiwara no Yukinari's diary Gonki records "koto zo kanashiki," while Fujiwara no Michinaga's diary Midō Kanpakuki records "koto o koso omoe." It is not known which is correct, but the meaning is almost the same.

In Japanese

露つゆの身みの 風かぜの宿やどりに 君きみを置おきて
塵ちりを出いでぬる 事ことぞ悲かなしき tsuyunomino kazenoyadorini kimiookite
chirioidenuru kotozokanashiki Gonki, Emperor Ichijō

In English

How sad it is to leave you behind in this world, a passing shelter for a life as fleeting as the dew, and to leave this dust-filled world myself.

Episode 41: "Wavering"

After Emperor Ichijō died, Atsuhira, too young to understand his father's death, was innocently holding a pink (nadeshiko) flower. Seeing him like that, Akiko composed the following poem.

In Japanese

見みるままに 露つゆぞこぼるる おくれにし
心こころも知しらぬ 撫子なでしこの花はな mirumamani tsuyuzokoboruru okurenishi
kokoromoshiranu nadeshikonohana Goshūi Wakashū, Akiko (Shōshi)

In English

Each time I look at you, tears fall like dew: my little pink flower, holding a nadeshiko, not knowing you have been left behind.

Akiko held a poetry gathering at the Fujitsubo. There her ladies-in-waiting, Akazome Emon, Murasaki Shikibu, and Izumi Shikibu, presented their poems.

In Japanese

omofubakarini mimuhitomogana Akazome Emon Shū, Akazome Emon

In English

Whom should I tell? I wish someone were by my side who could enjoy these autumn leaves with me to my heart's content.

In Japanese

なにばかり 心こころづくしに ながめねど
見みしにくれぬる 秋あきの月影つきかげ nanibakari kokorozukushini nagamenedo
mishinikurenuru akinotsukikage Murasaki Shikibu Shū, Murasaki Shikibu

I was not gazing at the autumn moon with any special feeling, yet as I watched it, it blurred with my tears.

In Japanese

tsukinihamiyuru kokochikososure Izumi Shikibu Shū, Izumi Shikibu

Both the painful things and the things I long for seem to be reflected in the moon of the clear autumn night.

Episode 41: "Wavering" — Travel Segment: Kyoto, Kyoto Prefecture

Fujiwara no Kintō was a man of wide learning and many talents, gifted in all three arts of Chinese poetry, waka, and music. The historical tale Ōkagami tells this story: when Fujiwara no Michinaga held a boating party on the Ōi River, he prepared three boats and put people skilled in music, Chinese poetry, and waka in each. Kintō was accomplished in all three, so Michinaga asked him which boat he would ride. Kintō boarded the waka boat and composed the following poem, which was highly praised. Yet Kintō is said to have regretted that, had he boarded the Chinese poetry boat, his fame would have risen even higher, and the story shows how many talents he had.

In Japanese

小倉山おぐらやま 嵐あらしの風かぜの 寒さむければ
散ちるもみぢ葉ばを きぬ人ひとぞなき ogurayama arashinokazeno samukereba
chirumomijibao kinuhitozonaki Ōkagami, Fujiwara no Kintō

In English

The wind blowing down from Mount Ogura and Arashiyama is so cold that the falling autumn leaves land on everyone's robes: there is no one not clothed in a brocade of autumn leaves.

Episode 42: "A Vow by the River"

Fujiwara no Michinaga visited Murasaki Shikibu's room and consulted her about Emperor Sanjō not visiting Kenshi, Michinaga's daughter and Emperor Sanjō's empress. Michinaga said, "Between Emperor Ichijō and his empress Akiko there was The Tale of Genji, but between Emperor Sanjō and Kenshi there is nothing, and The Tale of Genji is of no use." After their conversation, Murasaki Shikibu quietly wrote down the following poem. It is the last poem Hikaru Genji composes in The Tale of Genji.

In Japanese

もの思おもふと 過すぐる月日つきひも 知しらぬ間まに
年としもわが世よも 今日きょうや尽つきぬる monoomofuto sugurutsukihimo shiranumani
toshimowagayomo kyouyatsukinuru The Tale of Genji, Murasaki Shikibu

In English

Lost in thought, I never noticed the months and days go by. Do both this year and my life end today?

Episode 44: "The Night of the Full Moon"

Fujiwara no Michinaga composed the following poem on the night Ishi was installed as empress (chūgū) of Emperor Go-Ichijō. For a long time this poem was often taken as a poem of a powerful man's arrogance, but recent research has offered a new interpretation: that "kono yo" ("this world") also means "this night," and that the poem likens his daughters who had become empresses to the moon while also playing on the sake cups held by the court nobles. According to this view, it is a poem that shows Michinaga's feelings as a father, calling on the nobles: "My daughters have all become empresses. Not one of you nobles sharing cups with me is missing. So let us all support my daughters together." See here for details.

In Japanese

この世よをば 我わが世よとぞ思おもふ 望月もちづきの
欠かけたることも なしと思おもへば konoyooba wagayotozoomofu mochizukino
kaketarukotomo nashitoomoheba From the Shōyūki

In English

This world, I think, is my world. Like the full moon with nothing missing, everything is complete.

Episode 48: "Beyond the Tale"

On the very day Fujiwara no Michinaga died, Fujiwara no Yukinari also passed away. Yukinari, together with Fujiwara no Kintō, Fujiwara no Tadanobu, and Minamoto no Toshikata, was called one of the "Four Counselors," companions who supported Michinaga. Having lost dear friends one after another, Kintō and Tadanobu exchanged the following poems.

In Japanese

見みし人ひとの 亡なくなりゆくを 聞きくままに
いとど深山みやまぞ 寂さびしかりける mishihitono nakunariyukuo kikumamani
itodomiyamazo sabishikarikeru From the Eiga Monogatari, Fujiwara no Kintō

In English

Each time I hear that someone I knew has died, I feel ever lonelier, as if I were deep in the mountains.

In Japanese

消きえ残のこる 頭かしらの雪ゆきを 払はらひつつ
寂さびしき山やまを 思おもひやるかな kienokoru kashiranoyukio harahitsutsu
sabishikiyamao omohiyarukana From the Eiga Monogatari, Fujiwara no Tadanobu

In English

Brushing off the snow that still remains on my head, my white hair, I think of the lonely mountain.

In the broadcast on the 15th, the "頭" in "kashira no yuki o" was pronounced "atama," but in the rerun on the 21st it was pronounced "kashira," so we corrected the furigana to match the drama.

Mahiro collected her own poems into the Murasaki Shikibu Shū and entrusted the precious collection to her daughter, Kenshi. Kenshi read out the first poem of the Murasaki Shikibu Shū. This poem was also chosen for the Hyakunin Isshu. See here for details.

In Japanese

めぐり逢あひいて 見みしやそれとも わかぬ間まに
雲隠くもがくれにし 夜半よわの月つきかな meguriahite mishiyasoretomo wakanumani
kumogakurenishi yohanotsukikana Shin Kokin Wakashū, Miscellaneous, Murasaki Shikibu (c. 970–c. 1014)

In English

Meeting you again after so long, before I could even tell whether it was really you, you were gone, like the midnight moon hidden behind the clouds.
