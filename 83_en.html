Poem 83
世よの中なかよ 道みちこそなけれ 思おもひい入いる
山やまの奥おくにも 鹿しかぞ鳴なくなる
Author: Fujiwara no Shunzei (1114–1204)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
From pain and sorrow all around There's no escape, I fear; To mountain wilds should I retreat, There also I should hear The cry of hunted deer.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
There is no escape from this world. Even deep in the mountains, the deer cry out.
Commentary
Shunzei composed this poem around the age of twenty-seven, at a time when the Heian period was drawing to its close. As many of his contemporaries were taking Buddhist vows, Shunzei too may have entered the mountains, contemplating renunciation of the world. But hearing the mournful cry of a stag deep in the mountains, he seems to have realized that there was, after all, no path by which to escape his troubles.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Shunzei, Master of the Empress Dowager's Household, was the father of Fujiwara no Teika, who compiled the Hyakunin Isshu, and is known as the compiler of the Senzai Wakashū. He served as judge in numerous poetry contests, and in his critiques frequently used the terms "yūgen" (mysterious depth) and "en" (elegant charm). His aesthetic sensibility left a lasting mark not only on waka poetry, but on Japanese culture more broadly, influencing the tea ceremony and Noh theater as well.

The Poet's Feelings (A Personal Interpretation)

Even deep in the quiet mountains,
this sorrow refuses to disappear.

The echoing cry of the deer
softly speaks of this world's fleeting nature.
