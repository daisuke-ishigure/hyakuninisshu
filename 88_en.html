Poem 88
難波江なにわえの 葦あしのかりねの ひとよゆゑえ
みをつくしてや 恋こひいわたるべき
Author: Attendant to Empress Kōka (dates unknown)
Source: Senzai Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
I've seen thee but a few short hours; As short, they seemed to me, As bamboo reeds at Naniwa; But tide-stakes in the sea Can't gauge my love for thee.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
For just one brief night together, like the cut end of a reed at Naniwa — must I go on longing for you forever?
Commentary
Written on the theme "love met at a traveler's inn" at a contest at Kujō Kanezane's home. Naniwa Bay was a busy port with many travelers and many courtesans, and the poet is thought to have had their lives in mind.
About the Poet
The poet served as steward (bettō) to Kōkamon-in (Fujiwara no Seishi), empress of Sutoku.

The poet served Kōkamon-in, the daughter of Fujiwara no Tadamichi.

Because Kōkamon-in's younger half-brother was Kujō Kanezane, the poet often took part in Kanezane's poetry gatherings and became well known as a poet.

Appendix
What Is "Ashi no Fushi"?

Ashi (also called yoshi) is the common reed, a plant of the grass family that grows by the water. Its stem is thin and has joints (fushi) like bamboo.
It sprouts in late April and grows very fast until about June, and its stem can reach 3 m.
Its underground stems spread sideways, so it grows in large clumps.

Fushi no ma, "the space between the joints," is used as an image of something very short.
Naniwa was famous for its reeds and appears in many poems.

When you look at a real reed, the space between the joints does not seem short. One theory says that the phrase stands for "a short time" because a reed grows so quickly, adding joint after joint to its stem.

Miotsukushi (Channel Markers)

Miotsukushi are posts set in shallow waters where ships might run aground, to show where the water is deep enough for ships to pass. At Naniwa, the mouth of the Yodo River spread out into many shallows that made sailing difficult, so the miotsukushi of Naniwa were famous.
The word comes from mio-tsu-kushi, "posts of the mio." Mio means the same as 水脈みお: the line where water flows in the sea or a river. In waka, miotsukushi is often used as a kakekotoba together with mi o tsukushi, "giving oneself completely."

Nobles and Courtesans
From the mid-Heian period to the early Kamakura period, popular songs of the day called imayō were a favorite among nobles. Some courtesans (yūjo) performed by singing imayō and were invited to nobles' mansions. Emperor Go-Shirakawa learned imayō from a courtesan named Otomae, and was such a keen supporter that he compiled the Ryōjin Hishō, a collection of imayō songs. It may seem surprising that the poet wrote a poem thinking of courtesans, but through imayō, nobles had contact with courtesans and felt close to them.
