Poem 3
あしびきの 山鳥やまどりの尾おの しだり尾おの
ながながし夜よを ひとりかも寝ねむん
Author: Kakinomoto no Hitomaro (dates unknown–c. 708)
Source: Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
Long is the mountain pheasant's tail That curves down in its flight; But longer still, it seems to me, Left in my lonely plight, Is this unending night.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
As long as the trailing tail feathers of the mountain bird — through these long, long nights, must I sleep alone?
Commentary
The poem compares a long, lonely night to the long, drooping tail of the copper pheasant. Pheasant couples were believed to spend the day together but sleep apart, on mountains across a valley. The poet seems to see his own lonely nights in this habit.
About the Poet
Kakinomoto no Hitomaro served at court, writing poems praising the emperor and poems of mourning. Honored with Yamabe no Akahito as one of the two "Sages of Poetry," he is worshipped as a god of waka. He is said to have died in Iwami Province (Shimane Prefecture), but whether he collapsed on the road or was executed is unknown.

Appendix
The Original Poem
Hitomaro's poem in the Hyakunin Isshu comes from the Shūi Wakashū, but its original is said to be a poem by an unknown author in the Man'yōshū (Book 11, Poem 2802). Over time it was reshaped and came to be known as Hitomaro's.

In Japanese

思おもへども 思おもひもかねつ あしひきの
山鳥やまどりの尾おの 長ながきこの夜よを Man'yōshū, author unknown

In English

However much I think of you, my longing never ends,
for this night is as long as the tail of the copper pheasant.

A Long Poem About the Copper Pheasant (Excerpt)
Copper pheasants were believed to spend the day together as a pair, but to sleep apart at night, on mountains across a valley. This idea appears in the Man'yōshū, Japan's oldest poetry collection, so it seems to have been well known at the time.

In Japanese

あしひきの 山鳥やまどりこそば 峰向おむかひに
妻問つまどひすと言いへ うつせみの
人ひとなる我われや 何なにすとか
一日一夜ひとひひとよも 離さかり居ゐて 嘆 なげき恋こふらむ Man'yōshū, Ōtomo no Yakamochi

In English

The copper pheasant, they say, calls to its mate on the facing peak across the valley.
So why do I, a mere human, grieve and long for you so much
when we are apart for just one day and one night?

The Copper Pheasant in The Pillow Book
Sei Shōnagon also seems to have heard that copper pheasant pairs spend the night apart across a valley. She wrote about it in The Pillow Book:

In Japanese

山鳥やまどり。友ともを恋こひてなくに、鏡かがみ を見みすれば慰なぐさむらむ、心こころわかう、いとあはれなり。谷たに隔へだてたる程ほどなど心苦こころぐるし。 The Pillow Book, Sei Shōnagon

In English

The copper pheasant cries out missing its mate, but it is said to calm down if you show it a mirror. How innocent and touching! It is sad to think of the pair apart, with a valley between them.

Retired Emperor Go-Toba's Poem Based on This One
Retired Emperor Go-Toba wrote a poem borrowing from Hitomaro's "Ashibiki no yamadori no o no..." (a technique called honkadori).
"Tōyamadori" is another name for the copper pheasant.

In Japanese

桜さくら咲さく 遠山鳥とおやまどりの しだり尾おの
ながながし日ひも あかぬ色いろかな Shin Kokin Wakashū, Retired Emperor Go-Toba

In English

Cherry blossoms bloom on the distant mountains. Even if I gaze at them all day long,
a day as long as the drooping tail of the copper pheasant, I never tire of their color.

A Romantic Poem About the Night Sky
The next poem is by Kakinomoto no Hitomaro. He sees the clouds as waves, the moon as a boat, and the stars as a forest.

In Japanese

天あめの海うみに 雲くもの波立なみたち 月つきの船ふね
星ほしの林はやしに 漕こぎ隠かくる見みゆ Man'yōshū, Kakinomoto no Hitomaro

In English

On the sea of heaven, waves of cloud rise, and I see the boat of the moon
rowing away and hiding in the forest of stars.

Hitomaro's Poems Mourning a Prince
Kakinomoto no Hitomaro was a court poet who served Empress Jitō.
When her son, Prince Kusakabe, died, Hitomaro wrote the following poem.

In Japanese

あかねさす 日ひは照てらせれど ぬばたまの
夜渡よわたる月つきの 隠かくらく惜おしも Man'yōshū, Kakinomoto no Hitomaro

In English

The sun is shining, yet how sad that Prince Kusakabe has passed away, like the moon that crosses the night sky and sets.

The next poem is set at Akino, where Prince Karu (Prince Kusakabe's son) was hunting. Hitomaro sees Prince Kusakabe as the setting moon and Prince Karu as the light of dawn. While mourning Prince Kusakabe, who passed away like the sinking moon, the poem grandly shows Prince Karu carrying on his father's will and ruling a new age, just as the dawn begins to light the world.

In Japanese

東ひんがしの 野のに炎かぎろひの 立たつ見みえて
かへり見みすれば 月傾つきかたむきぬ Man'yōshū, Kakinomoto no Hitomaro

In English

As the light of dawn begins to glow over the fields in the east,
I look back and see the moon sinking in the western sky.

A Poem on the Fleeting World, Written by the Uji River
Hitomaro is said to have written the next poem by the Uji River on his way from the ruined province of Ōmi to Fujiwara-kyō.
"Mononofu no" is a makurakotoba (pillow word) that leads into "yaso" (many), and "uji" works as a pun on both "clans" and "Uji."
Having seen the abandoned capital of Ōtsu, Hitomaro is thought to have written about how nothing in the world lasts.

In Japanese

もののふの 八十氏やそうじ河がわの 網代木あじろぎに
いさよふ波なみの 行ゆく方え知しらずも Man'yōshū, Kakinomoto no Hitomaro

In English

Like the waves that linger at the fish-trap stakes in the Uji River, no one knows where the many officials who serve the court will end up.

Hitomaro's Farewell Poem
There are many theories about how Kakinomoto no Hitomaro died, and no one knows for sure. But in his last poem, he thought of his wife.

In Japanese

鴨山かもやまの 岩根いわねし枕まける われをかも
知しらにと妹いもが 待まちつつある

Man'yōshū, Kakinomoto no Hitomaro

In English

Here I lie dying, my head pillowed on the rocks of Mount Kamo. Does my wife, knowing nothing of this, still wait for me?

The Iroha Poem
The Iroha is a poem that uses each of the 47 kana letters exactly once, and it has long been used to learn the kana. No one knows for sure who wrote it, but one theory says it was written in memory of Kakinomoto no Hitomaro. The Iroha is written in the imayō style, with a rhythm of seven and five syllables. But if you split it into lines of seven letters and read the last letter of each line, you get "to ka na ku te shi su," which can be read as "咎なくて死す" (toga nakute shisu, "I die without guilt"). So some think the poem is a hidden message mourning Hitomaro, who is said to have been put to death for a crime he did not commit.

In Japanese

色いろは匂にほへど 散ちりぬるを
我わが世よ誰たれぞ 常つねならん
有為うゐの奥山おくやま 今日けふ超こ えて
浅あさき夢ゆめ見みじ 酔ゑひもせず

Author unknown

In English

Though flowers bloom brightly, they scatter in the end.
In this world of ours, who can stay unchanged?
Crossing today the steep mountains of life,
I will have no shallow dreams, nor lose myself in drink.

Akino Hitomaro Park

Remains from the Asuka period have been dug up in Uda City, Nara Prefecture. Akino Hitomaro Park was built there to recall the age of the Man'yōshū. In the park stands a stone statue of Kakinomoto no Hitomaro on horseback, gazing toward the east.

The base of the statue is carved with the poem he wrote here, "Hingashi no no ni kagiroi no...".

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

By drawing people pulling (hiku) a net, Hokusai seems to show the pillow word "ashibiki no." The long trail of smoke may also stand for the long night.
