Poem 43
逢あひい見みての のちの心こころに くらぶれば
昔むかしは物ものを 思おもはわざりけり
Author: Acting Middle Counselor Atsutada (906–943)
Source: Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
How desolate my former life, Those dismal years, ere yet I chanced to see thee face to face 'Twere better to forget Those days before we met.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Compared to this feeling after meeting you, the longing I felt before seems like nothing at all.
Commentary
The Shūishō says this was sent the morning after the poet first spent the night with a woman, a so-called morning-after poem. Atsutada is known to have loved Ukon (Poem 38), but he had affairs with many women, so we cannot be sure whom it was for.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Atsutada was the third son of Minister of the Left Fujiwara no Tokihira. Gifted at poetry and the biwa, he died of illness at only 38. Because his father had brought down Sugawara no Michizane, rumors spread that Michizane's angry spirit had cursed him.

Fujiwara no Atsutada was the son of Minister of the Left Fujiwara no Tokihira. In the Shōtai Incident of 901, Tokihira played the central role in having Sugawara no Michizane sent away to Dazaifu. People believed that Michizane became an angry spirit after his death. In 908, Fujiwara no Sugane, who had taken part in Tokihira's plot, was struck by lightning and died suddenly, and Tokihira himself died suddenly in 909 at the age of 39. Then, in 930, lightning struck the Seiryōden hall of the palace, and Fujiwara no Kiyotsura, another who had taken part in Tokihira's plot, also died suddenly.

Because the men of his family died young, Atsutada is said to have expected a short life himself. He died in 943, at only 38.

Appendix
Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

For some reason, this picture shows ushi no koku mairi, a kind of curse in which a person goes to a shrine at around 1 to 3 a.m. and nails a straw doll, standing for the hated person, to the shrine's sacred tree. Ukon wrote a poem worrying that Atsutada would be punished by the gods. If the woman in this picture is Ukon, her poem may really have been an ironic jab at the man who left her.

In the Heian period, a woman who was not a man's principal wife, even if they were married, faced an unfair situation: the relationship simply ended when he stopped visiting her. By the way, ushi no koku mairi seems to have been done only by women.
