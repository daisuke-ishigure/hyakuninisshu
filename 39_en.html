Poem 39
浅茅生あさじうの 小野おのの篠原しのはら しのぶれど
あまりてなどか 人ひとの恋こいしき
Author: Councillor Hitoshi (880–951)
Source: Gosen Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
'Tis easier to hide the reeds Upon the moor that grow, Than try to hide the ardent love That sets my cheeks aglow For somebody I know.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
Though I try to hide it, my longing spills over. Why is it that I cannot stop thinking of that person?
Commentary
A poem of one-sided love that can no longer be hidden. "Asajiu no ono no shinohara" (the low bamboo grass of the moor) leads into "shinobu" (to hide). Some say the scene has no meaning of its own; others say the bleak moor reflects the pain of hiding love.
About the Poet
Councillor Hitoshi was a great-grandson of Emperor Saga but became a commoner named Minamoto no Hitoshi. He served as governor of Mikawa, Tanba, Mino, and Bizen, and finally rose to Councillor, joining the top nobles.

Minamoto no Hitoshi was a descendant of Emperor Saga. He served as a provincial official in many places, including Mikawa, Tanba, Mino (acting governor), Bizen (acting vice-governor), and Yamashiro. In 947, at 68, he became a Councillor and joined the top nobles (kugyō), but he died only four years later, at 72.

Hon'ami Kōetsu's Writing Box with Boat Bridge

A writing box (suzuribako) by Hon'ami Kōetsu, an artist of the Edo period. On its domed lid, the characters of Minamoto no Hitoshi's poem "Azumaji no Sano no (funabashi) kakete nomi omoiwataru wo shiru hito zo naki" are scattered across the surface. The characters for "boat" (funa) and "bridge" (hashi) are left out: the boats are drawn in gold lacquer (maki-e), and the bridge is a strip of lead.
Source: ColBase (https://colbase.nich.go.jp/)

The Poem on the Writing Box

In Japanese

東路あづまぢの 佐野さのの舟橋ふなはし かけてのみ
思おもひ渡わたるを 知しる人ひとぞなき Gosen Wakashū, Minamoto no Hitoshi

In English

Like the boat bridge laid across the river at Sano in the eastern lands,
I keep you always in my heart and go on loving you,
but you do not notice.
The Boat Bridge at Sano, from Remarkable Views of Bridges in Various Provinces

The "boat bridge at Sano" crossed the Karasu River in what is now Takasaki, Gunma Prefecture. A boat bridge is made by lining up many boats side by side and laying boards across them.

This is Katsushika Hokusai's ukiyo-e print The Boat Bridge at Sano (Shokoku Meikyō Kiran: Sano no Funahashi). Click the picture to enlarge it and look at the many boats lined up under the bridge.

in The Tale of Genji
The next poem appears in the "Sakaki" (The Sacred Tree) chapter of The Tale of Genji.
Hikaru Genji wrote it while staying at a temple, away from the wife he loved, to test whether he was ready to become a monk.

In English

I have left you in a place as fleeting as dew on the cogon grass,
and when I hear the fierce winds blowing from all sides, my heart cannot rest.
A Poem About Asajiu
in The Tosa Diary

This poem appears in The Tosa Diary (Tosa Nikki) and is said to have been written near what is now Tōchi, Nankoku City, Kōchi Prefecture.

The place had a name with "pond" (ike) in it, but in fact there was no water, only a wide field thick with cogon grass (asaji). There Ki no Tsurayuki was given presents such as fish from the rivers and sea, and young spring greens. The diary notes that the fish he was given was not carp. He may have been a little surprised by food that did not suit a place called "Pond."

The diary tells how deeply he was moved when he read this poem, which came with the young greens.

In Japanese

浅茅生あさじうの 野辺のべにしあれば 水みずもなき
池いけに摘つみつる 若菜わかななりけり From The Tosa Diary

In English

Here in a field of cogon grass there is no water,
yet these are young greens picked at the place called "Pond."
One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures.

The nobleman walking along the path between the rice fields seems to hang his shoulders. Together with the gloomy, misty scene, the picture seems to show the sadness of lost love.
