Poem 66
もろともに あはわれと思おもへえ 山桜やまざくら
花はなよりほかに 知しる人ひともなし
Author: Senior High Priest Gyōson (1055–1135)
Source: Kin'yō Wakashū, Miscellaneous
English Verse Translation
In lonely solitude I dwell, No human face I see; And so we two must sympathize, Oh mountain cherry tree; I have no friend but thee.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
O mountain cherry — feel moved together with me. There is no one else here who knows me but you.
Commentary
The Kin'yō Wakashū says this was written on unexpectedly seeing cherry blossoms in Ōmine. Gyōson was training as a mountain monk deep in the Ōmine mountains. A lone cherry tree in bloom may have seemed like himself enduring hard training, as if he had met a true friend, a feeling close to Ki no Tsurayuki's poem (Poem 35).
About the Poet
Gyōson was a great-grandson of Retired Emperor Sanjō. He lost his father at 10 and became a monk at Mii-dera at 12, studying esoteric Buddhism. After long mountain training he headed Mii-dera and Tennō-ji, and became Senior High Priest (Daisōjō) at 71.

Retired Emperor Sanjō was Gyōson's great-grandfather, and Prince Atsuakira, who was born as Emperor Sanjō's first son but gave up his place as crown prince under pressure from Fujiwara no Michinaga, was Gyōson's grandfather. So Gyōson's father left the imperial family and took the family name Minamoto.

Though he had imperial blood, Gyōson did not rest on his family ties; he chose Shugendō, centered on hard training in the mountains. As a guardian monk (gojisō) who protected the emperor's health with prayers, he served Emperors Shirakawa, Toba, and Sutoku.

Appendix
In the Drama Umi ni Nemuru Daiyamondo

Gyōson's Hyakunin Isshu poem, "Morotomo ni aware to omoe yamazakura," appeared in episode 3 of the TBS drama Umi ni Nemuru Daiyamondo (The Diamond Sleeping in the Sea), broadcast in autumn 2024. The drama is set on Hashima in Nagasaki, now known as Gunkanjima (Battleship Island).
An old woman from Hashima, now the head of a company in Tokyo, recites the poem from memory and then says:

Remember the old days with me, mountain cherry.
No one but you truly knows my heart.
Even if you don't understand me,
even if I can't understand you, that can't be helped.
Everyone has a mountain cherry in their heart.

Everyone reads a poem differently, which is fascinating. It was a moving scene in which the strength of a woman who had lived through a hard life rang out together with the poem.

Irakamichi (Setagaya, Tokyo)

On the road from the north exit of Yōga Station on the Tōkyū Den-en-toshi Line to Kinuta Park and the Setagaya Art Museum, there is a street paved with roof tiles called "Irakamichi" (Tile Road). Hyakunin Isshu poems are carved here and there along it. Most of the poems are carved into the pavement, but Gyōson's poem is carved into a stone wall and really stands out. Along the street there are benches designed with tiles and a small stream that seems to run with water in summer. The Hyakunin Isshu blends nicely into this street where local people come to relax.
(February 9, 2025)

A Poem Written During His Training
According to the headnote in the Shin Kokin Wakashū, Gyōson wrote this poem at the end of spring while training in the mountains.
In spring, people may have come to his hermitage in the mountain village to enjoy the blossoms.
When the crowds were gone and spring came to an end, the quiet must have made him feel even lonelier.

Onjō-ji (Mii-dera)

The head temple of the Tendai Jimon school, in Ōtsu City, Shiga Prefecture. It is commonly known as Mii-dera. Gyōson became a monk at this temple at 12 and studied esoteric Buddhism here.
