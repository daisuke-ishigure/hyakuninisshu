Poem 19
難波潟なにわがた みじかき葦あしの ふしのまも
逢あはわでこの世よを 過すぐしてよとや
Author: Ise (c. 872–c. 938)
Source: Shin Kokin Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
Short as the joints of bamboo reeds That grow beside the sea On pebble beach at Naniwa, I hope the time may be, When thou 'rt away from me.
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
In this short life, even as brief as a joint of reed at Naniwa Bay, must I pass through this world without ever meeting you?
Commentary
The Ise Shū says this was written in autumn when someone said something unpleasant. It is said to show Ise's mixed feelings when her former lover, Fujiwara no Nakahira, left her.
About the Poet
Ise was a leading poet of the Kokin Wakashū period, and her life was full of love affairs. After parting from Nakahira she had a son with Emperor Uda, and later a daughter, Nakatsukasa, with Uda's son Prince Atsuyoshi. Nakatsukasa also became a famous poet.

Many people courted Ise. She had a son with Emperor Uda, the husband of Onshi, whom she served, and she came to be called "Ise no Miyasudokoro" (Lady Ise, mother of an imperial child). But the boy died young.

Later, with Prince Atsuyoshi, the husband of Onshi's daughter, she had a daughter, the poet Nakatsukasa. Mother and daughter are both counted among the Thirty-Six Immortal Poets.

Appendix
What Is "Ashi no Fushi"?

Ashi (also called yoshi) is the common reed, a plant of the grass family that grows by the water. Its stem is thin and has joints (fushi) like bamboo.
It sprouts in late April and grows very fast until about June, and its stem can reach 3 m.
Its underground stems spread sideways, so it grows in large clumps.

Fushi no ma, "the space between the joints," is used as an image of something very short.
Naniwa was famous for its reeds and appears in many poems.

When you look at a real reed, the space between the joints does not seem short. One theory says that the phrase stands for "a short time" because a reed grows so quickly, adding joint after joint to its stem.

A Poem Ise Wrote About Lost Love
Ise had once been passionately in love with Fujiwara no Nakahira, but at some point their love grew cold. Sunk in loneliness, as if she had lost her place at court, Ise decided to go to the province of Yamato (now Nara Prefecture), where her father, Fujiwara no Tsugukage, was serving as governor. She wrote the next poem as she left the glittering capital to travel alone, still unable to let go of her feelings.

In Japanese

三輪みわの山やま いかに待まち見みむ 年とし経ふとも
尋たずぬる人も あらじと思おもへば
miwanoyama ikanimachimimu toshifutomo
tazunuruhitomo arajitoomoheba Ise-shū, Ise

In English

How shall I wait for you at Mount Miwa?
I think that no one will come to visit me, however many years go by.

A Poem Ise Wrote When She Gave Up Her House
Ise is said to have written the next poem, with a touch of humor, when she gave up the house she had long lived in. Se ni is thought to be a kakekotoba (a word with two meanings): se ni, "into shallows," and zeni, "money." Another theory says fuchi is also a kakekotoba: fuchi, "a deep pool," and 扶持ふち (fuchi), meaning a living or wages, a word known today in the phrase kuibuchi, "one's living."

In Japanese

あすか川がわ ふちにもあらぬ わが宿やども
せにかはりゆく ものにぞありける asukagawa fuchinimoaranu wagayadomo
senikahariyuku mononizoarikeru Kokin Wakashū, Ise

In English

Just as the deep pools of the Asuka River turn into shallows, my house, too, is turning into money to live on.

A Poem Quoted in "Utsusemi" in The Tale of Genji
The next poem is in Ise-shū, Ise's personal poetry collection. It also became famous because Murasaki Shikibu quoted it in "Utsusemi," the third chapter of The Tale of Genji. Comparing herself, her heart left empty by lost love, to the empty shell of a cicada, the poet expresses her loneliness.

In Japanese

空蝉うつせみの 羽はねにおく露つゆの 木隠こがくれて
しのびしのびに 濡ぬるる袖そでかな utsusemino haneniokutsuyuno kogakurete
shinobishinobini nururusodekana Ise-shū, Ise

In English

Just as the morning dew on the wings of a cicada's shell is hidden in the shade of the leaves, my sleeves, too, are secretly wet with tears of a hidden love.

Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures. It shows a woman viewing plum blossoms against the scenery of Naniwa Bay. Could this woman be Ise? The workers laying new roof tiles also seem to express the feelings of a woman putting a lid on memories she cannot forget.
