Poem 52
明けぬれば 暮くるるものとは 知しりながら
なほおうらめしき 朝あさぼらけかな
Author: Fujiwara no Michinobu Ason (972–994)
Source: Go Shūi Wakashū, Love
English Verse Translation
Although I know the gentle night Will surely follow morn, Yet, when I'm wakened by the sun, Turn over, stretch and yawn— How I detest the dawn!
— William N. Porter, 1909
Modern Translation
I know that after dawn comes dusk — and yet how I resent this pale morning sky.
Commentary
Even knowing they will meet again when night falls, he still finds the dawn parting painful and hateful. In the Heian period lovers met only at night and had to part in the morning. The poem shows his wish not to leave his love even for a moment.
About the Poet
Fujiwara no Michinobu was raised as the adopted son of his uncle, Fujiwara no Kaneie, the Regent. The Ōkagami calls him a fine poet of quiet, gentle character. Much was expected of him, but he died of smallpox at only 23.

Fujiwara no Michinobu was the third son of Fujiwara no Tamemitsu. He came of age as the adopted son of his uncle, Fujiwara no Kaneie, who had just become Regent (sesshō) for Emperor Ichijō, and was given Junior Fifth Rank, Upper Grade. His real father, Tamemitsu, died when Michinobu was 21. He served as a provincial official in Bingo, Echizen, Tango, and other places, and his final post and rank were Middle Captain of the Left Palace Guards and Junior Fourth Rank, Upper Grade.

Appendix
A Poem of Lost Love Sent to an Emperor's Consort

Michinobu sent this poem to Princess Enshi. She was a granddaughter of Emperor Murakami and a consort (nyōgo) of Emperor Kazan. Perhaps because of the difference in their rank, his love could not come true, and he is said to have put his sad feelings into this poem.

In Japanese

嬉うれしきは いかばかりかは 思おもふらむ
憂うきは身みにしむ 心地ここちこそすれ Shika Wakashū, Fujiwara no Michinobu

In English

How happy you must be, now that your love has come true.
As for me, who lost my love, the sorrow soaks deep into my heart.
Linked Verse with Ise no Taifu
The next poem is a linked verse (renga) by Fujiwara no Michinobu and Ise no Taifu.
When Michinobu was passing the rooms of Akiko, Emperor Ichijō's empress, holding yamabuki (kerria) flowers, the ladies-in-waiting begged him to compose a poem. So Michinobu wrote the upper verse, and Ise no Taifu wrote the lower verse.

In Japanese

くちなしに ちしほやちしほ
染そめてけり
こはえも言いはぬ
花はなの色いろかな Toshiyori Zuinō

In English

I, who hold feelings I cannot speak, like the kuchinashi (gardenia, "no mouth"),
carry these yamabuki flowers, dyed again and again in gardenia yellow.
What a color, too lovely for words!

Yamabuki has yellow flowers. The fruit of the kuchinashi (gardenia) is used as a dye and gives a yellow color. "Kuchinashi" also means "no mouth," that is, unable to speak. In the lower verse, "e mo iwanu" (beyond words) answers that "no mouth."

His Death Poem
Here is one more gardenia poem: Michinobu's death poem.
Just before he died of illness, Michinobu sent this poem, together with a robe the color of yamabuki, to the woman he loved.

In Japanese

くちなしの 園そのにやわが身み
入いりにけむ
思おもふことをも
言いはでやみぬる Senzai Wakashū, Fujiwara no Michinobu

In English

Have I wandered into a garden of gardenias ("no mouth")?
My life is ending without my being able to say what I feel.
Ukiyo-e by Katsushika Hokusai

This print is from One Hundred Poems Explained by the Nurse (Hyakunin Isshu Uba ga Etoki) by Katsushika Hokusai, an ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period. The idea of the series is a nurse explaining the Hyakunin Isshu poems with easy-to-understand pictures. It shows palanquin bearers carrying customers who have spent the night in the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter. Hokusai seems to have seen, in the customers inside the palanquins, the feeling of resenting the dawn.
